Federal Funds Market

Instructions

Things to try:

  4. Answer the three prediction questions below the diagram to test your understanding.

Monetary policy regime

Banks operated very differently before and after the Fed's massive post-crisis reserve injections. Toggle between the two regimes to see how the demand curve for reserves changes shape.

Open-market operations

In the Pre-2009 regime, an open-market purchase of $2 billion shifts the supply curve right by $2B. You can also use the slider to set the reserve quantity directly.

IOR is the rate the Fed pays banks on reserves held at the Fed. It sets the floor of the demand curve. Before October 2008 the Fed paid 0% on reserves; the IOR tool was introduced during the crisis to put a hard floor under the federal funds rate.

Lab complete

Before 2009, the Fed controlled the money supply through reserve scarcity — small open-market operations could shift the federal funds rate dramatically and trigger multiplier-driven expansions or contractions. After 2009, the Fed's massive reserve injections and the introduction of interest on reserves (IOR) fundamentally changed the transmission mechanism. The federal funds rate is now set primarily by the IOR rate, not by reserve scarcity, and large open-market operations within the flat region of the demand curve have little or no effect on the money supply. Understanding this regime change is essential to understanding why $4 trillion of quantitative easing did not produce 1970s-style inflation.
