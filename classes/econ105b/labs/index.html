Practice labs organized by topic. Use these as warm-ups before lecture, review after class, or exam prep.

Aggregate Supply

Aggregate Supply

Explore how a shock to aggregate demand splits between a change in the price level and a change in output, and how that split depends on the share of firms with sticky prices. Watch the macro AS/AD diagram and the representative sticky and flexible firms react together.

Real Wage Adjustment

Step through the mechanism that drives long-run output back to the natural rate after a price-level shock. Watch the real wage get eroded, then restored when workers demand higher nominal wages and marginal cost shifts up.

Long and Short-Run Equilibrium

Long and Short-Run Equilibrium

Apply demand and supply shocks across linked AD-AS and IS-LM diagrams, choose a policy response, and adjust expected inflation to walk the economy from A to B to C. Practice scenarios cover stagflation tradeoffs and permanent vs. temporary supply shocks.

Spot the Shock

The reverse of the standard equilibrium lab: each problem shows a completed A → B → C path on the linked AD-AS and IS-LM diagrams, and you diagnose which shock produced it — by category, direction, and (for supply shocks) permanence. Builds the exam-relevant skill of reading diagrams diagnostically.

Stabilization Policy

Fed Rule Simulator

Be the Fed. Pick the weights α, β, γ in the reaction function r = αY + βP + γZ, run your rule against batteries of demand and supply shocks, and watch the dual-mandate tradeoff: a rule tuned for demand shocks misbehaves under supply shocks, and vice versa.

Expectations

Expectations & Disinflation

Work out what it costs to bring inflation down to target when expectations are only partly credible. Compute expected inflation, the unemployment rate the Fed needs, output growth via Okun's law, and the sacrifice ratio — then compare the same problem under no credibility, partial credibility, and full credibility.
Open Lab →

Expected vs. Unexpected Policy

Trace one policy shock across linked IS–LM, AS–AD, and Phillips-curve diagrams and see why anticipation changes everything: an expected change moves prices but not output, while an unexpected one does both. Raise expected prices to walk the economy from the short-run point B back to long-run equilibrium C, and read off whether the real interest rate ends up higher (fiscal crowding out) or unchanged (monetary neutrality).
