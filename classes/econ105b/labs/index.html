Transition Path: Impact to Long Run

Trace one shock through the numbered stages of the course worksheet, from the moment of impact to the long run. Build each causal chain a step at a time, say what the curve does, then animate the stacked IS–LM and AS–AD figure to watch the economy move from A to B to C. Expected vs. Unexpected Policy

Trace one policy shock across linked IS–LM, AS–AD, and Phillips-curve diagrams and see why anticipation changes everything: an expected change moves prices but not output, while an unexpected one does both. The Open Economy

Small Open Economy in the Long Run

Solve a small open economy with perfect capital mobility from end to end: total saving, investment at the world interest rate, net capital outflow, and the real exchange rate that clears the market for foreign exchange. Steps unlock one at a time — you commit an answer before any hint appears — and once you have ε*, the two-panel diagram from the lecture notes is drawn from your own numbers. A closing set of interpretation questions makes you say what the sign of S − I actually means: surplus or deficit, lender or borrower, spending above or below output.

The Large Open Economy in the Long Run

Now drop the assumption that the interest rate is handed to you. In a large open economy r is determined at home, where saving equals investment plus net capital outflow — so the first two steps ask for saving and the demand for loanable funds as functions of r, not as numbers. Solve for r1, then for the real exchange rate that net capital outflow delivers, and watch the three-panel appendix figure get drawn from your own numbers, with r1 and CF1 carried across the panels. Closing questions ask what the sign of CF means and which way it moves when the domestic rate rises relative to r*.

Small Open Economy in the Long Run: Graphical Analysis

Stop solving and start shifting curves. One fixed economy, four experiments — a fiscal expansion at home, a fiscal expansion abroad, a boom in investment demand, and a tariff — each applied as a single clean shock. Predict what happens to the world interest rate, saving, investment, net capital outflow, the exchange rate and net exports, then check your predictions and read the causal chain behind every row. The two panels redraw together so the same S − I is visibly a horizontal gap on the left and a vertical line on the right, and a reference line marks the closed-economy rate that decides surplus from deficit. Includes the chapter's most counterintuitive result: protection appreciates the currency and leaves the trade balance exactly where it was.
Open Lab →

Large Open Economy in the Long Run: Graphical Analysis

The same four experiments, now with the interest rate free to move. Because saving has to cover investment and net capital outflow, a shock at home changes r — so the shift you predict in one panel propagates into the next two. Follow the chain across the three-panel appendix figure: loanable funds fix r1, the net-capital-outflow schedule turns r1 into CF1, and the market for foreign exchange turns CF1 into ε1, with r1 and CF1 drawn at literally the same place on the shared axes. Two results the small-economy lab cannot show: crowding out here is only partial, sitting strictly between the closed and small open cases, and when a fiscal expansion abroad shifts two curves at once, the model settles which one wins. Net capital outflow is allowed to go negative, so you can also see this economy as a net borrower running a trade deficit.
