ECON 105B — The Open Economy

The Large Open Economy in the Long Run

Take a large open economy apart in five steps: saving as a function of the interest rate, the total demand for loanable funds, the domestic interest rate that clears them, and the real exchange rate net capital outflow delivers — then say in words what the sign of CF actually means.

Instructions

You are handed a large open economy: output is fixed at full employment, and — unlike the small open economy — the domestic real interest rate is not handed to you. This country is big enough that its own saving and borrowing move the world market, so r is determined at home, where saving equals the total demand for loanable funds. Net capital outflow then determines the real exchange rate.

Net capital outflow depends on both interest rates, CF = CF0 − CF1·r + CF2·r*. In every problem here CF1 = CF2, so the schedule collapses to CF = CF0 − CF1(r − r*): capital responds to the interest differential. A domestic rate above the world rate makes domestic assets the better buy, and capital flows in. That is the economic content of the CF curve, not a modelling convenience — but note that CF0 is positive, so r above r* does not by itself make CF negative.

Steps unlock one at a time. You have to commit an answer to a step before the next one opens and before any help for that step becomes available — that is the point of the lab. Once you have the exchange rate, the three-panel diagram from the appendix is drawn from your numbers.

You will be asked to answer the following questions:

  1. Total saving as a function of r. Substitute the consumption function into S = Y − C − G and collect the r terms. Two boxes: the intercept, then the coefficient on r.
  2. Investment plus net capital outflow as a function of r. Add the two schedules and collect the r terms. Two boxes again, but this line is printed with a minus between them, so the second box takes the size of the slope. Remember r* is a given number, not an unknown — substitute it.
  3. The equilibrium real interest rate. Set saving equal to the total demand for loanable funds and solve for r1.
  4. The equilibrium real exchange rate. Evaluate CF at r1, set it equal to NX(ε), and solve for ε1.
  5. Interpretation. Three questions about the economy you just solved: lender or borrower, surplus or deficit, and which way CF moves when r rises relative to r*.

Enter your answer and click Check Answer (or press Enter). All dollar amounts are in billions of dollars, r and r* are in percent, and ε is a unitless index. Round to two decimals. After you submit, a three-step help ladder opens — Hint, then Formula, then Worked solution. A step you got wrong stays open, so you can fix it once you see where it went off.

Worked Example

Here is the whole chain worked through once, on a different economy from the one below. Suppose we are given:

Y = 8000 C = 350 + 0.70(Y − T) − 30r I = 1910 − 40r CF = 400 − 50r + 50r* G = 1400 T = 1600 NX = 950 − 500ε r* = 6 (percent)

Step 1 — Saving as a function of r

Saving is output minus consumption minus government purchases. We cannot reduce it to a number, because consumption depends on r and r is exactly what we do not know yet. So substitute and collect:

S = Y − C − G S = 8000 − [350 + 0.70(8000 − 1600) − 30r] − 1400 S = (8000 − 350 − 4480 − 1400) + 30r S = 1770 + 30r

The coefficient on r is positive: a higher interest rate makes households consume less, and what they do not consume they save. Saving slopes up.

Step 2 — Investment plus net capital outflow

I + CF is the total demand for loanable funds. Investment is the obvious use of domestic saving; net capital outflow is the less obvious one. Buying a foreign bond is a use of saving in exactly the same way that buying a machine at home is — the dollars leave the domestic pool either way. So the two schedules add:

I + CF = (1910 − 40r) + (400 − 50r + 50(6))  ← r* = 6 substituted I + CF = (1910 + 400 + 300) − (40 + 50)r I + CF = 2610 − 90r

Notice where the 50(6) went. r* is the world rate — a number we are given, not something we solve for — so CF2·r* = 300 sits in the intercept, not in the coefficient on r. Both slopes are negative, so the total slope is −90.

Step 3 — The equilibrium real interest rate

This is where the large open economy departs from the small one. The rate is not handed to us; the loanable funds market has to clear at home:

S = I + CF 1770 + 30r = 2610 − 90r 30r + 90r = 2610 − 1770 120r = 840 r1 = 840 / 120 = 7.00

Step 4 — The equilibrium real exchange rate

Now evaluate CF at that rate. The market for foreign exchange clears when the dollars supplied by net capital outflow equal the dollars demanded to pay for net exports:

CF = 400 − 50(7.00) + 50(6) = 350 CF = NX(ε):  350 = 950 − 500ε 500ε = 950 − 350 = 600 ε1 = 600 / 500 = 1.20

Notice the order. Saving and the demand for funds fix r; r fixes net capital outflow; net capital outflow fixes net exports; and ε is whatever it has to be to deliver them. The exchange rate is the last thing determined, not the first.

Step 5 — What it means

CF = +350, so this economy sends 350 billion of saving abroad: it is a net lender. In equilibrium the foreign-exchange market forces NX = CF, so net exports are +350 too — a trade surplus. And spending falls short of output by the same 350:

C = 350 + 0.70(6400) − 30(7) = 4620 I = 1910 − 40(7) = 1630 C + I + G = 4620 + 1630 + 1400 = 7650 Y − (C + I + G) = 8000 − 7650 = +350 = NX

The trap worth noticing

Here r1 = 7 is above r* = 6, and yet CF is positive. It is tempting to reason "domestic rates are high, so capital flows in, so CF must be negative" — but that ignores CF0. What the differential controls is the direction of the effect, not the sign of the level: raising r relative to r* pushes CF down, from wherever CF0 put it. Whether it ends up above or below zero is an arithmetic question, and you have to actually compute it.

Problem

A large open economy is described by the equations below. Output is fixed at its full-employment level. The domestic real interest rate is not given: it is determined where saving equals investment plus net capital outflow.

The economy

Units. Y, C, I, G, T, S, CF and NX are measured in billions of dollars. The interest rates r and r* are in percent — so r = 5 means 5%, and a term like 30r contributes 150 when r = 5. The real exchange rate ε is a unitless index. Round every answer to two decimals.
