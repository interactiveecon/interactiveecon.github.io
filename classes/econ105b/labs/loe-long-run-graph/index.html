ECON 105B — The Open Economy

Large Open Economy in the Long Run: Graphical Analysis

In a large open economy the interest rate is neither handed down by the world market nor settled behind a closed border — it clears at home, where saving meets investment plus net capital outflow. Pick a shock and follow it across three panels: loanable funds fix r1, the net-capital-outflow schedule turns r1 into CF1, and the market for foreign exchange turns CF1 into ε1.

Instructions

This is an exploration lab, not a graded one. Nothing is recorded and nothing is randomized: the baseline economy is always the same, so you can run an experiment, reset, and run it again with one structural assumption changed and see exactly what that assumption was doing.

The workflow is the same every time:

  1. Pick an experiment. Four buttons, one clean shock each. Exactly one is active at a time.
  2. Predict the six impacts. For each quantity, choose Fall, Stay the Same, or Rise before you check. The diagrams update as soon as you pick a scenario — read the shift off the picture, that is the skill.
  3. Check your answers. The table is graded against the state of the economy at the moment you clicked. Change anything afterwards — a dropdown, a slider, the saving toggle — and the result clears; you have to check again.
  4. Read the explanation. Every row gets the causal chain behind it, and the numeric column for the new economy is revealed alongside the baseline.
  5. Reset and try the same shock with the other saving assumption, or open Free explore and move the parameters yourself.

The six impacts you predict are the interest rate r, saving S, investment I, net capital outflow CF, the real exchange rate ε, and net exports NX. Unlike the small-open-economy lab, the interest rate row is a real question here: r is determined at home, so a domestic shock moves it.

The baseline economy, if you want the algebra behind the picture — but you should not need it. Everything below is on the diagrams:

  • Y = 6000, G = 1000, T = 1000
  • C = 250 + 0.75(Y − T) − c1r, so S(r) = 1000 + c1r
  • I(r) = 1950 − 250r
  • CF(r) = 400 − 150r + 150r*, with r* = 3
  • NX(ε) = 750 − 500ε

Equilibrium is a chain, and the chain is the point of the lab: S(r) = I(r) + CF(r) gives r1; r1 read off the CF schedule gives CF1; and CF1 = NX(ε) gives ε1. At the baseline with c1 = 50 that is r1 = 4.00%, S = 1200, I = 950, CF = +250, ε1 = 1.00.

CF2 = CF1 = 150 is economics, not bookkeeping. Writing the schedule as CF = CF0 − CF1(r − r*) says that capital chases the interest differential: what makes a saver send funds abroad is not the level of the domestic rate but how it compares with the rate available overseas. Raise r and r* together by the same amount and net capital outflow does not move at all.

Units. Y, C, I, G, T, S, CF and NX are in billions of dollars. The interest rates r and r* are in percent, so r = 4 means 4% and the term 250r contributes 1000 when r = 4. The real exchange rate ε is a unitless index.

Each slider starts at its baseline value. Moving one is a shock: the impacts table and all three diagrams compare the economy you have built against the baseline. The ranges are deliberately narrow — wide enough for a visible shift, tight enough that saving, investment, the interest rate and the exchange rate all stay sensible at every combination. Net capital outflow is the one quantity allowed to turn negative, because a large open economy that borrows from the rest of the world is half of what this chapter is about.
