ECON 105B — The Open Economy

Small Open Economy in the Long Run: Graphical Analysis

One fixed economy, four experiments. Pick a shock — a fiscal expansion at home, a fiscal expansion abroad, a boom in investment demand, or a tariff — predict what it does to six quantities, then watch the market for loanable funds and the market for foreign exchange move together.

Instructions

This is an exploration lab, not a graded one. Nothing is recorded and nothing is randomized: the baseline economy is always the same, so you can run an experiment, reset, and run it again with one structural assumption changed and see exactly what that assumption was doing.

The workflow is the same every time:

  1. Pick an experiment. Four buttons, one clean shock each. Exactly one is active at a time.
  2. Predict the six impacts. For each quantity, choose Fall, Stay the Same, or Rise before you check. The diagram updates as soon as you pick a scenario — read the shift off the picture, that is the skill.
  3. Check your answers. The table is graded against the state of the economy at the moment you clicked. Change anything afterwards — a dropdown, a slider, a toggle — and the result clears; you have to check again.
  4. Read the explanation. Every row gets the causal chain behind it, and the numeric column for the new economy is revealed alongside the baseline.
  5. Reset and try the same shock with the other saving assumption, or with the world interest rate on the other side of the closed-economy rate.

The six impacts you predict are:

  • the world interest rate r*
  • saving S and investment I
  • net capital outflow S − I — the quantity that links the two diagrams
  • the real exchange rate ε and net exports NX

Two structural toggles sit under the experiments. They change the baseline economy, not the shock: whether saving responds to the interest rate, and whether the world rate sits below, at, or above the rate this economy would have in autarky. Under Free explore you can move the parameters yourself instead of using a named experiment.

The baseline economy, if you want the algebra behind the picture — but you should not need it. Everything below is on the diagram:

  • Y = 6000, G = 1000, T = 1000, all fixed
  • C = 250 + 0.75(Y − T) − c1r, so S(r) = 1000 + c1r
  • I(r) = 2000 − 200r
  • NX(ε) = 1000 − 500ε
  • rclosed = 1000 / (200 + c1) — 4% when c1 = 50, 5% when c1 = 0 — and r* = rclosed + offset

Units. Y, C, I, G, T, S and NX are in billions of dollars. The interest rates r and r* are in percent, so r* = 4 means 4% and the term 200r contributes 800 when r = 4. The real exchange rate ε is a unitless index.

Why net exports did not move

Protection raises the demand for domestic goods at any given exchange rate, so the net-export schedule shifts out. But equilibrium net exports are not set in the market for goods — they are pinned down by saving and investment, because NX = S − I, and a tariff changes neither. The only variable left free to adjust is the real exchange rate, and it appreciates by exactly enough to offset the protection. A tariff can change the composition of trade — fewer imports and, through the stronger currency, fewer exports — but it cannot change the trade balance.
