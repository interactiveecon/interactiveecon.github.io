Setting up the LM curve

Money demand = money supply

The LM curve is just the money-market equilibrium condition, L(r, Y) = M/P, solved for r. Start from equilibrium and isolate r:

Model A · d = 50
set L = M/P:  Y − 50r = 1000
move r:  50r = Y − 1000
divide by 50:  r = (1/50)Y − 20
LM (A):  r = 0.02 Y − 20
Model B · d = 5
set L = M/P:  Y − 5r = 1000
move r:  5r = Y − 1000
divide by 5:  r = (1/5)Y − 200
LM (B):  r = 0.2 Y − 200

These two lines are the LM curves — every (Y, r) pair that clears the money market. Everything from here just reads points off them.

The interest-rate response

Same shock, very different r

Model A · r = 0.02Y − 20
Y = 1000 : r = 0
Y = 1100 : r = 2
Δr = 2 (for ΔY = 100)
Model B · r = 0.2Y − 200
Y = 1000 : r = 0
Y = 1100 : r = 20
Δr = 20 (for ΔY = 100)
Reveal Same output shock. In A the interest rate rises 2 points; in B it rises 20. Model B needs a ten-times-larger interest-rate swing — it's the steeper LM curve.
Why r must rise — step by step (Model A)
  1. Money supply is fixed: M/P = 1000. At Y = 1000, r = 0: money demand L = 1000 − 50(0) = 1000. Supply = demand ✓
  2. Now output rises to Y = 1100, still r = 0: L = 1100 − 50(0) = 1100.
  3. Money demand (1100) now exceeds supply (1000) by 100 — the money market no longer clears.
  4. The interest rate must rise to pull demand back down. Solve 1100 − 50r = 1000 → r = 2. In Model B, 1100 − 5r = 1000 → r = 20.

Because B's demand barely responds to r, it takes a far bigger rate rise to absorb the same excess — the steeper LM.

Same shock, two slopes

Watch the identical shock land differently

The money market behind it

Higher Y shifts both demand curves right. Against the fixed supply, the steep demand (B) forces a much larger rise in r — that larger rise is the steeper LM.

The resulting LM curves
Y = 1000  ·  r on flat LM (A) = 0.0  ·  r on steep LM (B) = 0.0
The punchline

Why the sensitive one is the flat one

Write money demand in general form, naming the two coefficients:

L(r, Y) = e·Y − d·r

e = how much money demand rises with income  ·  d = how much it falls when r rises (the interest-sensitivity). In our models e = 1 and d = 50 or 5.

Set L = M/P and solve for r — same steps as before, just with letters:

r = (e / d)·Y − (M/P)/d   ⇒   slope = e / d
The inversion

The interest-sensitivity d sits in the denominator. So the model where money demand is more sensitive to r — Model A, d = 50 — is the flatter one, not the steeper one. A larger value of d means a small rise in r already frees up enough money demand to soak up the extra transactions demand from higher Y.

interest-sensitive demand (larger d) ⇒ flat LM
interest-insensitive demand (smaller d) ⇒ steep LM

A quick reminder

The investment multiplier

Before we trace the IS curve: a change in investment doesn't change output one-for-one. It sets off a chain — new investment becomes someone's income, part of which is spent again, and so on.

ΔY = ΔI + MPC·ΔI + MPC²·ΔI + … = 1/(1−MPC) · ΔI

where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume. The factor m = 1/(1−MPC) is the multiplier.

Example
MPC = 0.6
m = 1/(1−0.6) = 1/0.4 = 2.5
So
ΔI = 80 ⇒ ΔY = 2.5 × 80
= 200

We'll use m = 2.5 throughout. The bigger the multiplier, the more a given change in investment moves output.

A rate cut, through the multiplier, to output

The punchline

Why the sensitive one is the flat one — again

Build the goods-market relationship from the two pieces we already used. Investment responds to r, and the multiplier turns ΔI into ΔY:

ΔI = −b·Δr   and   ΔY = m·ΔI   ⇒   ΔY = m·b·(−Δr)

m = the multiplier, 1/(1−MPC)  ·  b = how much investment responds to r. In our models m = 2.5 and b = 40 or 4.

Rearrange for the slope of IS in (Y, r) space — how much r changes per unit of Y. Since ΔY = m·b·(−Δr), divide:

Δr/ΔY = −1/(m·b)   ⇒   slope of IS = −1/(m·b)
The inversion, again

The interest-sensitivity b is in the denominator. A larger b means a given rate cut produces a bigger output swing — a flatter IS. Same logic as LM: the more responsive the underlying behavior, the flatter the curve.

interest-sensitive investment (larger b) ⇒ flat IS
interest-insensitive investment (smaller b) ⇒ steep IS

An increase in government spending

Shock: the government raises G, with the price level fixed (short run).

Question 8 (write)

Write the full causal chain in words, justifying every arrow. Where does the interest rate enter, and why?

G↑ → PE↑ → U.I.↓ → Y↑ → (M/P)ᵈ↑ → r↑ → I↓ → Y rises by less than the initial push
Two links students skip

1. PE↑ → U.I.↓ → Y↑: when planned expenditure jumps above output, firms sell more than they produce, so unplanned inventories fall. That drop is the signal that tells firms to raise production — which is how output actually rises.

2. Y↑ → money demand↑ → r↑: higher income raises money demand; with M/P fixed, r must climb to re-clear the money market. The higher r then pulls investment down — this is how expansionary fiscal policy crowds out investment.

On the diagram

IS shifts right along LM

At the new point B: output is higher and the interest rate is higher.
The higher r pulls investment down — crowding out part of the boost.

G doesn't touch the money market directly, so LM stays put. IS slides right; the economy climbs LM to a higher r.

Diagnose · silent poll

Find the flawed sentence

Students pick a number on paper (or hands). Then reveal.

Why sentence 2 fails

Investment depends on the interest rate, not directly on output. Along LM the rate rose, so planned investment falls even as output rises. Sentence 2 confuses the long-run capital stock with short-run investment demand.

The transmission mechanism

The critical arrow is r↓ → I↑. Monetary policy has no direct effect on output; it works only by moving the interest rate, which changes investment. Fiscal enters the goods market; monetary enters the money market.

On the diagram

LM shifts right along IS

At the new point B: output is higher and the interest rate is lower.
Lower r is the only reason output moved — via investment.

More money pushes r down along the fixed IS curve; cheaper borrowing lifts investment and output.

Under which of these conditions is monetary policy strong at raising output? Commit to a guess for each before revealing.

Why AD slopes down

Question 12 (write)

Write the chain from P↑ to Y. What happens to real money balances, then r, then investment?

P↑ → M/P↓ → r↑ → I↓ → PE↓ → U.I.↑ → Y↓  (higher P ⇒ lower Y: AD slopes down)
Movement ALONG vs. SHIFT OF

A change in P moves the LM curve via M/P and traces IS–LM equilibria — a movement along AD. A change in M, G, or T at a fixed P shifts the whole curve. Confusing these is the most common AD error.

On the diagram

From IS–LM to AD

Higher P shrinks real money balances → LM shifts left → output falls from A to B. Plotting (Y, P) at each price level gives the downward AD curve on the right.

Why sentence 2 fails

An increase in M is not a change in P, so it can't be a movement along AD. At the fixed price level, higher M raises output → the whole AD curve shifts right. Only a change in the price level moves you along AD.

On the diagram

Shifting AD at a fixed price level

M↑, G↑, or T↓ at the fixed price level P̄ moves output right (A → B).
The whole curve shifts — this is not a movement along AD.

Rightward shifters: M↑ (money market), G↑ and T↓ (goods market). Leftward: their opposites.

How the five parts fit

Question 13 (predict)

In a liquidity trap, does expansionary monetary policy still shift AD right? Why or why not?

Answer

No. If extra M can't lower r, there's no investment response and AD doesn't move — fiscal policy is required. The same flat-LM condition that made monetary policy weak earlier also stops it from shifting AD here.

Flat LM: the money-supply increase (LM₁ → LM₂) leaves r and output almost unchanged, so AD hardly moves. Output barely responds to a change in M.

LM slope & IS slope (Part 1–2) → strength of fiscal & monetary policy (Part 3–4) → whether and how far AD shifts (Part 5).
