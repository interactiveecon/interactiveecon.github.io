Three relationships, one economy

Everything today is these three pieces talking to each other. Copy them onto the top of your page.

Goods market
PE = C(Y−T) + I(r) + G

Output adjusts until planned expenditure equals output. This is the IS side.

Money market
M/P = L(r, Y)

The interest rate clears it. This is the LM side. Note that P sits in the supply of real balances.

Aggregate supply
SRAS ↗  ·  LRAS: Y = Ȳ

SRAS slopes up because some prices are sticky. LRAS is vertical at Ȳ because in the long run every price is flexible.

You built the AD curve out of the first two. Today we finally put AD together with AS and let the price level move — which is what turns a short-run answer into a long-run one.

Write the mechanism

From confidence to the short run

Question 2 (write)

Write the full arrow chain from “confidence ↑” to the short-run equilibrium at point B. Name every market it passes through, in order, and say what clears in each one.

Two hints on form, not content: your chain should have more than four arrows in it, and it should visit the money market at least once.

If your chain goes “C↑ → Y↑, done,” you've written the goods-market half. The other half is the reason the answer isn't just the multiplier.

Reveal · the chain

The arrow almost everyone skips

C↑ → PE↑ → U.I.↓ → Y↑ → (M/P)ᵈ↑ → r↑ → I↓ → Y rises by less
Why it goes through the money market

Higher output means more transactions, so money demand rises. The supply of real balances hasn't moved, so the only thing that can re-clear the money market is a higher interest rate — and a higher r cuts investment, which claws back part of the original expansion.

The skipped arrow is Y↑ → (M/P)ᵈ↑ → r↑. Once the money market is in the room, r is not exogenous. You cannot hold the interest rate fixed and still claim to be describing the economy's response. (This is exactly the IS–LM logic you already know — the fiscal-policy chain carried the same feedback.)

U.I. = unplanned inventories. Falling inventories are the signal that makes firms raise production in the first place.

Before the diagram

What the AD shift actually measures

The chain you just wrote ends at an IS–LM equilibrium with the price level still at P₁. Call that output Y₂.

the horizontal AD shift  =  the fixed-P IS–LM outcome  =  Ȳ → Y₂
Movement along vs. shift of

AD tells you the output that clears goods and money at each price level. So when C rises, redo the IS–LM problem holding P at P₁: that answer, Y₂, is where AD′ sits at P₁. That is the shift.

But Y₂ is not the short-run answer. It is the answer before the price level is allowed to respond. Once flexible-price firms reprice, we slide along AD′ to a different point. Confusing the shift with the outcome is the single most common error on this diagram.

Keep three output levels straight on your page: Y₁ (goods market alone, r held fixed), Y₂ (IS–LM at P₁), Y₃ (short-run equilibrium, once P moves).

Why doesn't it stop at Y₂?

The IS–LM answer at the old price level was Y₂. The short-run equilibrium is Y₃, which is smaller.

Question 3 (write)

What happens between Y₂ and Y₃? Name who acts, what they do, and which curve moves as a result.

One-word hint: the thing we held fixed to define the AD shift is the thing that is no longer fixed.

Reveal · the short run

Prices move, so the money market moves again

AD↑ → flexible-price firms raise P to P₂ → M/P↓ → LM shifts up to LM(P₂) → r↑ to r₄ → I↓ → Y falls back to Y₃
Who acts, and why output stops short of Y₂

Not everyone can reprice — but the flexible-price firms can, and higher demand is exactly their cue to raise prices. That lifts the aggregate price level to P₂.

A higher P is a smaller supply of real money balances, M/P. The money market has to re-clear at a higher interest rate, so LM shifts up, r climbs to r₄, and investment falls again. That second round of crowding-out is the gap between Y₂ and Y₃.

Point B — the short-run equilibrium: output Y₃, price level P₂, interest rate r₄. Output is above Ȳ, but by less than the fixed-price diagram promised.

Notice the price level did two jobs here: it slid us along AD′, and it shifted LM. Same variable, both panels — that's what keeps the two diagrams consistent.

Predict · then show the shift

What carries B to C?

Question 4 (predict)

At B, output is above Ȳ. Something must push it back. Who is still unsatisfied at point B, and what do they do about it? Name the group and the curve their action moves.

Currently at point B: output Y₃, price level P₂, interest rate r₄.
Reveal · what carries B to C

Workers are the unsatisfied group. Prices rose to P₂ while the nominal wage didn't, so the real wage has fallen — and workers bargain for higher nominal wages to compensate. Higher wages are higher costs, which shifts SRAS up. At the same time the formerly sticky-price firms finally reprice, pushing the price level up to P₃.

The higher P shrinks real balances again, so LM shifts up to LM(P₃), r rises to r₃, and investment falls further — until output is back at Ȳ. That's point C, the new long-run equilibrium.

The punchline

What the shock actually bought

Short run · point B
output  Y₃ > Ȳ  (↑)
prices  P₂ > P₁  (↑)
interest  r₄ > r₁  (↑)
Long run · point C
output  Ȳ  (no change)
prices  P₃ > P₂  (↑)
interest  r₃ > r₄  (↑)
Complete crowding-out

In the long run output is exactly where it started — but its composition changed. Consumption is permanently higher (that was the shock). Output is unchanged. So something had to give: the higher interest rate r₃ means investment fell by precisely the amount consumption rose.

The economy got no extra output out of the confidence boom. It got a higher price level, a higher interest rate, and less investment — a permanently smaller share of output going to the capital stock.

long run:  ΔY = 0  ·  ΔP > 0  ·  Δr > 0  ·  ΔC = −ΔI

Part 2 of 5 · the mirror case

Run it backwards: taxes rise

A tax increase lowers disposable income, so households buy less: C falls, and aggregate demand falls at the price level P₁. Everything from Part 1 now runs in reverse.

Step 1 · the short run

Firms cut production and lay workers off, and flexible-price firms cut prices. The economy slides down SRAS to point B — output Y₁ and price level P₂, both below A.

Step 2 · the labor market closes it

The nominal wage hasn't moved but P has fallen, so the real wage is now too high. High unemployment is exactly the pressure that pushes the nominal wage back down, restoring the real wage — and falling wages are what shift SRAS down. Firms respond by producing more.

Long run (C): output back at Ȳ, price level P₃. And because P is lower, real balances are larger, so r is lower and investment is higher than before the tax rise — the exact mirror of Part 1.

At point A: output Ȳ, price level P₁. AD is about to fall.

Why sentence 3 fails

Its conclusion is right — output does return to Ȳ. But the mechanism is invented. Nothing in the model reverses the tax. The tax stays exactly where it was put.

What returns output to Ȳ is wage and price flexibility: unemployment drives the nominal wage down, SRAS shifts down, and the economy walks back to the long-run supply curve on its own. Question 7 (predict)

Look back at your Question 1 and Question 5 answers before you write. There is a pattern in them that is about to break.

On the diagram

Stagflation

At point A. The oil price is about to rise.
The asymmetry — this is the hinge

Higher input costs mean firms raise prices and cut output. SRAS shifts up. AD never moved. The economy slides along AD to point B: price level P₂ higher, output Y₁ lower. High inflation with high unemployment is called stagflation.

Now say the difference out loud. Every shock before this one moved P and Y in the same direction — up together, or down together. A supply shock moves them in opposite directions.

Hold that thought. In Part 4 we ask what policy can do about it, and the answer depends entirely on this one asymmetry.

Under the diagram

Why demand falls to meet the new supply

The AD curve did not move — but we still ended up at lower output. Something had to reduce the quantity demanded. Here is the machinery inside that slide along AD.

P↑ → M/P↓ → r↑ → I↓ → PE↓ → Y↓ and C↓
The fall in expenditure is the enforcement mechanism

The higher price level shrinks the supply of real money balances, so the money market re-clears at a higher interest rate. Higher r cuts investment; lower investment cuts planned expenditure; lower expenditure cuts output and consumption.

That reduction in planned expenditure is exactly what makes aggregate demand equal the new aggregate supply. Firms wanted to supply less at higher prices; the money market is what makes buyers want less too.

Same three arrows you used to derive AD — because sliding along AD is all we are doing here.

Write · then reveal each case

Temporary or permanent?

Question 8 (write)

What happens in the long run depends on whether the oil shock is temporary or permanent. For each case, write down which curve moves and why that curve and not the other one.

Case 1 · temporary
→ back to A · nothing permanent

SRAS moves, and then moves back. The oil price eventually returns to normal, costs fall again, and SRAS returns to where it started. The economy goes back to its original long-run equilibrium — same Ȳ, same P₁.

Case 2 · permanent
→ LRAS moves · Ȳ itself falls

LRAS moves too. A permanent supply shock is a permanent loss of productivity — the economy's productive capacity really has changed, so the vertical long-run curve shifts left to a new, lower Ȳ′. This is a fall in A, the same productivity term you shifted in the growth model.

Why sentence 2 fails

The principle in sentence 2 is one we spent all of Part 1 establishing, and it is true: output returns to the long-run supply curve. The error is in the application. It treats Ȳ as a fixed number when the whole point of a permanent shock is that Ȳ itself moved.

Sentence 3 states the principle correctly — output returns to LRAS, wherever LRAS now is. After a permanent shock, LRAS sits at Ȳ′ < Ȳ, so the economy returns to a permanently lower level of output. The loss is real and it does not heal.

This is why the temporary/permanent question in Q8 is not a technicality — it decides whether there is anything to stabilize at all.

Monetary
Policy

The Fed is legally required to deliver two things at once. We are about to find out when that is possible and when it is not.

Demand Shocks ✓ → Supply Shocks ✓ → Monetary → Fiscal → Synthesis
The legal frame

The dual mandate

In 1977, an amendment to the Federal Reserve Act gave the Fed two statutory objectives:

Mandate 1
maximum employment
Mandate 2
stable prices
the constraint:  the Fed has no direct lever on output

It cannot hire anyone or build anything. The only thing it controls is the money supply, and the only way that reaches output is through the interest rate. Every answer in this part has to travel that route.

Keep the two mandates written down side by side. In Part 3 we drew a shock that pushes them in opposite directions — we just haven't said so yet.

Write · then reveal

Leaning against the wind

Question 10 (write)

A negative demand shock has pushed the economy into a recession — it sits at point B, below A. Write the chain the Fed's response has to travel, from the instrument it actually controls all the way to output.

Left: the economy is stuck at B, below A. Right: the economy is overheating at B, above A.
M↑ → M/P↑ → r↓ → I↑ → PE↑ → Y↑  ·  AD shifts right, back to A
Lean against the wind

In the recession the Fed raises M: real balances grow, r falls, investment rises, planned expenditure rises, firms produce more and raise prices. AD shifts right and the economy returns to A. In the expansion it does the reverse — lowers M, r rises, investment falls, AD shifts left, back to A.

When the economy is down the Fed brings it up; when it is up the Fed brings it down. The goal is stability, not growth. And notice what makes this work: a change in the money supply moves prices and output in the same direction, and a demand shock moved them in the same direction too. One instrument, both mandates, no conflict.

How the Fed knows when to act

The Fed rule

The Fed watches prices and output. That information is summarized in a rule for the interest rate it targets:

The Fed rule
r = αY + βP + γZ

with α, β, γ > 0.  Z collects the other factors that might affect the Fed's decision.

Question 11 (write)

Read the rule in words, not symbols. What does α > 0 commit the Fed to doing? State it as a sentence about what the Fed does when it sees a particular number move.

Answer

α > 0 commits the Fed to raising its interest-rate target whenever output is high — and lowering it whenever output is low. β > 0 does the same for the price level. That is "lean against the wind", written as an equation.

Note what the rule can and cannot see. It reads Y and P, the two numbers. It does not read which curve moved to produce them. That distinction is the whole of the next slide.

Predict · then reveal

Output is rising fast. Raise rates?

Question 12 (predict)

The Fed rule says α > 0, and output is climbing. Should the Fed always raise rates in response to rising output? Commit to yes or no — then write the condition your answer depends on.

If it is an AD shock

Yes — raise rates. The long-run effect of that shock is inflationary (that was Part 1), so leaning against it is exactly right. The diagram below is the other case.

Step 1 · a positive LRAS shock is deflationary

LRAS shifts right to Ȳ′, and SRAS follows it right. Output grows — but the new equilibrium B sits at a lower price level P₂. Deflation is bad for business and can hold back growth.

Step 2 · this is a Z-term, not an α-response

The Fed accommodates the shock by increasing the money supply to keep prices stable. AD shifts right to AD′; wages then rise, pushing SRAS up to SRAS″; the economy settles at C — the original price level P₁ at the new, higher Ȳ′.

The 1990s. The tech boom and the spread of the internet made output grow fast, which shows up in Y. But the nature of that growth said this was LRAS moving, not AD — so the economy could absorb a lot of money without high inflation. Alan Greenspan kept interest rates low.

Output is rising. The rule sees the number — it does not see the cause.
Predict · then reveal both options

Can the Fed undo stagflation?

Question 13 (predict)

A negative supply shock has produced stagflation: prices up, output down. Both mandates are violated at once. What should the Fed do — and what will it get? Write your answer before anything is revealed.

Both panels start at the stagflation point B: prices up, output down.
The lever that stopped working

To fix prices the Fed must lower AD (M↓) — real balances fall, r rises, investment falls, output and consumption fall. Prices return to P₁, but output falls by even more than if the Fed had done nothing.

To fix output the Fed must raise AD (M↑) — real balances rise, r falls, investment rises, output and consumption rise. Output returns to Ȳ, but prices end even higher than the short run would have delivered on its own.

Here is why. Changes in the money supply move prices and output in the same direction. A supply shock moved them in opposite directions. So fixing one mandate necessarily worsens the other. The Fed cannot undo a supply shock — it can only choose which one to rescue: inflation or unemployment. That decision is context-specific, and it is not an economics question with a right answer.

Fiscal
Policy

A different instrument, a different branch of government, and a channel that reaches output directly. Ask yourself whether any of that changes the dilemma you just met.

Demand Shocks ✓ → Supply Shocks ✓ → Monetary ✓ → Fiscal → Synthesis
The legal and political frame

Who else is obligated to stabilize

Much like the Fed, the federal government is legally obligated to maintain full employment and price stability — under the Employment Act of 1946. Fiscal policy can be used in similar ways to monetary policy to stabilize the economy.

Before Reagan

Fiscal policy was largely concerned with keeping the federal budget deficit to a minimum. Departures from the balanced-budget priority — changing G or T — were made in order to stabilize the economy.

Since Reagan

Changes in government spending and taxes have more often been motivated by political rather than business-cycle concerns.

Worth holding alongside the model: the diagrams treat G and T as dials a planner turns. The institution that actually turns them answers to voters on a two-year cycle.

A different route to the same curve

Question 14 (write)

Both instruments end up shifting AD. But they get there differently. How does the fiscal channel reach output differently from the monetary channel? Write both chains side by side and mark where they diverge.

Left: recession at B. Right: overheating at B. Congress and the President hold the instruments.
fiscal:  G↑ (or T↓ → C↑) → PE↑ → Y↑
monetary:  M↑ → M/P↑ → r↓ → I↑ → PE↑ → Y↑
Where the two chains diverge

Fiscal policy enters the goods market directly. G is a term in planned expenditure — no intermediary. T works through consumption, one step removed but still inside the goods market. Unlike monetary policy, fiscal policy has a direct impact on output.

Monetary policy has to travel first. M only reaches output via r → I. That is why the LM and IS slopes decided how potent each instrument was — the monetary chain has two extra links that can be weak.

But look at where both chains end: PE↑ → Y↑, and AD shifts. Different routes, same destination. Hold on to that for the next slide.

Why sentence 2 fails

Sentence 1 is true, and it is the premise sentence 2 leans on. The channel really is more direct. But the conclusion does not follow, because the channel was never the problem.

Both instruments end in the same place: they shift AD, and shifting AD moves prices and output in the same direction. A supply shock moves them in opposite directions. So fiscal policy faces the identical trade-off — lower G or higher T brings prices down but drives output down further; higher G or lower T restores output but pushes prices higher still. Congress must choose between inflation and unemployment exactly as the Fed must.

A faster route to a lever does not change what the lever is attached to.

Apply it · four cases

Sort the scenarios

Questions 17 and 18 (write)

Q17: classify each scenario below — AD shock, temporary supply shock, or permanent supply/LRAS shock. Q18: for each one, say what stabilization policy should do, and name the cost of doing it.

  1. Consumer confidence collapses after a financial scare.
  2. A supply-chain disruption raises input costs; it is expected to resolve within a year.
  3. An economy permanently loses access to a major energy source.
  4. A genuine productivity boom — new technology makes every worker more productive.
1 · confidence collapses
negative AD shock

P and Y fall together. Policy: expand — M↑, or G↑/T↓. AD shifts back right and the economy returns to A. Cost: essentially none in this model — both mandates are served by the same move. This is the easy case, and it is the only easy case.

2 · supply chain, resolves in a year
temporary supply shock

Stagflation: P↑, Y↓. Policy: seriously consider doing nothing. Any AD response worsens one mandate to help the other, and the shock unwinds on its own when costs normalize and SRAS returns. Cost of acting: you buy a short-run fix and are left with the side effect after the shock has already gone.

3 · permanent energy loss
permanent supply / LRAS shock

P↑, Y↓, and LRAS shifts left to Ȳ′. Policy still faces the stagflation trade-off — but worse, there is no old Ȳ to return to. Chasing the old output level with AD policy just delivers inflation against a capacity that no longer exists. Cost: the real loss is permanent and no demand instrument can reverse it.

4 · productivity boom
positive LRAS shock

Output grows fast — but this is LRAS moving right, not AD, so the new long-run equilibrium is deflationary. The Fed rule sees high Y and wants to raise r; the right read is the opposite. Policy: accommodate with M↑ (a Z-term) to keep prices stable. This is Greenspan in the 1990s — output booming, rates held low, and no inflation, because the economy's capacity had genuinely grown.

Scenarios 1 and 4 both show rapid movement in output. They call for opposite policies. The number alone never tells you which curve moved.
