Notation · read this before Part 5 bites you

Per worker is not per effective worker

Three families of variables. The hat is the one that causes trouble — it appears only in Part 5, and by then a lot of people have stopped reading it.

Aggregate · no marks
Y  K  L  C  I  A

Whole-economy totals. A is technology — the level of labor-augmenting productivity.

Per worker · lower case
y = Y/L  k = K/L
c = C/L  i = I/L

Divide by the number of bodies. This is what living standards mean and what the data measure.

Per effective worker · hat
ŷ = Y/(AL)  kˆ = K/(AL)
ĉ = C/(AL)  î = I/(AL)

Divide by AL, bodies weighted by how productive each one is. Nobody's living standard is measured this way — it is a change of units that makes the algebra work.

The one sentence to keep

The two are linked by y = A·ŷ. In Part 5, ŷ will be constant in the long run while y grows — and both statements will be true at the same time, about the same economy. That is not a contradiction; it is the whole point of the chapter.

Also: x′ means next period's value of x, and x* means the steady-state value. Parameters: α (capital share), s (savings rate), δ (depreciation), n (population growth), g (technology growth).

Part 1 of 6

The Engine:
Δk = sf(k) − δk

Every result in this discussion comes out of one equation. New capital is built by saving; old capital breaks. The difference is the whole model.

Δk engine → Saving → Golden rule → n → g → Empirics
Throughline: diminishing returns to capital means saving changes the LEVEL of income; only technology changes its GROWTH RATE.
Start from what you already have

Read the equations before you compute

Two equations, both recycled from earlier courses. Nothing here is new machinery.

Supply · the production function
y = f(k) = kα = √k

Constant returns to scale let us write output per worker as a function of capital per worker alone. We calibrate α = ½, so f(k) = √k. Its slope is the MPK, and it falls as k rises.

Demand · saving is investment
i = s·y = 0.3·f(k)

In a closed economy with no government, y = c + i and c = (1−s)y, so i = sy. This is the same saving-equals-investment condition you closed the goods market with in ECON 105A.

A change of habit, not of substance

In 105A households were described by the MPC. Here they are described by s, the savings rate — and MPC = 1 − s. Same household, different half of the sentence. We use s because the model is about accumulation, and what accumulates is the part that is not consumed.

Write · then reveal

Where did the labor force go?

The aggregate production function is Y = F(K, L). Constant returns to scale says that scaling both inputs scales output by the same factor: zY = F(zK, zL) for any z. Set z = 1/L and the whole thing collapses to y = f(k), with L gone.

Question 1 (write)

Why does constant returns to scale let us drop L entirely? And — harder — what is being assumed away when we do? Name one thing about the real world that this step rules out.

Reveal · Question 1

Why it works. With constant returns, output depends only on the ratio of the inputs, not on their absolute size. Setting z = 1/L gives Y/L = F(K/L, 1) = f(k). So the size of the workforce has no effect on the relationship between output per worker and capital per worker. A country of 3 million and a country of 300 million with the same k have exactly the same y.

What it assumes away — this is the part worth writing down. Every source of scale disappears. No gains from a larger market. No agglomeration, no thicker supply chains, no ideas being cheaper to spread over more people. And in the other direction, no fixed factors: no land, no oil, no coastline that a bigger population has to divide up. Doubling the country simply doubles everything.

That is an assumption, not a fact — and it is doing real work. It is what makes “why is this country rich?” a question about k alone rather than about how big the country is. Hold onto it: in Part 4 we add population growth, and it will matter that extra workers dilute capital without adding anything but hands.

The punchline

Why the destination is a destination

Nothing pushed those two economies to the same point. Nobody chose k* = 9. It is where the two curves happen to cross, and the crossing is forced by the shapes: sf(k) bends over, δk does not.

k < k* → sf(k) > δk → Δk > 0 → k↑ → MPK↓ → sf(k) flattens toward δk
k > k* → sf(k) < δk → Δk < 0 → k↓ → MPK↑ → sf(k) rises back toward δk
Name the property · this is what a steady state is

k* is a sink. Knock the economy off it in either direction and it comes back. That is a much stronger claim than “k* is where Δk = 0” — a pencil balanced on its tip also has Δ = 0, and it does not come back.

The return happens because saving is concave and depreciation is linear. Every extra unit of capital adds less output than the last (MPK falls), so saving per worker grows more and more slowly — while the replacement bill δk keeps climbing at a constant rate. Below the crossing, saving is still winning. Above it, replacement has overtaken. They can only cross once.

So the answer to Question 2 is no — the capital-poor country does not stay poorer forever, provided it shares the same s, δ and f(k). That proviso is doing a lot of work, and Part 6 is where we cash it in.

Notice what has not been explained yet: the model has just told us that growth stops. Fact 1 on the scoreboard says it doesn't. Keep going.

Question 4 (write)

Starting from y* = (k*)α, show that %Δy* = α·%Δk*, and hence that %Δy* = 0 in any steady state. Then say in one sentence what the same equation tells you about the years during the transition.

The three lines
y* = (k*)α
log y* = α · log k*
%Δy* = α · %Δk*

In a steady state %Δk* = 0 by definition, so %Δy* = α·0 = 0. With α = ½, output per worker grows at half the rate capital per worker does.

And aggregate output
Y = L · y*
log Y = log L + log y*
%ΔY = %ΔL + %Δy*

With L fixed (%ΔL = 0) and %Δy* = 0, aggregate output growth is 0 too. In this version of the model there is no long-run growth of any kind. Part 4 changes the first term; Part 5 changes the second.

The sentence about the transition — this is the half people miss

The equation %Δy = α·%Δk is not a steady-state result. It holds every year. While the economy is climbing from k = 9 to k = 16, %Δk > 0, so %Δy > 0 — the growth is real, and a country living through it genuinely gets richer year on year.

What the equation also says is that this growth is borrowed from the transition, not owned. It lasts exactly as long as k is still moving, and k stops moving because sf(k) is concave. So the same one line explains both why saving more does raise growth for a while and why it cannot raise growth forever. That is the throughline, in three lines of algebra.

Question 5 · fingers on the chest, then reveal
Sentence 2 · the flaw

Sentence 3 is true. That is what makes this hard. The country really is permanently richer, and if you only checked the conclusion you would sign off on the whole chain. But 3 is true in levels, and sentence 2 got there by claiming something about growth rates.

Why the mechanism in 2 fails. Growth in y comes from Δk, and Δk = sf(k) − δk. Raising s lifts sf(k) at every k, so at the old k* saving now exceeds depreciation and capital does start rising — sentence 2 gets that much right. What it misses is what happens next: as k rises, the MPK falls, so each new unit of capital adds less output and sf(k) flattens, while the replacement bill δk keeps rising in a straight line. The gap closes. Growth is not a new state the economy enters; it is the journey between two states, and journeys end.

What believing 2 would commit you to. If a higher s really did buy a permanently faster growth rate, then a country saving 40% would pull further ahead of an otherwise identical country saving 30% every single year, without limit — the income gap would widen forever. It doesn't. The gap widens for a few decades and then stops at a fixed ratio. That is the observable difference between “richer” and “growing faster,” and it is the difference the scoreboard is about to punish us for.

The algebra · three lines
c* = f(k*) − δk*
∂c*/∂k* = f′(k*) − δ = 0
MPK = δ

Check it against our numbers: MPK = αkα−1 = 0.5/√k. At the golden-rule k* = 25 that is 0.5/5 = 0.1 = δ ✓. At our actual k* = 9 it is 0.5/3 = 0.167 > δ — we are below the golden rule, so more capital would still help.

The words · marginal benefit vs marginal cost

Add one more unit of capital per worker.

The benefit is the extra output it produces — that is exactly what MPK means.

The cost is that this unit will break, every year, forever, and has to be replaced — that is exactly what δ means.

Keep adding while the benefit exceeds the cost. Stop when they are equal.

Why this framing is worth more than the algebra

Read as calculus, MPK = δ is a first-order condition and nothing more. Read as economics, it is a rule you already know — buy until marginal benefit equals marginal cost — applied to a good whose cost is not a price but a maintenance obligation.

That reading is what will get you through Parts 4 and 5 without memorizing anything. When we add population growth, the marginal cost of a unit of capital gains a second component (new workers also need equipping) and the condition becomes MPK = δ + n. Add technology and it gains a third: MPK = δ + n + g. The rule never changes. Only the bill does.

The golden-rule savings rate, and why it never moves

Substitute k* = (s/δ)1/(1−α) into MPK = αkα−1 = δ and the δ's cancel completely, leaving s = α. With our α = ½ the golden-rule savings rate is 0.5, which is exactly where the bar chart peaked. Option (d) is the tempting wrong answer: a higher δ does lower kgr, but it lowers the steady-state k for any given s by precisely the same factor, so the savings rate that hits the peak is unchanged.

Now notice what that argument did not depend on. The δ canceled because it appears in the same place in both expressions — in the break-even term. Anything else that lands in that term will cancel the same way. In Part 4 the break-even term becomes (δ+n) and the golden-rule savings rate is still α; in Part 5 it becomes (δ+n+g) and it is still α. Three chapters of algebra, one answer. If you see s = α come out again in twenty minutes, that is not a coincidence — it is this cancellation.

Two units, two jobs

Of the two units of capital we now build, one replaces the machine that broke and one equips the new worker. Only then does k stay at 1. The transition equation becomes

Δk = sf(k) − (δ + n)k

Nothing has happened to saving. The sf(k) curve does not move. What moved is the line it has to clear — the break-even line rotates up, from δk to (δ+n)k. Population growth is not a drain on output; it is an increase in the standing bill.

Note on numbers: this table uses n = 0.1 so every entry is a whole number. Our running calibration uses n = 0.05, giving a break-even line of (δ+n)k = 0.15k and a steady state at k* = 4, y* = 2 — down from k* = 9, y* = 3 with no population growth at all.

Question 9 · fingers on the chest, then reveal
Sentence 2 · the flaw

The sf(k) curve never moves. Look at the animation again: the blue curve is labeled “unchanged” for a reason. Saving per worker at a capital stock of k is s·f(k) — it depends on k and on nothing else. At k = 4 the economy saves 0.6 per worker whether n is 0.05 or 0.20. Sentence 2 invents a fall in saving that does not happen.

What actually changed is the requirement. The same 0.6 of investment per worker now has to do a bigger job: replace broken machines and equip a much larger cohort of new workers. It is no longer enough, so k falls. Saving didn't drop — the bar rose. The distinction is not pedantic: it tells you that a policy which raised s could offset a baby boom, whereas if sentence 2 were true you would be looking for the mechanism that damaged saving, and there isn't one.

Sentence 3 is correct: k* falls from 4 to 1 and y* from 2 to 1. Getting the right destination from the wrong map is the hazard this deck keeps returning to.

Read the equations before you compute

What A actually does to the production function

Labor-augmenting technology
Y = F(K, AL)

A does not sit beside L in the function — it multiplies it. So the input that matters is not the number of workers but AL, the number of effective workers.

And it grows, exogenously
A′ = (1 + g)A

Technology improves at rate g every period, for reasons the model does not explain. Our calibration sets g = 0.05. Exogenous is doing a lot of work here — come back to it in Part 6.

Say this one out loud before moving on

If A = 2, one worker does the work of two. Not “works twice as hard” — the same person, the same hours, doing what it used to take two people to do, because the tools and methods are better.

So an economy with 10 workers and A = 2 has AL = 20 effective workers. It behaves, in every equation we have written so far, exactly like an economy of 20 ordinary workers. And that is the trick: constant returns still hold in K and AL, so dividing through by AL gives back the model we already solved —

Y/AL = F(K/AL, 1) = f(kˆ) = ŷ

Every result from Parts 1–4 survives, with hats on. What changes is what a hat means when you try to read a living standard off it.

Sentence 2 · the flaw · and why sentence 3 survives it

Sentence 2 is false. Capital per worker is k = K/L = A·kˆ*. The hat term is frozen, but A is growing at g, so k grows at g — 5% a year, forever. This is Kaldor fact 2, and sentence 2 denies it. It is precisely the slip slide 4 warned about: reading a hatted variable as if the hat were decoration.

And yet sentence 3 is true. K/Y = k/y = (A·kˆ*)/(A·ŷ*) — the A cancels. Both numerator and denominator grow at exactly g, so their ratio is pinned at kˆ*/ŷ* = 2.25/1.5 = 1.5 forever. That is Kaldor fact 3, and this is the first version of the model that delivers it non-trivially: on slide 16 the ratio was constant because nothing moved, and here it is constant while both parts are growing at 5% a year.

Now sit with what just happened. A true conclusion came through a false premise without a scratch. If you had believed sentence 2, you would have reached sentence 3 and been right — and you would also have been committed to k being constant, which contradicts the data, and to y being constant, which contradicts sentence 3's own companion facts. The conclusion survived because K/Y is a ratio, and ratios are exactly the quantities that are blind to the error sentence 2 makes.

Which is the transferable lesson: arriving at the right answer is not evidence that your reasoning was sound. On an exam, sentence 2 will cost you every question that asks about a level and none of the ones that ask about a ratio — and you will not be able to tell which kind you are looking at until you have already answered.

Synthesis · balanced growth

Everything grows together, and the model finally passes

In the steady state with technological progress, a whole family of variables moves in lockstep. Solow called this balanced growth. Each of these comes out of the same log-and-total-derivative trick from Question 4:

%Δy = g
%ΔY = n + g
%Δk = g
%Δw = g
%Δr = 0
%Δ(K/Y) = 0

The wage is w = MPL = (1−α)A kˆα, so log w carries a log A and grows at g. The rental rate is r = MPK = αkˆα−1, which contains no A at all — so it does not grow. The two gold entries are constants that stay constant while everything around them rises 5% a year.

The throughline, stated once more

Diminishing returns to capital means saving changes the LEVEL of income; only technology changes its GROWTH RATE.

Look down the last column and notice which parameter is responsible for every single ✓ that changed: g. Not s — s appears nowhere in that column, because s only ever set how high kˆ* sits, and kˆ* is a constant. Not n — n bought aggregate growth that no individual could spend. The one thing that moved the growth rate of a living standard was the one thing the model refuses to explain, since g was handed to us exogenously on slide 28.

That is the honest summary of Chapters 8–9: the Solow model explains why growth cannot come from accumulation, and then names — without explaining — the only thing it can come from.

Who catches up, and who doesn't

Slide 9 showed two countries converging. Slide 13 showed a country moving to a permanently higher level. Those are the two cases — here they are as a question.

Pair 1

Same s, same δ, same n, same g.
Different k₀ — one was bombed flat, the other wasn't.

Pair 2

Same k₀, same δ, same n, same g.
Different s — one saves 30%, the other 10%.

Question 12 (write)

Which pair converges in income per worker, and which does not? For the pair that does not, say what — if anything — the two countries do end up sharing.

Reveal · Question 12

Pair 1 converges. Identical parameters mean an identical kˆ*, and the sink property from Part 1 drags both economies to it regardless of where they started. This is the model's cleanest prediction, and it is the one that fits post-war Germany and Japan.

Pair 2 does not converge in levels. Different s means different kˆ*, so the thriftier country ends up permanently richer per worker and the gap never closes. But they do share one thing, and it is the important one: both grow at rate g in the long run. Parallel lines, permanently different heights.

That distinction has a name. Unconditional convergence — poor countries catching up with rich ones simply because they are poor — is what Pair 1 describes, and it is not what the world looks like. Conditional convergence is what the model actually predicts: every country converges to its own steady state, set by its own s, n, g and δ. Countries converge to different destinations, so being behind is no guarantee of catching up.

Back to Figure 1 of the notes — the cross-country income spread

Income per person differs across countries by a factor of thirty or more. Run that through the model: kˆ* = (s/(δ+n+g))1/(1−α), and with α = ⅓ the exponent is only 1.5 — so plausible differences in savings rates and population growth produce income differences of maybe two- or three-fold, nowhere near thirty.

The arithmetic doesn't reach. The residual has to sit in A — differences in technology, and in everything a country's institutions do to how effectively its capital and workers are actually used. Which is exactly the variable slide 28 handed us for free. The model's biggest empirical gap is the term it does not explain.

Compute · then reveal, one fact at a time

Is the U.S. saving too little?

The golden rule is a test a real economy can be put to. With population growth and technology the condition is MPK − δ = n + g: above it, too little capital; below it, too much.

Question 13 (compute · on paper)

Reveal the three facts one at a time, and after all three work out δ and MPK yourself before pressing the verdict. Two divisions, that's all.

Fact 1
k = 2.5 y

The capital stock is about 2.5 times one year's GDP.

Fact 2
δk = 0.1 y

Depreciation runs about 10% of GDP a year.

Fact 3
MPK × k = 0.3 y

Capital income is about 30% of GDP — capital's share.

Switch of units, flag it: here n + g = 0.03, the realistic figure — roughly 1% population growth and 2% productivity growth. Our cartoon calibration used 0.05 and 0.05 to keep the arithmetic clean; this slide uses the real world.

Solve for depreciation
δk / k = 0.1y / 2.5y
δ = 0.04
Solve for MPK
(MPK × k) / k = 0.3y / 2.5y
MPK = 0.12
The verdict
MPK − δ = 0.12 − 0.04 = 0.08  >  0.03 = n + g

The net return on an extra unit of capital is 8% a year; the golden rule says it should be bid down to 3%. The United States is below the golden-rule capital stock — it has too little capital, and would reach a permanently higher level of consumption per worker by saving more.

Read the inequality as marginal benefit against marginal cost one last time: a unit of capital returns 12%, costs 4% a year to keep alive, and only 3% is needed to keep pace with more workers and better technology. There is 5 percentage points of unclaimed surplus sitting in every unit of capital not yet built.

So why hasn't anyone done it? · argue this one out on paper

If the calculation is that clean and the gap that large, why has no government simply raised the savings rate and collected the higher consumption?

Because of who pays. Getting to the higher steady state means saving more now, which means consuming less now — for as long as the transition takes, which on these parameters is decades. The generation that pays the bill is not the generation that collects. The golden rule compares two steady states and says nothing about the path between them, and the path is where all the politics is.

Notice that this is not an objection to the model — the model computed the destination correctly. It is a reminder that “this steady state is better” and “moving to it is an improvement” are different claims, and the second one needs a judgment about whose consumption counts.
