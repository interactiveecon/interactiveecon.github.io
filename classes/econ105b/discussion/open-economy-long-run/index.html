Part 1 of 5

The Accounting
Spine

Before any behaviour, any curve, any policy: two identities that must hold no matter what anyone does. Everything else in this discussion is a story about which side of them moved.

Identity → ε → SOE → LOE → Synthesis
Throughline: net exports are decided in the capital market, not the currency market. The real exchange rate is the messenger — it moves to whatever value makes NX equal S − I.
Read the equations before you compute

Where net exports come from

Open the economy and output has four destinations — but only the domestic part of each counts, plus what foreigners buy from us:

Step 1 · the open-economy identity
Y = Cd + Id + Gd + X
Cd, Id, Gd — spending on domestically produced goods  ·  X — exports
Step 2 · split total spending
C = Cd + Cf
I = Id + If
G = Gd + Gf
Step 3 · substitute back
Y = (C − Cf) + (I − If) + (G − Gf) + X
Y = C + I + G + X − IM
Y = C + I + G + NX

Nothing behavioural has happened yet. This is bookkeeping: the Cf + If + Gf we stripped out of each term is imports.

Rearrange it, then read it

What a trade deficit actually says

NX = Y − (C + I + G)   ⇒   net exports = output − domestic spending
Question 1 (write)

In one sentence: what does a trade deficit tell you about this country's spending relative to its output? And — careful — does a deficit mean the country exports nothing?

Reveal

A deficit means total consumption exceeds production. Households, firms and the government together are consuming more goods than domestic factors of production made. Those extra goods have to come from somewhere — they come from abroad, and we call them imports.

No, exports are not zero. NX is a net figure. A country with a deficit can be exporting enormously; it is simply importing more. Students who read "trade deficit" as "we don't sell anything abroad" have misread a difference as a level — and they will make the same mistake with S − I in ten minutes.

Flip it: if spending is less than output, the excess goods are sold abroad and NX > 0. Again, imports are not zero.

Read the equations before you predict

The second identity — and it is the important one

Start from the identity you just derived and subtract consumption and government purchases from both sides:

Derivation
Y = C + I + G + NX
Y − C − G = I + NX   ← the left side is national saving, S
S = I + NX
S − I = NX
Left side · S − I
Net capital outflow
What domestic residents lend abroad, minus what foreigners lend to us. A capital-market quantity.
Right side · NX
The trade balance
Exports minus imports. A goods-market quantity.

Two completely different markets, forced to the same number by an identity. That equals sign is the whole chapter.

Predict

A country runs a trade surplus: it sells more goods and services to the rest of the world than it buys from them.

Question 2 (predict)

Is that country a net buyer of foreign assets, or a net seller of its own assets to foreigners? Write down a commitment — and one sentence saying why — before the next slide.

Don't derive it from the identity yet. Guess it from the goods. If you get it right for the wrong reason, that will show up in a moment.

The goods intuition

They cannot pay you back in goods

NX > 0  ⇒  S − I > 0  ⇒  net capital outflow is positive
Reveal

A net buyer of foreign assets. Here is why, without touching the algebra.

You ship them goods. The natural way for them to compensate you is to ship goods back — that is what imports are. But a surplus means precisely that they did not ship back enough goods. So they settle the balance the only other way available: by handing over assets. They sell you their bonds, their equity, claims on their future output.

Acquiring those claims is a form of saving. That is why S − I rises exactly in step with NX — not by coincidence, but because they are the same transaction described from two sides.

Run it backwards for a deficit: we consume more than we produce, so we hand over claims on ourselves. Foreigners lend to us, buy our debt, buy stock in our firms. S − I < 0.

Capital flows take many forms — loans between countries, purchases of government or corporate debt, foreigners buying shares in a domestic company. The model does not care which.

Question 3 · diagnose · silent poll

One of these three is reasoning badly

All three statements below are about the same identity. Two are sound. One reaches a familiar-sounding claim by a route that does not hold up.

Students pick a number on paper. No hands until everyone has written.

Why the reasoning fails

Statement 2 has the co-movement exactly right and the causation backwards. Capital inflows and trade deficits do move together — statement 1 and statement 3 say so too. But an identity is not a causal claim. S − I = NX is true by construction; it holds every year, in every country, whatever anyone's motives were.

To say what caused the deficit you need a model, and in this model the primitives are saving and investment — set by Ȳ, T, G and the interest rate. Those move first. NX is what falls out. "Foreigners bought our assets, therefore we import more" gets the arrow pointing from the accounting residual to the primitive.

Connect it to the throughline: net exports are decided in the capital market. That is the claim statement 2 is closest to getting right — and it is precisely the claim it inverts.

Part 2 of 5

The Real Exchange
Rate as a Price

ε is not a mysterious international variable. It is a relative price of goods, and prices are set by supply and demand — so we are going to find the supply curve and the demand curve.

Identity ✓ → ε → SOE → LOE → Synthesis
Throughline: net exports are decided in the capital market, not the currency market. The real exchange rate is the messenger — it moves to whatever value makes NX equal S − I.
Read the equations before you compute

Two exchange rates, one convention

State this convention out loud — everything downstream depends on it
e = units of foreign currency per unit of domestic currency
e = 80 ¥/$  →  one dollar buys eighty yen.
e ↑ = appreciation — the domestic currency buys more foreign currency, so it is more valuable.
e ↓ = depreciation — it buys less.

The nominal rate has a problem: it says nothing about what the money buys. One dollar gets you 80 yen — but does a dollar buy 80 times as much in the U.S. as a yen buys in Japan? That depends on prices. So deflate it:

The real exchange rate
ε = e × (P / P*)
ε = nominal rate × (domestic price level ÷ foreign price level)
Units: foreign goods per domestic good. It is a relative price of goods, not of money.
Compute · then say it in words

One car, two countries

The data
American car:  $25,000
Japanese car:  ¥4,000,000
Nominal rate:  80 ¥/$
Question 4 (write)

Compute ε. Then write the number as an English sentence — "one American car is worth ______". The sentence is the part that matters.

The arithmetic
ε = (80 ¥/$) × ($25,000 ÷ ¥4,000,000)
   = ¥2,000,000 ÷ ¥4,000,000
   = 0.5

The middle line is the whole trick: convert the American car into yen first (80 × 25,000 = 2 million yen), then compare it to a Japanese car at 4 million yen.

Say it out loud

"One American car buys half a Japanese car."

ε is not a number of dollars or a number of yen. It is goods per good. That is exactly why it, and not e, is the price that governs trade flows: an importer cares how many foreign cars a domestic car is worth, not how many yen a dollar fetches.

In the model we use whole price levels rather than one good, so ε = e·P/P* is the same calculation done for the basket.

Reason it through

What a high ε does to trade

Suppose ε rises. That could happen because the nominal rate rose, or because domestic prices rose relative to foreign prices — the model does not care which.

Question 5 (write)

Which way do exports (X) and imports (IM) move, and why? Write the "why" as a sentence about who finds what expensive — not just two arrows.

Reveal

A high ε means one domestic good trades for a lot of foreign goods — which is another way of saying domestic goods are expensive in foreign terms, and foreign goods are cheap in ours.

So foreigners buy fewer of our goods: X ↓. And we buy more of theirs: IM ↑. Both move against the trade balance, so NX falls.

Run it the other way and every sign flips: a low ε makes domestic goods cheap abroad, so X ↑, IM ↓, and NX rises.

ε↑ → domestic goods expensive abroad → X↓ and IM↑ → NX↓
The first curve

Net exports as a function of ε

NX(ε) — downward sloping

Note the axes: ε is on the vertical axis and NX on the horizontal. That is the convention for the rest of the deck, and it is worth ten seconds of confusion now rather than twenty minutes later.

The justification, not just the curve

Every point on this curve is a different relative price of goods. Move up it and domestic output becomes dearer in foreign terms, so foreigners buy less of it and we buy more of theirs. Both effects push NX left.

The slope therefore comes entirely from substitution in the goods market. There is no capital, no interest rate, and no saving anywhere in this curve — which is exactly why it will turn out to be unable to set NX on its own.

The move that makes the model work

ε is a price, so find its supply and demand

If ε is a price, then it is determined the way every price is: by the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied. So name them.

Demand · NX(ε)
Net demand for domestic currency
When exports exceed imports, foreigners need more of our currency to pay for our goods. When imports exceed exports, they need less. NX(ε) is that net demand — and it slopes down in ε.
Supply · S − I
Supply of domestic currency
Net capital outflow is domestic residents sending funds abroad — putting domestic currency onto world financial markets. That is the supply side.
S − I = NX(ε)   ⇒   supply = demand   ⇒   ε is pinned down

Read that equation from left to right and the throughline falls out on its own. The left side is a capital-market quantity. It does not contain ε. So the left side is a number, and ε is whatever it takes on the right to hit it.

The market for foreign exchange

A vertical supply meets a downward-sloping demand

The foreign-exchange market

The horizontal position of the red line is decided in the loanable-funds market, before we ever get here. The blue curve then tells us the only ε consistent with it.

Read the picture in the right order

Left to right, not up and down. First S − I fixes the horizontal position. Then ε slides to wherever NX(ε) crosses it. The exchange rate is the last thing to be determined, not the first.

Reverse that order and you get every wrong answer in this chapter.

Question 6 · quick check · the hinge for Part 3

Why is the S − I line vertical?

Not a trivia question. Everything surprising in the next twenty minutes is a consequence of the answer.

Commit on paper. Then reveal.

The reasoning

Option 1. Look at what S and I are made of. S = Ȳ − C(Ȳ − T) − G — output, taxes, government purchases, none of which is a function of the exchange rate. I = I(r) — the interest rate, which in a small open economy is pinned at r*. So ε is simply absent from both. A curve that does not respond to the variable on the vertical axis is a vertical line.

Option 2 is the opposite of our assumption. We have assumed perfect capital mobility. Immobile capital would force S − I to zero, which is a different model entirely.

Option 3 confuses the conclusion with the assumption. NX will indeed turn out to be fixed in some experiments — but as a result of S − I being fixed, not as an assumption about trade policy. Part 3 makes that distinction pay.

Read the equations before you predict

The small open economy in four lines

Carried over from Chapter 3
Ȳ = F(K̄, L̄)  output fixed by factors
C = C(Ȳ − T)  consumption from disposable income
I = I(r)  investment falls in r
The one new assumption
r = r*
Small — this country's actions have a negligible effect on the world economy.
Perfect capital mobility — residents have full access to world financial markets.
The arbitrage argument · say it, do not just assert it

From an investor's point of view all small economies offer the same opportunity. So if this country offered a rate below r*, no foreign funds would come and domestic savings would leave for the better return elsewhere — pushing the rate back up. If it offered a rate above r*, funds would pour in until the rate was competed back down. Neither gap survives. Hence r = r*.

NX = [Ȳ − C(Ȳ − T) − G] − I(r*)  =  S̄ − I(r*)

Every term on the right is now a number. That is the sentence to underline: saving does not have to equal investment, and neither side is waiting on the exchange rate.

The model, assembled

Why a small open economy lends abroad

Here the world rate sits above the rate this country would have had on its own. Read the left panel first, then carry the number across.

1 · Loanable funds — fixes S − I
2 · Foreign exchange — ε adjusts

Why capital leaves. If domestic savers could only invest at home, the rate would be bid down to rc at point A. But the world pays r* > rc, so they lend abroad instead. The horizontal distance B′→B is that lending — and by the identity it is also the trade surplus.

From here on: r* = rc, so NX starts at zero in all four experiments. Same starting point, four shocks — that is what makes them comparable.

Experiment 1 · the mechanism

Twin deficits — and the step almost everyone skips

Reveal · the answer

NX falls. A budget deficit and a trade deficit arrive together — the twin deficits. Higher G cuts national saving, saving minus investment goes negative, and the exchange rate appreciates until net exports have fallen to match.

Why r returns to r*

Write the chain as "S falls, therefore ε rises" and you have the right answer with a broken mechanism. Nothing in that sentence explains why capital comes in.

What actually happens: lower saving means a shortage of loanable funds, which — exactly as in the closed economy — pushes the domestic real interest rate up. That rise is real but temporary, and we do not draw it. It is the bait. The higher return attracts foreign investors, capital flows in, and the inflow keeps arriving until the rate has been competed back down to r*.

So r ends where it started — but only after net capital outflow has fallen. The overshoot is what does the work; the final r just does not record it.

G↑ → S↓ → shortage of funds → r temporarily ↑ → capital flows in → r back to r*, S−I↓ → ε↑ → NX↓

The same story in goods, with no money in it at all: a net capital inflow means the country is consuming more than it produces — that is why it is borrowing. A country consuming more than it produces must be importing more than it exports.

Same policy. Opposite sign. It depends on where.

What to take away

Identical policies, opposite effects on our trade balance — and no contradiction anywhere. The sign of ΔNX is not a property of "fiscal expansion." It is a property of which side of S − I the shock lands on.

That is the throughline doing real work. If you can say which way S − I moved, you already know which way ε and NX moved. If you can only say "the government spent more," you know nothing yet.

Note the asymmetry buried here: a small foreign economy doing the same thing would have no effect on us at all. Size is what lets a policy touch r*.

Read the mechanism, not just the arrows

Before the shift, households were saving enough to meet domestic demand for loanable funds. After it, national saving is insufficient — firms have to borrow from abroad to make up the shortage. That inward borrowing is a fall in net capital outflow.

As in Experiment 1, the shortage puts temporary upward pressure on the domestic rate, which is what draws the foreign funds in; once they arrive the rate is back at r*. Less domestic currency is supplied to world markets, its price rises, ε appreciates, and net exports fall.

In goods: by borrowing, the country is plainly consuming more than it produces — so it must be importing more than it exports.

Same sign as Experiment 1, and for the same underlying reason: both shocks made S − I smaller. Experiment 1 shrank the left term; this one grew the right term. The identity does not care which.

Experiment 4 · the punchline

Why

Trade policy touches neither saving nor investment. A tariff is not in the saving function and it is not in the investment function, so S − I is exactly the number it was. The vertical line does not move a pixel.

The rightward shift in NX(ε) therefore cannot end anywhere except directly above A. The excess demand for domestic currency at ε₁ bids ε up, and it keeps rising until exports have fallen by precisely the amount imports fell. Both are lower; the difference between them is untouched.

This is the throughline in its sharpest form. The currency market moved — dramatically. Net exports did not, because nothing happened in the capital market.

Question 11 · diagnose · silent poll

Three defences of the same conclusion

All three reach the correct conclusion — a tariff does not raise net exports. Two of them get there honestly. One is right by accident.

Why the reasoning fails

Statement 2 gets the right answer with a mechanism the model never used. Go back through slide 32: at no point did any foreign government do anything. Retaliation was not assumed, drawn, or needed.

Neutrality holds with zero retaliation. That is what makes the result surprising and what makes it strong. Exports fall because our own currency appreciated — a price effect inside our own model — not because anyone abroad punished us. Statement 2 replaces an unavoidable equilibrium force with an optional political choice.

Test it the way you should test any mechanism: ask what would happen if it were false. If no country retaliates, statement 2 predicts the tariff works. Statements 1 and 3 still predict NX unchanged — and they are right.

Retaliation is a real phenomenon and would make the welfare loss worse. It is simply not what delivers neutrality.

Restate it before moving on

Composition, not balance

Net exports are decided in the capital market, not the currency market.

The real exchange rate is the messenger. It carries whatever number S − I has already set, and it will move as far as it has to in order to deliver it. On slide 32 it moved a great deal — and delivered exactly the same message as before.

the volume of trade

the real exchange rate ε ↑

A country that genuinely wants to change its trade balance has to change its saving or its investment. Tariffs, quotas and subsidies are aimed at the wrong market — which is why the model calls them a policy about composition, not about the balance.

And note what "unchanged NX" is not saying: that the tariff was harmless. Less trade at a worse relative price is a real welfare cost. The model is silent on nothing here — it says the cost is paid and the stated goal is not achieved.

Either way the conclusion is the same: r is now determined inside the model, not handed to it.

Read the equations before you graph

Net capital outflow becomes a curve

Put yourself in an investor's shoes. Your willingness to put funds into the U.S. rises with the U.S. interest rate and falls with the returns available elsewhere — for which we use r* as the proxy. Flip that around: capital flowing out of the U.S. falls when the domestic rate rises.

The new object
CF = CF(r, r*)
Downward sloping in r. Positive CF = lending abroad; negative CF = borrowing from abroad.
If the U.S. pays 5% and the world pays 7%, funds leave — CF is high. Raise the U.S. rate and they stay.
Loanable funds — savers have two uses
S = I + CF
S̄ = I(r) + CF(r, r*)

Lend to domestic firms, or lend overseas. Note S − I = CF — the same net capital outflow as before, now given its own name and its own curve.

Foreign exchange — unchanged from Part 1
NX = S − I
NX(ε) = CF(r, r*)

The identity was never a small-economy result. It holds for large and small economies alike — which is exactly why the throughline survives this part intact.

Callback · flat versus steep, one more time

The whole difference is a slope

You have run this comparison before, in Worksheet 0 and again on the LM and IS curves. Same vocabulary, new curve — and once again everything downstream is a consequence of the slope alone.

Model A · small open economy
CF perfectly flat at r*
Infinitely responsive capital. r cannot differ from r*, so any shock is absorbed entirely by the quantity CF.
Model B · large open economy
CF downward sloping in r
Finitely responsive capital. A higher r keeps funds at home, so r can move — and every shock is split between r and CF.
Everything downstream follows from this one slope

The small open economy is not a different model. It is Model B with the CF curve laid flat — the limiting case where capital is infinitely responsive. Rotate the red curve until it is horizontal and every Part 3 result reappears.

So when a Part 4 answer differs from its Part 3 counterpart, do not memorise it as a new fact. Ask: what does a shock get to do here that it could not do when the curve was flat? The answer is always the same — it gets to move r.

The equilibrium, built in three steps

r₁ and ε₁ are determined jointly

Say this out loud — it is the part students get wrong on exams

The arrows run left to right, but that does not mean r₁ is settled before ε₁ exists. Look at panel 1: the curve is I(r) + CF(r, r*), and CF is part of the very same system that produces ε. r₁ and ε₁ are determined jointly — the three panels are one equilibrium drawn in three pieces, not a sequence of three separate markets.

The left-to-right order is how you solve it with a pencil. It is not the order in which the economy decides anything.

Where the difference comes from

In both economies the fall in saving has to be absorbed. The question is by what.

In the small open economy investment cannot help — r is stuck at r*, so I does not move, and the entire reduction in saving is taken out of net capital outflow. NX falls by the full amount.

In the large open economy the interest rate rises, so domestic investment absorbs part of the shock. Only the remainder is left for CF. Less CF movement means less exchange-rate movement, and a smaller fall in net exports.

With the numbers behind these graphs, the SOE fall in NX is exactly twice the LOE fall. Nothing sacred about the factor of two — it is set by how steep CF is. What is general: crowding out at home is the mirror image of a smaller trade effect abroad.

This is the shape of every Part 4 result. Ask what r does; whatever r absorbs, the exchange rate does not have to.

What delivers it is much cheaper: a tariff is not an argument of any equation in panels 1 or 2. It does not appear in S, in I(r), or in CF(r, r*). So r cannot move, so CF cannot move, so the vertical line in panel 3 holds still — and every rightward shift of NX(ε) has nowhere to go but straight up.

Option 1 has the right instinct and the wrong trigger. Yes, r is free to move in the LOE. But "free to move" is not "moved." Something has to push it, and a tariff does not touch anything that could.

Experiment · a rise in the world interest rate

Two competing effects — and one of them always wins

The world rate rises (another large economy raised its G, say). Investing abroad is now more attractive, so both the CF curve and the I + CF curve shift right. But the rightward shift raises the domestic rate too — and a higher r pulls capital back home. So which way does CF end up?

r* = 5.0  ·  r = 5.0  ·  r* − r = 0.0  ·  I = 750  ·  CF = 0  ·  ε = 1.00  ·  NX = 0
Watch the two shifts, they are not the same size

Measure each shift vertically — in interest-rate units — and the asymmetry is obvious. The CF curve shifts up by the full rise in r*, because r* is the only thing in it. The I + CF curve shifts up by less, because half of it is investment, and investment does not care what the world rate is.

So the domestic rate rises by less than r* did. The gap r* − r therefore widens — and CF, which depends on that gap, rises on net. The homeward pull from a higher r is real, but it is never enough to reverse the outward push.

More domestic currency reaches world financial markets, so its price falls: ε depreciates, and net exports rise.

r*↑ → CF curve shifts up more than I+CF → r↑ but by less → (r* − r)↑ → CF↑ → ε↓ → NX↑

Same sign as the small open economy's Experiment 2 — and again about half the size, for the same reason: investment absorbed part of the shock.

Question 15 (compute)

Foreign inflation π* = 6%. Domestic inflation π = 2%. The real exchange rate is unchanged, so %Δε = 0. What happens to the nominal exchange rate e? Give a number and a direction, then say in words why it moves that way.

Reveal

%Δe = 0 + (6 − 2) = +4%. The domestic currency appreciates by 4% — one unit of it buys 4% more foreign currency than last year.

Why, in words: inflation is the rate at which a currency loses value. The foreign currency is losing value faster than ours. So to keep the real price of goods where it was, households have to be handed more units of the fast-depreciating currency per unit of ours. The nominal rate moves purely to offset the inflation gap — nothing real happened at all.

The empirical test

Set %Δε = 0 and the equation predicts a slope of exactly one: a country whose inflation runs one point above the U.S. should see its currency lose one point of value against the dollar. The cloud does sit roughly along that 45° line. Iceland, Pakistan and Mexico inflated and depreciated; Switzerland, Japan and Singapore did not, and appreciated.

Note what the scatter is not. It is not tight, and it should not be — %Δε is not literally zero for any of these countries, and real exchange rates do move. What the picture establishes is that the inflation differential term dominates over long horizons. That is exactly the claim the equation makes.

Be honest about the model's reach

Partly — and it matters which part. The model speaks to the total: NX = S − I. The persistent overall U.S. deficit is a statement about American saving being low relative to American investment, and the model handles that directly.

The goods-versus-services split is composition, and the model is silent on it. Nothing in S − I distinguishes a container of steel from a software licence. Explaining why the U.S. runs a deficit in goods and a surplus in services takes comparative advantage — a different chapter, a different question.

That is the same distinction Part 3 drew about tariffs. The model sets the balance; it does not choose the mix.

Both blanks. The first one is the mechanism; the second one is what the economy does instead. A sentence that fills only the first blank has not finished the argument.
