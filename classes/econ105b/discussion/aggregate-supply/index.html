Aggregate Supply:
Who Gets to Reprice?

One number decides whether a demand shock becomes output or inflation: the share of firms that cannot change their price. We build that number from a single firm and end with the whole SRAS curve.

Work on your own paper. Write before each reveal — the point is your reasoning, not a clean final answer.

Firm → LRAS → Sticky/Flexible → SRAS
Where we are

AD tells you the shift. It can't tell you the split.

Last time we built the aggregate demand curve out of IS–LM and worked out what shifts it: fiscal policy, monetary policy, anything that moves spending at a given price level.

G↑ or M↑ → AD shifts right → ???

But an AD shift only tells you that the economy moves along some supply relationship. It says nothing about how the movement splits between more output and higher prices — and that split is the entire policy question. A boom that is all Y is a success. A boom that is all P is inflation with nothing to show for it.

Where this ends up

The answer turns out to be one number. Not a new theory of firms, not a new market — one share, between 0 and 1. We'll build it from a single firm deciding what to charge, and by the end it will be the slope of the whole SRAS curve.

Pricing Rule

One monopolistically competitive firm

Read the equations before you compute

The firm faces its own downward-sloping demand curve and maximizes profit, Π = TR − TC. That gives the familiar condition MR = MC.

Inverse demand
P = 9 − q
Marginal revenue
MR = 9 − 2q
Marginal cost
MC = q

The intercept 9 is the demand shifter — call it A. It's the only thing we will shock all discussion.

Question 1 (write)

Find the firm's profit-maximizing quantity q and the price P it charges. Careful: which curve do you read the price off?

Question 1

A price-setter, not a price-taker

The firm at A = 9
Set MR = MC
9 − 2q = q
9 = 3q  ⇒  q = 3
price off demand: P = 9 − 3 = 6
(MC at q = 3 is only 3)
Reveal

q = 3, P = 6. Read the quantity off MR = MC, but read the price off the demand curve — MR is what the firm earns on the marginal unit, not what any customer pays.

The part that matters for the rest of the hour: P = 6 sits well above MC = 3. Because demand slopes down, this firm chooses its price rather than being handed one. That single fact is what makes the whole unit possible — a price-taker has no price to be sticky about.

From one firm to the economy

Where does the firm's demand come from?

Question 2 (write)

We just took A = 9 as given. But what actually determines the demand facing one firm? Name the two things.

Reveal

Aggregate demand, plus the firm's price relative to the price level. When the economy is booming, everyone's demand curve shifts out. And at any level of aggregate spending, a firm that charges more than its rivals loses customers to them.

AD↑ → firm's D↑ → MR↑ → q↑, p↑
The hinge

Notice what a firm must do to set a price: forecast the aggregate price level P and aggregate output Y. A price posted today has to be right for an economy the firm can only guess at. Every remaining slide is a consequence of that — some firms get to revise the guess, and some are stuck with it.

Question 4 (write)

Something has to give. Where did the extra output go? Don't answer "it didn't happen" — the firm's calculation was correct. Find the market we've been ignoring.

The market we ignored

Factor markets close the loop

Reveal · the mechanism

We held MC fixed while every price in the economy rose. That's the error. Workers don't care about the nominal wage W, they care about the real wage W/P — and in the long run W/P is pinned down by the marginal product of labor, which depends on the capital stock and the labor force, not on the price level.

So when P rises, W/P falls below MPL, workers are worse off at the old wage, and W is bid up one-for-one with P until W/P = MPL again. Capital owners run the identical argument: R/P = MPK. Every input price rises by the same proportion as output prices.

Which means the firm's marginal cost was never going to stay put. MC rises until MR′ = MC′ back at q = 3.

P↑ → W/P↓ → W↑ & R↑ → MC↑ → q back to 3

Nothing forced the firm to contract. It expanded because expanding was profitable, then contracted because it stopped being profitable — both times freely.

Our firm · MC catches up
The punchline

In the long run, demand moves prices — not output

Every nominal magnitude went up together: output prices, wages, rents. Every real magnitude — quantity produced, the real wage, the real rental rate — came back exactly where it started. Aggregate demand had no lasting grip on anything real.

Why this is the same result you already know

Long-run output is determined by capital, labor, and technology — the production function — with no role for the demand side at all. LRAS is vertical at Ȳ, so long-run growth comes from technology and population, and never from printing money or raising G.

long run: ΔAD → ΔP only
Ȳ moves only when K, L, or technology move

Hold on to why it was vertical: not because production was impossible, but because everyone got to reprice. Part 3 takes that freedom away from some of them.

Question 5 · commit a number on paper before the reveal
Sentence 1 · the flaw

Nothing in this model is at capacity. Watch what our firm actually did: it went from 3 to 5 units the instant demand rose — freely, with no constraint binding — and then walked back to 3 only once its costs caught up. Capacity never entered the argument.

Why the bad reasoning is expensive: a "capacity ceiling" story predicts that output can never exceed Ȳ. But the entire rest of this discussion is about booms where Y > Ȳ. If you hold the capacity version, Part 4 will look impossible. Ȳ is the level firms choose when all prices are right — not a wall.

The short run is a repricing problem

Some prices move today. Most don't.

"Short run" in this model doesn't mean "a few months." It means the window before every price has been reset. Some prices in the economy change by the second; others are locked in by contract, print run, or plain inertia.

Question 8 · the answer

Same shock, same costs, opposite responses

Firm F · flexible ✓ can reprice
15 − 2q = q ⇒ q = 5, P = 10
Δq = +2   ΔP = +$4
Firm S · sticky ✗ cannot reprice
6 = q ⇒ q = 6, P = 6
Δq = +3   ΔP = $0
The punchline · first inversion

The firm that can't raise its price expands more

Why — the mechanism, not the result

When the sticky firm's price is frozen at $6, it stops facing a downward-sloping demand curve. Every extra unit it sells brings in exactly $6, so its fixed price becomes its marginal revenue — a flat line at 6 instead of a line falling twice as fast as demand.

At its old quantity of 3, marginal cost is only 3 — so it is earning $6 a unit against $3 of cost, with demand now abundant at that bargain price. It keeps producing until MC finally reaches 6 at q = 6.

The flexible firm never gets that far, because it does the thing the sticky firm can't: it raises its price to $10, choking off part of its own demand. It captures the shock as margin; the sticky firm has no choice but to capture it as volume.

sticky ✗ :  AD↑ → p̄ = MR → q↑↑, p unchanged
flexible ✓ :  AD↑ → MR′↑ → q↑, p↑↑
Hold on to this

Stickiness makes the real economy MORE responsive, not less. The instinct that "stuck prices mean a stuck economy" is exactly backwards — stuck prices are precisely why quantities have to do the adjusting.

Quick check

Scale it up to the whole economy

An economy where a larger fraction of firms have sticky prices gets hit by an aggregate demand boom.

Part 4 of 4

Building the
SRAS Curve

Take the mix of sticky and flexible firms, average their prices, and the aggregate supply curve falls out — slope and all.

Firm ✓ → LRAS ✓ → Sticky/Flexible ✓ → SRAS
Throughline: one parameter — the share of firms that cannot reprice, s — decides whether an AD shock becomes output or prices.
Two kinds of price-setter

Read the equations before you compute

Every firm has the same desired price. What differs is when they get to act on it.

Flexible firm · sets it today
p = P + a(Y − Ȳ)
Sticky firm · set it in advance
p = EP + a(EY − Ȳ)

P — costs and relative-price positioning. If everyone else's prices are 10% higher, so are your input costs, and charging the old price would make you 10% cheap.

a(Y − Ȳ) — demand pressure. A booming economy bids up inputs and pushes you along your MC curve, so your desired markup rises with the output gap. Set a = 1, Ȳ = 1000, EP = 100.

Question 10 (write)

The textbook writes the sticky firm's rule simply as p = EP. Why does the second term drop out? What must a firm setting a price in advance believe about the output gap?

Reveal

Because a firm's best guess at the output gap is zero. It has no way to know today whether the shock coming next quarter will be a boom or a bust, so on average it expects the economy to sit at its natural level: EY = Ȳ, which kills the whole second term.

That leaves p = EP. Note what this does not say: it doesn't say sticky firms are foolish or lazy. They're forecasting optimally. They're just forecasting — and the shock, by definition, is the part nobody forecast.

Average the two groups

The price level is a weighted average

A share s of firms is stuck at EP; the remaining (1 − s) set the desired price today. The aggregate price level is just the average of what everyone charges.

Line by line
P = s·EP + (1−s)[P + a(Y − Ȳ)]
expand the bracket
P = s·EP + (1−s)P + (1−s)a(Y − Ȳ)
collect the P terms on the left
P − (1−s)P = s·EP + (1−s)a(Y − Ȳ)
the left side is just sP
sP = s·EP + (1−s)a(Y − Ȳ)
divide everything by s
P = EP + a[(1−s)/s](Y − Ȳ)

That last division by s is the whole slide. The stickiness share ends up in the denominator of the slope.

Two implications

1. Expected prices become actual prices. The EP term says the price level inherits whatever firms expected it to be. Expectations aren't commentary on the economy — they are literally a component of it.

2. How much flexible repricing moves the index depends on how many firms are doing it. Flexible firms want to raise price by a(Y − Ȳ). But they're only (1 − s) of the index, and they must move the whole average — so each has to move by 1/s times as much to shift P by a given amount. Few repricers, big individual moves; the slope is a(1−s)/s.

Commit before computing · second inversion

Predict

SRAS:  P = EP + a[(1−s)/s](Y − Ȳ)
Question 11 (predict)

Before touching a number: as s falls toward 0 — fewer and fewer firms stuck, more and more free to reprice — does SRAS get flatter or steeper? Commit on paper.

Answer Q11 from the algebra, not from a feeling about stickiness. Then check it against your Q7 answer — the two inversions are the same fact seen at two different scales.

The callback

Last discussion we derived the LM curve and found its slope was e/d, with the interest-sensitivity d sitting in the denominator. The surprise there: the model where money demand responded more to r gave the flatter LM.

Here the SRAS slope is a(1−s)/s, with the stickiness share s sitting in the denominator. Same shape, same surprise: as s falls, (1−s)/s grows, so fewer sticky firms give a steeper SRAS. A parameter in a denominator inverts the intuition you brought in — a habit worth building, because it will happen again.

Synthesis

One shock, one parameter, every outcome

The same 300-unit demand shock produced four completely different economies. Nothing changed but s.

s = 1 · all sticky
Y: 1000 → 1300
P: 100 → 100
✓ all output, zero inflation
s = 1/3 · mostly flexible
Y: 1000 → 1060
P: 100 → 220
✗ mostly inflation
s = 0 · all flexible
Y: 1000 → 1000
P: 100 → 250
✗ all inflation, and SRAS is LRAS
The aggregate model reproduces the two firms exactly

Part 3's sticky firm raised output and left price alone — that is the s = 1 column. Part 3's flexible firm raised price and moved output much less — push that to every firm and you get the s = 0 column, where output doesn't move at all because there are no stuck relative prices left to exploit. The macro curve is nothing but those two firms, weighted.

Throughline, restated: one parameter — the share of firms that cannot reprice, s — decides whether an AD shock becomes output or prices. The firm that cannot raise its price is the one that raises output more; stickiness makes the real economy more responsive, not less. And fewer sticky firms gives a steeper SRAS.

AD↑ → s high: Y↑↑, P→  |  s low: Y→, P↑↑

Question 13 · commit a number on paper before the reveal
Sentence 2 · the flaw

That's the naive competitive supply curve, and it runs the causality backwards. It says P rises first and firms respond with output. In this model the arrow points the other way: Y↑ → marginal cost↑ → the flexible firms reprice → P↑. Higher output causes the higher price level, not the reverse.

It also smuggles in price-takers. There is no "the price level makes it profitable" in a world of price-setters — each firm chooses its own price, and the price level is just the average of those choices.

The test that exposes it: a student holding the naive version cannot explain why s matters at all. Their story has no place to put stickiness — so they can't tell you why the same shock is all output in one economy and all inflation in another. Sentence 2 gets the slope right and the entire discussion wrong.

Where all three curves meet

Long-run equilibrium

AD, SRAS and LRAS at a single point

Here the economy sits exactly on LRAS: Y = Ȳ, no output gap, and SRAS happens to pass through the same point.

Question 14 (write)

SRAS is P = EP + a[(1−s)/s](Y − Ȳ). At this intersection, what must be true of EP? Show it from the equation, then say in words what it means about the people in this economy.

Reveal

Set Y = Ȳ. The whole second term is zero, so P = EP. SRAS crosses LRAS at exactly the price level firms expected — and nowhere else.

In words: the sticky firms guessed right. Their locked-in price is the price they would have chosen anyway, so being stuck costs them nothing and creates no gap. Long-run equilibrium is not "enough time has passed" — it is "expectations turned out to be correct."

Which raises the obvious next question: when a shock proves those expectations wrong, what happens to EP?
