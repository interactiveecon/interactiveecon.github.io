Reveal · Question 1

Four demand shocks, one supply curve

Successive positive demand shocks
And in the data — U.S., 1960–1969

Annual averages · CPI-U year-over-year and the civilian unemployment rate (BLS).

Reveal · Question 1

Each shock pushes AD right along a fixed SRAS. The price level climbs P₁ → P₄ — that is inflation. Output climbs Ȳ → Y₃, and producing more output takes more workers, so unemployment falls below its natural rate. Inflation up, unemployment down, over and over: a negative relationship.

That relationship is the Phillips curve, named for the economist who first wrote it up. When the paper was published in 1958, the relationship looked remarkably stable in the data going back to 1861 — and the 1960s U.S. data on the right kept obeying it.

Notice what did the work: demand shocks against a fixed supply curve. Hold on to both conditions — Part 5 is about what happens when either one fails.

Derive it yourself

Two lines of algebra, and you have the curve

Okun's Law · the output–unemployment link
%ΔGDP = 3% − 2Δu
unemployment 5% → 7%:
%ΔGDP = 3% − 2(7% − 5%) = 3% − 4% = −1%

These coefficients are empirically estimated, not theoretically derived — they are not universal constants like gravity, and they could change.

In the short run technology and capital are fixed, so output is determined by unemployment. That is what lets us trade one variable for the other.

Question 2 (write · 2 min)

Take the SRAS equation and subtract Pt−1 from both sides:

Pt − Pt−1 = EPt − Pt−1 + α(Yt − Ȳ) + vt

(a) What does each of the first two terms become? Name them.

(b) Now substitute the output gap for the unemployment gap, using α(Yt − Ȳ) = −β(ut − ū). Write the result.

Short write — two minutes. The substitution in (b) is the whole trick: it swaps the axis from output to unemployment, and the minus sign is where the negative slope comes from.

Reveal · Question 2, then Question 3

The short-run Phillips curve

Reveal · Question 2

(a) Pt − Pt−1 is inflation, πt. And EPt − Pt−1 is expected inflation, Eπt — expected prices measured against the prices people actually last saw.

πt = Eπt + α(Yt − Ȳ) + vt

(b) Substituting α(Yt − Ȳ) = −β(ut − ū) gives the short-run Phillips curve:

πt = Eπt − β(ut − ū) + vt

Read the special case off it: if the economy sits at the natural rate, u = ū, and there are no supply shocks, both extra terms vanish and inflation is simply equal to expected inflation. That single line is the engine of the entire rest of this discussion.

In the long run, unemployment is always at ū and there are no supply shocks. Which inflation rates is ū consistent with?

Question 3 · commit a number before the reveal
Reveal · Question 3

All of them. Set u = ū and v = 0 in the SRPC and you get π = Eπ — with no restriction whatever on what that number is. 2%, 5%, 12%: each is a perfectly good long-run equilibrium, as long as expectations have caught up to it. Plot every such point and you get a vertical long-run Phillips curve at ū.

This is the throughline in graphical form. The destination — unemployment at ū — never changes. Only the height at which you arrive does.

Question 5 (write · 3 min)

(a) Solve πt = Eπt − (ut − ū) + vt for the unemployment rate ut that delivers 2% inflation. Show the substitution line.

(b) Convert that into lost output with %ΔY = 3% − 2Δu. How much GDP growth does the disinflation cost?

Δu in Okun's Law is the change in the unemployment rate in percentage points — not the level.

Reveal · Question 5

Three points of unemployment, bought with a recession

Disinflation under adaptive expectations
Reveal · Question 5 (a) and (b)
2% = 5% − (ut − 5%) + 0
ut = 5% − 2% + 5%
ut = 8%  — a rise of 3 points
%ΔY = 3% − 2(3) = 3 − 6 = −3%

On 2022 U.S. GDP of $25.44 trillion, a 3% fall is roughly $763.2 billion of output — given up to buy three points off the inflation rate. (c) The Fed engineers it by reducing the money supply.

Reveal · what happens next period

Once inflation is actually down at 2%, adaptive expectations do their one trick: next period's forecast becomes Eπ = πt−1 = 2%. The whole short-run curve shifts down to SRPC′, and the economy returns to ū = 5% at the new, lower inflation rate — point C.

π↓ → Eπ↓ (next period) → SRPC shifts down
The sacrifice ratio — and a warning

The percentage of GDP given up per percentage point of inflation removed is the sacrifice ratio. Here: 3% of output for 3 points of inflation, so the ratio is 1.

Treat that as a floor, not an estimate. Empirical estimates vary substantially, but a typical one is around 5 — five times the pain this tidy example implies.

Question 6 · fingers on the chest, then reveal
Sentence 2 · the flaw

Unemployment rising to 8% is a movement along the curve, not a shift of it. u is the variable on the horizontal axis. Changing an axis variable slides you from A to B on the same curve — by construction it cannot move the curve, any more than walking east moves the road.

What actually shifted it is the one thing held fixed when we drew it: Eπ. Last period's realized inflation of 2% became this period's forecast, the intercept fell from 5% to 2%, and the whole line dropped.

Why this bad reasoning is expensive: it makes high unemployment look like the cause of the improvement, so you would predict that keeping unemployment at 8% keeps pushing the curve down forever. It doesn't. Unemployment was the price paid to change expectations — and in Part 3 the Fed gets the same shift without paying it at all.

Reveal · Question 7

Straight down the long-run curve

Disinflation under rational expectations
Reveal · Question 7

(a) If you believe the Fed, your expectation is 2% — immediately, this period, with no waiting.

2% = 2% − (ut − 5%) + 0
ut = 5% = ū

(b) Unemployment never leaves the natural rate. The disinflation is costless.

(c) Why: if everyone believes inflation will be 2%, firms only raise prices by 2%, and consumers, firms and government only raise their demand in expectation of 2%. Both sides of the market act on the new number, so prices in fact rise by 2%. The belief is self-fulfilling.

announce 2% → Eπ↓ → SRPC↓ → π↓ at u = ū
The point to carry forward

There is no need for the Fed to increase unemployment, because it changed inflation by changing expectations instead. Compare the two routes: adaptive expectations cost 3 points of unemployment and $763bn of output to move Eπ from 5% to 2%. Rational expectations moved the same Eπ with a press release.

Question 8 (write · 2 min)

An increase in the money supply is anticipated — everyone sees it coming. What happens to output?

Write the chain, link by link. Start at M↑ and don't stop until you've said what firms do about it.

Reveal · Question 8

M↑ shifts aggregate demand right, as always. But firms know the increase is coming, so they raise their prices in advance — which shifts SRAS upward and offsets the demand increase. The economy lands at C: the price level is higher, and output is back at Ȳ.

M↑ anticipated → EP↑ → SRAS↑ → P↑, Y unchanged

Expected monetary policy has no effect on output. Only the unexpected part of a policy can move real quantities.

Close the part · the honest verdict

Both models are caricatures, in opposite directions. Purely adaptive expectations assume people are unintelligent — capable only of repeating their most recent observation. Rational expectations assume people are incredibly smart. And in practice, it is unlikely the Fed hits its target by announcement alone.

So the real economy is somewhere between them. Part 4 asks the obvious next question: where?

Reveal · Question 10

Half the weight, none of the output loss

Reveal · (a) and (b)
Eπt = 0.5 × 5% + 0.5 × 2%
      = 2.5% + 1% = 3.5%
2% = 3.5% − (ut − 5%) + 0
ut = 3.5% − 2% + 5%
ut = 6.5%  — a rise of 1.5 points
Reveal · (c)
%ΔY = 3% − 2(1.5)
        = 3 − 3 = 0%

The Fed still reduces the money supply and still pushes unemployment up 1.5 points — but the growth rate lands at zero rather than −3%. And in the next period, expected inflation falls to 2%, so the curve completes its move on its own.

Read the 0% honestly

0% growth is not free. Trend growth in this economy is 3% — that is what the first term of Okun's Law means. A year of zero growth is a year in which the economy produced three percentage points less than it otherwise would have. Nobody was laid off relative to last year's level of output; a full year of gains was simply never collected.

Halving the weight on the past cut the unemployment cost exactly in half (3 points → 1.5). The output figure improved by more than half only because Okun's constant 3% absorbs the first 1.5 points of it.

The punchline

They were never two theories

Part 2 and Part 3 looked like rival accounts of how people think — the goldfish and the oracle. Put them on the same equation and they stop being rivals:

Eπt = e·πt−1 + (1 − e)·πTt
e = 1  →  Eπ = πt−1
that is adaptive expectations
e = 0  →  Eπ = πT
that is rational expectations (fully credible)
What this buys you

Adaptive and rational are not two competing theories of the world. They are e = 1 and e = 0 on one dial — the two endpoints of a single equation, with every real economy somewhere in between. And the position of that dial is not a fact about psychology. It is a fact about the Fed.

The unemployment cost of disinflation is set by credibility: the more credible the Fed, the smaller e, the more weight people put on the announcement, and the closer we sit to the costless case. That is precisely what the Fed is buying when it protects its target — not a number on a page, but the price of the next disinflation.

Note what never moved across all three cases: unemployment ends at ū = 5% and inflation ends at 2% every single time. The destination was never in question. Only the fare.

Question 11 (write · 2 min)

We derived the SRPC and it is still correct. So what has to be true of the world for a decade of data to trace it out? Name the conditions — and then name what broke each one.

Reveal · the two conditions

The SRPC shows a negative relationship between inflation and unemployment only if (i) inflation expectations are constant and (ii) supply shocks are absent. Every point on one curve shares the same Eπ and the same v. Let either drift and the data are sampling different curves — and a cloud of points off different curves has no reason to look like any one of them.

Reason 1 · supply shocks broke condition (ii)

A negative supply shock raises inflation and unemployment together — the opposite co-movement. Business cycles before the 1970s are generally thought to have been demand-driven, which is why the 1960s behaved. The two recessions of the 1970s were primarily supply-driven, which is why that decade loops instead of sloping.

v↑ → π↑ and u↑
Reason 2 · an active Fed broke condition (i)

The Fed has taken a more active role since the late 1970s. If it can move expectations at all — even imperfectly — then Eπ is no longer a constant sitting in the background. It is a policy variable. The curve is still there; the economy simply stops staying on any one of them long enough to draw it.

The SRPC did not stop existing. The scatter just stopped tracing it. Everything you computed in Parts 2–4 still holds — those exercises held Eπ and v fixed on purpose.

A negative supply shock
The other half of the model

Expectations don't only sit on the supply side

So far EP has done all its work inside SRAS — it is what firms think when they set prices. But consumption plausibly depends on more than disposable income, and one of those things is what you expect prices to do:

Consumption with price expectations
C = C(Y − T, EP)

If you expect higher prices in the future, you buy today. EP↑ → C↑.

The chain

Higher expected prices raise consumption. Planned expenditure now exceeds output, so inventories fall unexpectedly and firms respond by increasing output. That shifts the IS curve right, raising short-run equilibrium output at any given price level — which is exactly what it means for aggregate demand to shift right.

EP↑ → C↑ → PE↑ → U.I.↓ → Y↑ → IS→right → AD→right
Why this matters for the last slides

Expectations now move both curves: firms' expectations move SRAS up, consumers' expectations move AD right. If the two groups expect the same thing, those movements offset. If they don't, they can't. That is the whole of anchoring.

Higher expected prices shift AD right
Anchored expectations

Inflation without fluctuations — then without that

Anchored · firms and consumers expect the same inflation
The anchored case

Suppose inflation has been stable for years — roughly March 2010 to March 2020. Expectations settle close to actual inflation, so it becomes predictable, and firms and consumers end up with relatively close expectations of future prices. SRAS and AD then shift by similar amounts, period after period.

Result: the price level marches P₁ → P₄ — there is inflation — while output never leaves Ȳ. Anchored inflation causes no short-run fluctuations at all.

Reveal · Question 12

Either mismatch produces economic fluctuations. When consumers expect more inflation than firms, AD shifts further than SRAS and output comes in above Ȳ. When firms expect more, SRAS shifts further and output comes in below Ȳ. Same mechanism, opposite sign.

The genuinely destabilizing part

Read the hollow rings on each diagram — the legend under each one says which is which. Where AD′ crosses LRAS is the price level consumers expected. Where SRAS′ crosses LRAS is the price level firms expected. The realized price level P₂ sits between them — it matches neither group.

So both groups are now wrong, and — worse — neither learns whether its way of forming expectations works. Each was wrong partly because the other was. Forming next year's expectation gets harder, not easier, and if expectations come unanchored the economy can be destabilized as they spiral out of control. The hope is that the Fed re-anchors them by announcing a target — and whether that works is, once again, entirely a question of credibility. If people do not believe the Fed is willing or able to pursue the recessionary policy required, the announcement changes nothing.

Question 13 · fingers on the chest, then reveal
Sentence 2 · the flaw

The premise is right, which is exactly why this one is dangerous. Slide 26 did establish that as long as inflationary expectations are shared by all economic actors, expected inflation does not cause economic fluctuations. Taken alone, 3.5% really is as workable a resting point as 2%.

The inference is what fails. Retargeting is not a free relabelling — it spends the credibility that keeps e low. The Fed would be demonstrating that when the target becomes inconvenient, the target moves. In the next inflationary episode people would reasonably think it might move again, so they would weight the announcement less and weight the past more: e rises.

And by Part 4 that is not a rhetorical loss, it is a priced one. A higher e means the next disinflation costs more unemployment and more output — that is what the credibility dial is. The 2% target is not valuable because 2 is the right number. It is valuable because it has been kept.
