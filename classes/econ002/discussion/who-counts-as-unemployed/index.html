How to run this deck

Write first, reveal second. Every question gets 1–2 minutes of silent writing before you press a button. Nothing reveals itself — no answer, no filled-in table and no marked equilibrium is on screen when a slide loads.

The deck is one argument, in order. Build the labor market, show that it clears completely, treat that as the puzzle, and only then let the three types of unemployment arrive as three answers to it. Diagnose slides are silent polls. Numbered sentences, one subtly flawed. Fingers on the chest, not hands in the air — then reveal. The point is always why the reasoning fails, not that the conclusion is wrong.

Part 1 of 4

A category
with rules

Being without a job is not the test. There are three conditions, they all have to hold at once, and one of them is about what you did last month.

Setup · no question yet

The population, cut twice

Everyone in that box is 16 or older, a resident, not institutionalized, and not on active duty. Fail one of those screens and you are not in the diagram at all — not employed, not unemployed, not out of the labor force.

To be counted as unemployed, three things have to be true at the same time. Two out of three is not a partial score — it puts you in a different box.

1 · No job
Not working for pay at all. One paid hour in the reference week is enough to make you employed instead.
2 · Available to work
You could start if a job were offered. Someone who could not take a job this month — for any reason — fails this one.
3 · Actively looked
A real search step in the last four weeks: an application, an interview, a call. Wanting a job is not searching for one.

Reveal · Dev and Ruth

Dev is employed. The measure does not ask whether the job is enough. Ten hours a week at a coffee shop, wanting full-time work and not finding it, is a person the statistics call employed — the same word they use for a full-time salaried engineer. Whatever you think of that, it is the rule, and it means the unemployment rate is silent about hours, pay and whether the job fits.

Ruth is not unemployed — and that is the more surprising one. Ruth has no job, would take one, and has been out of work for eight months. Every ordinary sense of the word applies. But the search stopped in March, so condition 3 fails, and failing it does not demote Ruth to some lesser category of unemployment. It moves Ruth out of the labor force altogether — out of the numerator and out of the denominator at the same time. Hold on to that. The next three slides are about what it does to the unemployment rate.

Setup · two formulas, two denominators

The unemployment rate everyone quotes, and the participation rate that checks it

Unemployment rate
unemployed ÷ labor force
The denominator is the labor force: the employed plus the unemployed. Everyone outside the labor force is invisible to this number.
Participation rate
labor force ÷ working-age population
The denominator is everyone the screens let in. This is the number that notices whether people are in the labor force at all.
The thing to notice before the next slide

The two rates do not share a denominator. Move one person from unemployed to not in the labor force and the unemployment rate loses a person from its numerator and from its denominator, while the participation rate loses one from its numerator only. The same person, moving once, pushes the two rates in different directions.

labor force = employed + unemployed   |   working-age population = labor force + not in the labor force

Reveal · Q2

The unemployment rate falls, from 5.0% to 3.4%. It falls because 2,000 people were subtracted from the numerator and from the denominator at once: 4,000 ÷ 118,000 instead of 6,000 ÷ 120,000.

The participation rate falls too, from 60.0% to 59.0% — because its denominator, the working-age population, did not move. And the employed row is identical in both columns. Not one job was created. By any honest reading, this town is worse off than it was a month ago, and its unemployment rate improved by 1.6 percentage points.

The habit to carry out of Part 1

A moving unemployment rate has two stories, always

When the unemployment rate falls
people found work  — or  people stopped looking

The first is good news and the second is bad news, and the unemployment rate reports them with the same downward move. It cannot tell you which happened, because it never looks outside the labor force.

When the unemployment rate rises
conditions worsened  — or  discouraged workers came back

This is the case people forget. Early in a recovery, workers who had given up start searching again. They re-enter the labor force as unemployed, the denominator and numerator both grow, and the measured unemployment rate can climb while the labor market is improving.

people stop looking → LF↓ → U↓ → unemployment rate↓   with zero jobs created
discouraged workers return → LF↑ → U↑ → unemployment rate↑   while conditions improve
So what do you do about it

Read the two rates together. The unemployment rate tells you what is happening inside the labor force; the participation rate tells you whether the labor force itself is emptying out or filling back up. A falling unemployment rate alongside a falling participation rate is the signature of discouragement, not recovery.

Reveal · a conclusion that is usually true and never implied

Sentence 1 is the flaw, and notice how little of it is wrong. The premise is fine, the conclusion is often correct in practice, and the word doing the damage is so.

The unemployment rate is a fraction, and a fraction falls when the numerator shrinks or when the denominator grows relative to it. Hiring shrinks the numerator. So does giving up. The town on the last slide produced exactly this fall in the unemployment rate — 5.0% to 3.4% — with the employed row unchanged. Nothing in a falling unemployment rate, on its own, distinguishes the two.

Sentences 2 and 3 are both simply true, and they are the tools: sentence 3 is why Ruth left the labor force, and sentence 2 is the check that would have caught the mistake. Whenever the unemployment rate moves, ask which part of the fraction moved.

A market that
clears completely

Now build the labor market the way we built the market for burritos: a downward-sloping demand, an upward-sloping supply, a crossing point. Follow it honestly and it hands you a number that cannot possibly be right.

Throughline: unemployment is not simply “not working.” It is a measured category with rules — and it never reaches zero because three different things keep the labor market from clearing.
Setup · the same two curves, a different market

Labor is bought and sold like anything else

Labor demand · LD
W = 24 − 2L
How many workers firms want to hire. It falls in the wage: labor is a cost, so the dearer it is, the fewer hours a firm buys.
Labor supply · LS
W = 4 + 2L
How many people are looking for jobs. It rises in the wage: the better the pay, the more people are willing to give up their time for it.
Why the vertical axis says real wage

Neither side cares about the number printed on the check. Firms care what an hour of work costs them in goods they could have sold, and workers care what an hour of work buys. That is W/P — the wage divided by the price level.

This market is deliberately stylized: ten workers, round intercepts, clean crossings. Read the mechanism off it, never the magnitudes — the percentages it implies are nothing like real ones.

Reveal · Q4

Into LD: 18 = 24 − 2L, so L = 3. Into LS: 18 = 4 + 2L, so L = 7. At $18, firms want three workers and seven people want jobs.

Stop there. You can see there is a gap, and you can already guess what it is called. Do not name it yet — whether a gap like this can survive is exactly the question Part 3 exists to answer.

Setup · the crossing point, two ways

Where the two curves agree

Route one · look at it

One point sits on both curves at once. There the number of workers firms want and the number of people looking are the same number.

Route two · set them equal
24 − 2L = 4 + 2L
20 = 4L
L = 5
W/P = 4 + 2(5) = $14

Check it against LD as well: 24 − 2(5) = 14. Both curves give the same wage at the same number of workers, which is what makes it the crossing point.

Reveal · Q5

Zero

0

At $14, firms want five workers and exactly five people are looking. Every single person who wants a job at the going wage has one.

Reveal · Q5 · and be careful with the sixth person

Somebody is standing at home at $14 — the sixth person, who would work for $16 but not for $14. Are they unemployed? Run the three conditions: no job, available, and actively looking. They are not looking, because at this wage they do not want the job. They are out of the labor force, exactly like Nina.

So the labor force here is five people and five people are employed. The unemployment rate is 0 ÷ 5 = 0%. Any unemployment in this model is momentary: a wage above $14 gets bid down, a wage below $14 gets bid up, and the market lands back where nobody who wants work at the going wage is without it.

The hinge of the whole discussion

The unemployment rate has never been zero

Not in a boom, not in a recovery, not in the best year anyone can name. Never. And the model we just built — two ordinary curves, a crossing point, nothing exotic anywhere in it — says it should be.

Take the failure seriously

The honest response is not to abandon supply and demand. It is to ask what the model left out. Something real is keeping this market from doing the one thing our diagram says it always does: clear.

There are three answers, and each one is a different thing going wrong. The rest of the discussion is those three, in order:

Frictional
The first answer. We meet it in Part 3.
Structural
The second answer. Also Part 3, and it is the one people misapply.
Cyclical
The third answer, and the hardest. All of Part 4.

Write the three words down now. By the end you should be able to say, for any story you are handed, which one it is and what test you used.

Reveal · Q6 · the first answer to the puzzle

Frictional unemployment

Reveal · Q6 · no, and the reason matters more than the answer

Frictional unemployment is the unemployment that comes from people switching jobs and searching for the right match. Workers differ, jobs differ, and neither side knows where the other one is. Finding out takes time, and during that time the searcher is counted as unemployed.

The defining feature — and this is the sentence to write down — is that the right job exists and there is a vacancy waiting to be filled. Nothing is missing from this economy except information and a few weeks.

The test
Is there a vacancy out there that matches this person’s skills? If yes, the problem is search, and search is frictional.
Duration
Short. Weeks, not years. It ends when the match is found, and it ends on its own.
What it tells you about the economy
Nothing bad. A perfectly healthy labor market has frictional unemployment in it every single day.

The refused offer is the giveaway in the other direction: a market where every offer had to be accepted on the spot would be matching people to jobs worse, not better. Taking six weeks to find work you can actually do is the mechanism succeeding, slowly.

The second answer to the puzzle

Structural unemployment

A change in the structure of the economy leaves too few jobs for a particular set of skills. It is sometimes called rationing unemployment, and the name is exact: the jobs are being rationed because they are not there to be had.

Cause one · the skills stopped being wanted
technological change

A technology permanently reduces the demand for a kind of worker. The vacancies do not exist anywhere at any wage — not this month, not next year. Searching harder does not help, because there is nothing to find.

Cause two · the wage cannot fall to clear
a wage held above equilibrium

By a union contract, by a minimum wage, or by efficiency wages — firms paying above the market wage on purpose, to keep workers productive and to hold on to them. Whatever the source, the wage will not fall, so the gap it opens does not close.

The test
Is there a vacancy that matches? No. That single answer is what separates structural from frictional.
Duration
Long. Months and years. It does not end on its own, because waiting does not create the job.
The usual remedy
Retraining. Move the worker to where the vacancies are, in skills rather than in patience.
Question 7 · predict · then we show the shift

A wage floor at $18

Q7 · predict · write it before the reveal

The equilibrium wage is $14. Now a rule — a union contract, a minimum wage, a policy of paying above the market — sets a floor: the wage may not go below $18.

Write: how many people end up working, how many want to work, and what happens next. Does anything push the wage back to $14?

Reveal · Q7

At $18 firms want 3 workers and 7 people want jobs. Four people want work at the going wage and cannot get it: that is unemployment of 4, and it is drawn as the gold segment between the two curves.

In every earlier market, a gap like this closed itself — the price moved. Here nothing moves. The wage would have to fall to $14 to close it, and the floor forbids exactly that. The gap does not shrink, and the four people are not searching in the wrong places. There are three jobs.

Four out of seven is a made-up magnitude, not a plausible unemployment rate. Read the mechanism off this picture; never the size.

Reveal · the right box for the wrong reason

Sentence 2 is the flaw. “Between jobs” is true of every unemployed person who ever held a job, so it cannot be what makes someone frictionally unemployed — it does not distinguish anything. The reasoning is what fails, and it would fail in the same way even if the conclusion happened to be right.

The test is whether a vacancy exists that matches the skills. Online travel booking did not slow the agency down; it removed the work. If that industry shrank, the travel agent is structurally unemployed, and the difference is not academic: frictional unemployment ends when the search ends, so the advice is keep looking. Structural unemployment does not end by looking, so the advice is retrain. Tell a structurally unemployed person to search harder and you have told them to keep doing the thing that will not work.

Sentence 3 is the same point from the other side, and it is why the vacancy test says “matches.” An economy can be advertising a great many jobs and still leave someone stranded, if none of those jobs is one they can do.

Putting the first two answers together

The natural rate of unemployment

natural rate = frictional + structural

Both of these are with us in good times and bad. People keep switching jobs; industries keep changing shape; wage floors stay where they were put. So economists bundle them and treat the sum as a roughly stable feature of the economy — a property of how this labor market is built, rather than something that rises and falls with the business cycle.

What it is
the unemployment the economy carries anyway

Not a target, not a failure, not zero. It is what is left over once you accept that matching takes time and that some jobs are gone for good.

How the actual unemployment rate behaves
it fluctuates around the natural rate

Above it in bad times, below it in very tight ones. In the long run the economy is assumed to return to it. The gap between the actual rate and the natural rate is the thing Part 4 is about.

The one thing not in the sum

Cyclical unemployment is deliberately excluded. That is the whole point of the definition: the natural rate is what remains when you strip the business cycle out, which is why it can serve as the line the actual unemployment rate moves around.

Question 9 · quick check · commit before the reveal

Zero was never the goal

Too much unemployment is costly
idle resources, and real harm

Workers who could be producing are producing nothing — output the economy simply never gets. And the cost lands hardest on the unemployed themselves: worse mental and physical health, even after controlling for income, and worse the longer the spell runs.

Too little unemployment is also costly
labor shortages are not free

With almost nobody searching, firms cannot fill vacancies. They burn real resources competing for the few workers available — recruiting, bidding, poaching, waiting with jobs unfilled. That effort produces nothing either.

Students commit on paper (or a show of fingers). Then reveal.

Reveal · Q9

Sentence 2. The cost of unemployment is not a straight line that bottoms out at zero. Push the unemployment rate far enough down and the economy starts spending real effort on finding people rather than on making things — which is why “drive unemployment to zero” is not a policy any economist proposes.

Sentence 1 also quietly forgets what we established in Part 3: some of the unemployment out there is frictional, and a market with zero frictional unemployment would be one where nobody was ever allowed to look for a better match. Sentence 3 forgets the output nobody ever gets to consume.

The most important sentence in Part 4

The shock is not what makes unemployment

The demand shock alone does not create unemployment.
The shock plus a wage that will not fall creates unemployment.

Case one had the whole recession in it — the same fall in demand, the same lost jobs — and produced an unemployment rate of zero. The second ingredient is what turned a shock into people out of work. So cyclical unemployment is the unemployment caused by a downturn in a labor market whose wages do not adjust downward.

Implicit or social contracts
An unwritten understanding that pay does not get cut. Firms break it at the cost of morale, effort and reputation — so mostly they do not break it.
Explicit contracts
Union agreements and written terms fix the wage for a stated period. It legally cannot fall this year, whatever demand does.
Relative-wage concerns
Workers judge their pay against what comparable workers get. Accepting a cut while others hold means losing ground, so it is resisted even when the total pie has shrunk.
How it ends

Cyclical unemployment is not permanent. It resolves in one of two ways: demand recovers, and the labor demand curve slides back to where it was; or the wage eventually gives, as workers accept lower wages once the downturn has lasted long enough. The stickiness is a feature of the short run, which is exactly why cyclical unemployment sits above the natural rate rather than inside it.

Reveal · incomplete rather than false

Sentence 1 — and say plainly that this one is harder than the usual diagnose item. Nothing in it is untrue. Labor demand does fall in a recession, and cyclical unemployment does happen then. The sentence is incomplete, and an incomplete explanation is much harder to catch than a wrong one, because there is no false clause to point at.

What it leaves out is the second ingredient. We ran that shock with a flexible wage two slides ago: demand fell, the curve moved, employment dropped from five to four — and unemployment was zero. Falling labor demand on its own delivers a smaller, poorer labor market, not an unemployed one. The stickiness is what converts a shock into unemployment, and any account that stops at “demand fell” has skipped the mechanism it was supposed to explain.

Sentence 2 is the fact that proves it: employment falls in both cases, which is why the fall in employment cannot be what unemployment means. Sentence 3 is the definition, and it is worth noting it goes both ways — in a boom the actual unemployment rate sits below the natural rate, and that gap is cyclical too.

Synthesis · the whole discussion on one slide

Two claims, and the three answers between them

The throughline

Unemployment is not simply “not working.” It is a measured category with rules — and it never reaches zero because three different things keep the labor market from clearing.

Frictional
A vacancy exists. Matching takes time. Short. Healthy. In the natural rate.
Structural
No matching vacancy — the skills are not wanted, or a wage floor holds the market open. Long. Retraining. In the natural rate.
Cyclical
A downturn plus a wage that will not fall. Both ingredients required. Temporary. Not in the natural rate — it is the gap around it.
