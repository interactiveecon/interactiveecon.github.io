How to run this deck

Write first, reveal second. Every question gets 1–2 minutes of silent writing before you press a button. Nothing reveals itself — the buttons are your cue system, and no answer is on screen when a slide loads.

This is a review unit. Parts 1 and 2 can move briskly; students have met demand and supply before. Parts 3 and 4 are where the discussion earns its time. If you must compress, thin Q2 and Q5 — never the transfer slide or the closing diagnose.

Diagnose slides are silent polls. Numbered sentences, one subtly flawed. Fingers on the chest, not hands in the air — then reveal. The point is always why the reasoning fails, not that the conclusion is wrong.

The throughline · say it out loud at each divider

A change in price moves you along a curve. A change in anything else moves the curve — and then the market finds a new meeting point.

Protect these two payoffs

Q7 (cook wages, Part 2) is the hinge. The flawed sentence gets the right answer — supply shifts left — for a broken reason: “the taquería will charge more.” A shift is about willingness to sell at each given price; the seller does not pick the price. This is the single most common wrong mental model in the unit.

Slide 28 (transfer) is the only thing that survives the numbers: demand shifts move P and Q the same way, supply shifts move them opposite ways. Give it room.

Reveal · Q2

(a) 4 burritos — from $8 go right to the curve, then down. Or 8 = 10 − 0.5Q → Q = 4. (b) $4 — from 12 go up to the curve, then left. Or P = 10 − 0.5(12) = 4.

One read starts on the vertical axis, the other on the horizontal. Same curve, same rule, opposite direction.

The distinction · this is the whole unit

Quantity demanded is a number. Demand is the relationship.

quantity demanded
8 burritos

One amount, at one price. It is a single dot: point C, the pair ($6, 8). Change the price and you get a different dot — a different quantity demanded.

demand
P = 10 − 0.5·Qᵈ

The whole list of price–quantity pairs at once. The curve is the relationship; the equation is the relationship. Neither one is any single point.

price changes → new quantity demanded → same demand

Last week you did this without the words: the dot moved, the line did not. The words are new. The test is not.

Q3 · predict · write yes or no

The price of a burrito rises from $6 to $8. Does the curve move? Write yes or no, plus one reason, before anyone presses play.

price  →  $6.00
quantity demanded  →  8
Watch the curve, not the dot.
Reveal · Q3

No — the curve does not move. Quantity demanded fell from 8 to 4. Demand did not change at all: the same equation, P = 10 − 0.5Q, was true before and after. Price sits on an axis, so a price change has a height to slide to. That is a movement along the curve — and the grey guides show C is still sitting there on the same line.

Practice · commit to all three before revealing any

Along it, or move it?

Same axes: price per burrito up the side, burritos per week across. For each change write A (movement along) or M (the curve moves), plus your one-word reason.

1 · Delivery fees drop
⇒ the CURVE MOVES right

A complement got cheaper. Delivery is something you buy with a burrito, so a cheaper delivery makes the whole package cheaper. At every price on the list you now want more. Delivery fees have no axis here — nowhere to slide to.

2 · The poke place raises its prices
⇒ the CURVE MOVES right

A substitute got pricier. Poke is what you eat instead. When it costs more, burritos win more lunches — at every burrito price. The poke price is not on either axis, so again the whole curve moves.

3 · Burritos go on sale
→ movement ALONG the curve

This one is the burrito's own price. It sits on the vertical axis, so there is a height to slide to and the curve already has a partner waiting there. Quantity demanded rises; demand is unchanged.

is the thing that changed on an axis?  yes → along  ·  no → the curve moves

Question 7 · silent poll · pick a number on your paper

Cook wages go up. Which sentence is broken?

The taquería has to pay its cooks more. All three sentences below reach a defensible place. One of them gets there by reasoning that will fail you later. Find it.

Students pick a number on paper (or fingers on the chest). Then reveal.

Reveal · Q7 · right answer, broken reasoning

Sentence 3 lands on the correct conclusion — supply does shift left — and gets there the wrong way. A shift is a statement about willingness to sell at each given price. The price is held fixed while you ask the question; the seller does not get to pick it. Saying “they will charge more” smuggles in a price change, which is a movement along the curve, not a shift of it.

Sentence 1 says it properly: at $6, at $8, at every price, fewer burritos. Sentence 2 gives the test — wages have no axis, so there is nowhere to slide to. Watch for this one all quarter. Any time an argument explains a shift by saying what the price will do, the reasoning has collapsed the two ideas back together.

Q7 continued · what the shift actually looks like

Costs up, curve left

at $6, before  →  8 burritos
at $6, after   →  4 burritos
same price · fewer burritos · therefore a different curve
S₂ · after the wage rise
P = 4 + 0.5·Qˢ
The supply rule · mirror of Part 1

Cost of production up → supply shifts left. Cost down → supply shifts right. Three things move that cost: input prices (cook wages, avocados), technology (a second grill line makes each burrito cheaper to produce), and the alternative uses of those inputs — if the same grill and the same hour could make something more valuable, making a burrito costs more. That last one is opportunity cost, back from Unit 1.

Question 8 · predict · write two numbers

Both curves, one market

Q8 · predict · no arithmetic needed

Here are both curves at once. Write the price and the quantity where you think this market settles. Read it off the picture — do not solve anything yet.

demand · the buyers
P = 10 − 0.5·Qᵈ
supply · the seller
P = 2 + 0.5·Qˢ

Two relationships, one good. At most prices they disagree about how many burritos should change hands. There is exactly one price where they do not.

Reveal · Q8 · and the answer to Q1

$6 and 8 burritos

The same answer, second way
set them equal   10 − 0.5Q = 2 + 0.5Q
gather Q         8 = Q
put Q back in    P = 10 − 0.5(8) = 6

The crossing point and the one line of algebra are the same operation. “Where do the curves meet” and “where do the two equations agree” are one question.

Callback · Q1

Nobody decided $6. Buyers brought one relationship, the seller brought another, and $6 is the only number both can live with. That is what “the market sets the price” means — not a decision, an intersection.

price       →  $8.00
offered   →  ?   wanted → ?

Press play and watch the gap. Grey bars mark where it has been.
Reveal · Q9

At $8 the taquería makes 12 and buyers want 4. Eight burritos sit on the counter: a surplus of 8. Nobody has to be told what to do — unsold food is a reason to cut the price. By $7 the gap is already down to 4. As the price falls, quantity supplied slides down the red curve and quantity demanded slides up the blue one. Both moves are movements along unchanged curves. The gap closes at $6.

Reveal · Q10

At $4 buyers want 12 and the taquería makes 4. Eight buyers leave without lunch: a shortage of 8. A line out the door is a reason to raise the price, and by $5 the gap is already down to 4. Quantity demanded slides down the blue curve, quantity supplied slides up the red one — again, movements along curves that never move. The gap closes at $6.

The punchline · say this one slowly

Nobody chose $6

$8 was not wrong because someone disapproved of it. It was unstable: it left eight burritos on the counter, and leftover burritos push the price down. $4 was unstable the other way: it left eight buyers in line, and a line pushes the price up.

Equilibrium

The one price that leaves no leftover pressure in either direction. Not the fairest price, not the price anyone wanted, not a decision at all — just the only number that does not push itself somewhere else.

surplus → price falls → gap shrinks  ·  shortage → price rises → gap shrinks

And notice what did not happen in Q9 or Q10: neither curve moved. Nothing changed except the price, and price is on an axis. The whole adjustment was two dots sliding toward each other.

Quick check · commit before the reveal

A surplus. Now which way does everything go?

The price is above equilibrium and burritos are going unsold. Pick the sentence that gets both directions right.

Students commit on paper (or a show of fingers). Then reveal.

Why 2 and 3 fail

3 has the price right and the quantity backwards — price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions along a demand curve; that is the whole shape of it. 2 treats the price as a tool for getting rid of stock, which is exactly the Q7 mistake: raising the price would make the surplus worse.

Transfer · the part that outlives the burritos

Same direction, or opposite?

Q11 and Q12 both ended at 10 burritos. They did not end at the same price, and the difference is not an accident of these numbers — it follows from which curve moved.

Q11 · demand moved
P: $6 → $7  ↑
Q: 8 → 10  ↑

Buyers slid up the seller's unchanged supply curve. To get more burritos out of the same seller you have to offer more money — so price and quantity move together.

Q12 · supply moved
P: $6 → $5  ↓
Q: 8 → 10  ↑

The seller slid down the buyers' unchanged demand curve. To get buyers to take more burritos you have to charge less — so price and quantity move opposite ways.

demand shifts → P and Q same direction   ·   supply shifts → P and Q opposite directions

Run this backwards and it becomes a detective tool. Prices and quantities both up? Something moved demand. Quantity up but price down? Something moved supply. You can often tell which side of the market got hit without being told.

Closing diagnose · silent poll · pick a number

After the wage cut, which sentence is broken?

Cook wages fell. The price went from $6 to $5, and the taquería sold 10 burritos instead of 8. Three students describe what happened. All three describe it accurately. One of them explains it wrongly.

Students pick a number on paper (or fingers on the chest). Then reveal.

Reveal · true observation, wrong name

Sentence 1 is the flaw, and notice how little is wrong with it. People really did buy more burritos — 10 instead of 8. That part is simply true. What fails is the name: “demand went up” claims the whole relationship changed, and it did not. Nothing happened to incomes, to the poke place, to delivery fees. Quantity demanded rose; demand did not.

This is the Part 1 distinction coming back under pressure. It was easy when we asked it directly on slide 7. It is hard here, because the observation that tempts you — “more burritos were bought” — is genuinely correct. Ask the test instead: did anything on an axis change? The price did, and only the price. So it is a movement along.

The throughline

A change in price moves you along a curve. A change in anything else moves the curve — and then the market finds a new meeting point.

price↑ → Qᵈ↓ → same D
price↑ → Qˢ↑ → same S
income↑ → D moves → P↑, Q↑
wages↓ → S moves → P↓, Q↑

You have now used it four ways. Reading a curve from either axis (Q2, Q5). Telling a movement from a shift (Q3, Q4, Q6, Q7). Finding the one price with no leftover pressure (Q8–Q10). And moving a curve to move that price (Q11, Q12).

The only test you ever needed: is the thing that changed sitting on an axis? That question got you through last week’s graphs and a taquería today, and it will get you through every diagram this quarter.
