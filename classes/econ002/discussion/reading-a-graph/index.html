How to run this deck

Write first, reveal second. Every question gets 1–2 minutes of silent writing before you press a button. Nothing on this deck reveals itself — the buttons are your cue system, and no answer is on screen when a slide loads.

Do not fill the silence. The pause is the lesson. If nobody writes for thirty seconds, wait anyway.

Protect this payoff

Slide 8 (Q3, "you have $28") is the hinge of the whole hour. Students arrive believing a graph is read left-to-right because x is the input. Warm-up · no question yet

Gas is $4.00 a gallon

Before anything else, name the two axes out loud.

across  →  gallons pumped (0 to 12)
up      →  dollars paid (0 to 48)
the line  →  the pump's rule: $4 per gallon

The line is not a fact about you. It is the rule the pump is running. Every dot sitting on it is one possible fill-up.

Question 1 · write a full sentence

What does this dot tell you?

Question 1 (write)

Write one complete sentence saying what the dot P tells you. Not a number — a sentence, with both axis names in it.

Reveal · Q1

"If you pump 5 gallons, you pay $20." One dot carries two facts at once — never one. It is a pair: (5 gallons, $20). Drop straight down and the pair's first number is on the horizontal axis; slide straight left and its second number is on the vertical axis. A dot with only one number attached to it is not a point on a graph.

Commit before you look

Predict

You pull into the station and pump 8 gallons. Same pump, same rule: $4.00 a gallon.

Question 2 (predict)

Write down the number you will pay — before you look at the graph. Then, in a few words, write which axis you started from to get it.

The number is easy. The second half is the part that matters — we are about to reverse it.

Question 2 · read it off

Start at 8 gallons. Go up, then left.

gallons: 8.0
dollars: ?
Press play. Up to the line first, then left.

You knew the horizontal number and the graph handed you the vertical one. Most students expect this direction. Hold onto how it felt.

Commit before you look

Predict — and notice this one feels different

Now you walk in with exactly $28 in your pocket. Same pump. Same line. Nothing about the rule has changed.

Question 3 (predict)

How many gallons do you get? Write the number — and then write which axis you started from this time.

If your hand wanted to start on the horizontal axis again, that is the habit we are here to break. Write it down anyway.

Question 3 · the same graph, read backwards

Start at $28. Go right, then down.

dollars: $28.00
gallons: ?
Press play. Right to the line first, then down.
Say this out loud

Nothing on the graph changed between Q2 and Q3. The line is identical. Only the axis you entered from changed.

The punchline of Part 1

The graph has no input side

In your math classes, y = f(x) had a direction built into it: x went in, y came out. You read left to right because that is how the machine ran.

A graph is not that machine. A graph is a list of pairs that happen to lie on a line.

know gallons → line → get dollars   |   know dollars → line → get gallons
The rule

You choose which axis you know, and the graph hands you the partner. Neither axis is the "input." This is not a special trick for economics — it was always true, and economics is just the first class that makes you use it in both directions.

Next quarter you will see graphs with price up the side and be asked to read price → quantity. That is exactly Q3. If Q3 made sense, that will too.

A new rule · and a warning about the axes

Your pizza habit

Read the axes first — they are not what you expect

Economics puts price up the vertical axis and quantity across the horizontal axis. It has done this for a hundred and fifty years and it is not going to stop. Price is the thing you are usually given — so you will constantly be reading from the vertical axis across, exactly like Q3.

$4 → 2 slices  ·  $3 → 4 slices
$2 → 6 slices  ·  $1 → 8 slices
four pairs, one line, one rule

This is why Part 1 mattered. Nobody hands you a quantity and asks for a price.

Commit before you look

Predict

The pizza place drops the price from $3 to $2 a slice. You start buying more.

Question 5 (predict)

Where does that show up on the graph? Write one of these two, and say why:
(a) the dot moves to a different spot on the same line, or
(b) the whole line moves to a new position.

Commit to a letter. Do not hedge — you will get to defend it in a moment.

Question 5 · watch the dot, watch the line

Price $3 → $2

price: $3.00
slices: 4.0
Press play and watch the line, not the dot.
Reveal · Q5

(a) — the dot moves along the same line. Price has its own axis. So a change in price is just a different height on the axis you already have, and its partner is waiting on the line. Nothing about your buying rule changed: at $3 you still want 4, and you can go back and check. The line never moved.

Question 6 · write yes or no, then why

You get a raise

Your hours at work go up and you have more money each week. Price has not changed — pizza is still $3 a slice. But now you buy 8 slices instead of 4.

Question 6 (write)

Look back at the four pairs on your line: ($4, 2) ($3, 4) ($2, 6) ($1, 8).
Can any point on that existing line show "$3 and 8 slices"? Write yes or no — and then write the reason in one sentence.

the line already says:   $3 → 4 slices
the new fact says:      $3 → 8 slices

Two different partners for the same $3. Both cannot sit on one line. Write your reason before the next slide.

Question 6 · reveal

The line itself has to move

A = ($3, 4)  on the old rule
B = ($3, 8)  on the new rule
same price · different partner · therefore a different line
Reveal · Q6

No — and here is the test. A line can give each price exactly one partner. The old line already spent $3 on "4 slices." To also say "$3 and 8," you need a different line. Notice what did not happen: your income never appeared on either axis, so there was no height to slide to. When the thing that changed has nowhere to sit on the graph, the graph has to move.

The punchline of Part 2

One question tells you which it is

Run this test every single time

Did the thing that changed have its own axis?

Yes — it is on an axis
you already have a height for it
→ slide along the line
→ the rule is unchanged
e.g. price fell $3 → $2
No — it is nowhere on the graph
there is no height to slide to
→ the whole line moves
→ the rule itself changed
e.g. you got a raise
on an axis? → move ALONG   |   not on an axis? → the LINE MOVES

You do not need to know any economics to run this test. You only need to know what the two axes are labelled — which is why naming the axes is always step one.

Practice · commit to all three before revealing any

Along, or move it?

Same pizza graph: price per slice up the side, slices per week across. For each change, write A (along) or M (the line moves), plus your one-word reason.

1 · The price of a slice falls
→ movement ALONG the line

Price is on an axis. A lower price is just a lower height on the vertical axis, and the line already has a partner waiting there. The rule never changed.

2 · Your income rises
⇒ the whole LINE MOVES

Income has no axis. There is no height on this graph that means "richer," so there is nowhere to slide to. Every price now gets a new partner — the rule itself changed.

3 · Your roommate starts bringing pizza home
⇒ the whole LINE MOVES

Your roommate has no axis. Free pizza in the kitchen sits nowhere on this graph, so there is nothing to slide to. At every price you now buy fewer slices yourself — a new line, pointing the other way.

Cards 2 and 3 move the line in opposite directions — and the test never had to know that. It asks one thing: was the change on an axis? Which way the line goes is a separate question, and an easier one.

Question 7 · diagnose · silent poll

Find the flawed sentence

All three sentences are about the pizza graph, where the price fell from $3 to $2 and you went from 4 slices to 6. All three end up in the right place. One of them gets there by bad reasoning. Pick a number on your paper.

Students pick a number on paper (or fingers on the chest). Then reveal.

Why sentence 2 fails

The conclusion is right and the reasoning is wrong — which is exactly the dangerous kind. "6 slices" is true; you can read it off the graph. But "your buying rule must have changed" is a claim about the line, and you can test it in five seconds. If the rule had changed, then at $3 you would now want something other than 4 slices. Go look: at $3 the line still says 4. Nothing about it moved.

Price has its own axis, so a price change never needs a new line — it just sends you to a different height on the line you already have. Sentence 2 gets the right number by moving something that never moved. Do that on Thursday's graph and the number comes out wrong.

Question 8 · a graph you have never seen

A graph from somebody else’s class

Question 8 (write, before any button)

Nobody has taught you this graph. Use only the two questions. Two things change, one after the other — for each, write along or the line moves, and name the reason.
Change 1: you study 6 hours instead of 2.
Change 2: you sleep 8 hours the night before instead of 4, and every amount of studying is now worth 10 more points.

the rule you are handed:
40 points, plus 5 more for every hour you study
A = (2 hours, score 50)
Reveal · Q8

Change 1 — along. Hours studied has its own axis. Six hours is just a different height on the axis you already have, and the line had a partner waiting there: 70. B sits on the very same line as A.

Change 2 — the line moves. Sleep is on neither axis. There is no height on this graph that means "rested," so there was nowhere to slide to — and every number of hours now earns a different score, which takes a whole new line.

The tell is B and C: same 6 hours, different score. One value of the horizontal axis cannot have two partners on one line. And you sorted all of that on a graph nobody explained to you.

Gas, phone bills, pizza, exams. Every graph you meet for the rest of the course answers to the same two questions.
