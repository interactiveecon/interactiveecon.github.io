Question 1 · write · commit on paper

From counting the pieces to explaining them

When we measured GDP, C, I, G and NX were just bins. Every dollar of final spending went into exactly one of them, and the four bins added to the total. Nothing in that exercise said how big any bin would be — only that they summed correctly.

Now we want the sizes. Start with the biggest bin, consumption, which is roughly two thirds of United States GDP.

Q1 · write it down before the next slide

A household decides how much to spend this year. Write down what that decision depends on. List everything you can think of, then underline the one you think matters most.

Answer · Question 1

Households spend out of what is left after taxes

Households earn income Y from their work and from what they own, and they hand some of it to the government as taxes T. What is left is disposable income, written Y − T. That is the number a household actually gets to allocate, so that is what consumption is built on.

The consumption function
C = C0 + MPC × (Y − T)
Two parts. C0 is what gets consumed even with no disposable income at all — rent still has to be paid. The second part is the share of disposable income that gets spent.
Marginal propensity to consume
MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY
The amount consumption changes when disposable income rises by one dollar. It is the slope of the consumption function, and it sits between 0 and 1: some of an extra dollar gets spent, the rest does not.
Held aside, not denied

Your list almost certainly included wealth, the interest rate, and expectations about the future, and all three genuinely matter. The model holds them fixed so that one relationship — consumption against disposable income — can be traced cleanly. Holding something aside is not the same as claiming it does nothing.

Question 2 · compute · two numbers

An extra hundred dollars arrives

Take an economy where MPC = 0.75. Disposable income rises by 100.

Q2 · write both numbers before pressing

Write down how much more gets consumed, and how much more gets saved. One line of arithmetic each.

Answer · Question 2

ΔC = MPC × Δ(Y − T) = 0.75 × 100 = 75. The other 25 is saved, because a dollar of disposable income has nowhere else to go: it is either spent or it is not.

That gives the companion to the MPC. Saving is whatever income is not consumed, so the marginal propensity to save is the leftover share:

MPS = 1 − MPC = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25

Hold on to the 0.75. Every number in the second half of this discussion comes out of it.

Question 3 · predict · commit before you see the answer

Why would a higher interest rate mean less investment?

Investment is the purchase of new capital: a firm buying ovens, delivery vans or a warehouse, and a household buying a newly built house. It is about fifteen percent of United States GDP, and it is far and away the most volatile of the four pieces.

Investment depends on the real interest rate r, and it depends on it negatively: when r goes up, investment goes down.

Q3 · predict · the reasoning, not the label

Write down why. A firm that already has the cash in the bank is not borrowing anything — so make sure your explanation still works for that firm.

Answer · Question 3

The interest rate is the bar a project has to clear

Investing takes money, and there are only two places it comes from.

Borrow it
Then r is the cost of borrowing. It is on the invoice.
Use your own
Then r is the opportunity cost: what that money would have earned sitting somewhere else instead. Same number, same bite — the first discussion's point, that the cost of a choice is the option given up.
One project, read off the graph

Take a project that returns 6% a year. At r = 5 it clears the bar — 6 beats 5 — so it is one of the projects inside the I = 20 being invested at point A. At r = 7 it does not clear — 6 loses to 7 — so it is gone. The money does better elsewhere, and that is true whether the firm borrowed it or already had it.

Every project returning between 5% and 7% dies the same way. On the graph that is the shaded band, and its width is exactly the drop from I = 20 to I = 4: 16 of investment stops clearing the bar.

High-return projects are rare and low-return projects are common, which is why the schedule slopes down: the higher r goes, the fewer projects survive, and the less gets invested.

Planned investment against the real interest rate
The third piece

Government purchases and taxes are handed to us

Consumption and investment each get a rule. G and T do not. Explaining how a legislature settles on a budget is a different subject, so the model simply takes both as given:

G = Ḡ     T = T̄

Fixed here means determined outside the model, not unchanging. G and T move all the time; we just are not going to explain the moves. When one of them changes, we take the new value as given too and work out what follows.

Transfers are not purchases
Social Security, unemployment insurance and stimulus checks buy the government nothing. Households spend them, so those dollars land in C, not G — which makes a transfer work like a negative tax.
The budget deficit
The gap between what the government spends and what it takes in: G − T. If G > T it borrows and the debt grows; if G < T it runs a surplus and pays debt down.
Question 4 · predict · commit before you see the answer

The furniture that did not sell

A furniture maker went into the year planning to invest 30 — new saws, a second truck. That was the plan, made in advance.

The year happens. The firm produced 200 of furniture and sold 180. The other 20 is sitting in the warehouse.

Q4 · predict · one number and a sentence

Write down what the firm's investment actually turned out to be this year — and one line saying why it is not 30.

Answer · Question 4

Actual investment includes what nobody planned

Answer · Question 4

The 20 units of unsold furniture did not vanish. They were produced, and they are now inventory the firm is holding — which is capital it owns. So its investment is 30 planned plus 20 unplanned = 50, even though nobody in the firm ever chose the extra 20.

And it runs the other way too

Sell 230 out of 200 produced and the extra 30 comes off the shelves: the unplanned change is −30, so actual investment is 30 − 30 = 0. The saws still got bought; the inventory drawdown cancels them out.

actual investment = planned investment + unplanned change in inventory

This is the same accounting rule you met when we measured GDP: unsold output still counts as investment, because it was produced this year and somebody — the firm itself — is holding it. The rule does not ask whether the firm wanted to.

Two totals that are almost the same

What people plan to spend, and what gets spent

Planned aggregate expenditure
PE = C + I + G
What the economy intends to buy this period, using planned investment. This is a statement about decisions, and it can turn out to be wrong.
Actual aggregate expenditure
AE = C + Iᵃ + G
What the economy ends up buying, using actual investment. This is the accounting identity — it is what the GDP statistician records, and it cannot be wrong.
One term, and only one

Line the two up. C is the same in both. G is the same in both. The entire difference between planned and actual expenditure is the difference between planned and actual investment — which is the unplanned change in inventory.

And notice what the rule does not say. Planned investment always happens in full. The furniture maker bought every one of its planned 30 of saws and trucks; nothing got canceled, and nothing was scaled back. Iᵃ is I plus something, never I minus something — the unplanned inventory change is bolted on top of a plan that was carried out to the letter.

That unplanned piece has a name we will use from here on: unplanned inventory investment, written U.I. So Iᵃ = I + U.I., and PE and AE differ by U.I. and by nothing else.

So inventories are not a footnote. They are the one place where a plan that did not work out shows up in the numbers, and for the rest of this discussion they are the thing doing all the work.

Question 5 · compute · two numbers

Suppose firms produce 200

Nothing says 200 is right. It is a guess, and we are going to test it. If firms produce 200, then income is 200, because every dollar produced is a dollar of somebody's income.

Q5 · work it out on paper first

With Y = 200: compute C, then compute PE = C + I + G. Then write down whether PE is bigger or smaller than 200, and by how much.

Consumption
C = 25 + 0.75(200 − 20) = 25 + 0.75 × 180 = 25 + 135 = 160
Answer · Question 5
PE = C + I + G = 160 + 20 + 40 = 220

Firms made 200. Buyers plan to spend 220. PE exceeds Y by 20.

Follow the goods

Twenty units have to come from somewhere

Buyers want 220 and only 200 was made this period. The extra 20 does not appear out of nothing, and buyers do not simply go home empty-handed. It comes off the shelves.

The signal

Inventories fall unexpectedly by 20. Nobody planned that drawdown, so by the rule from Q4 it goes straight into actual investment: Iᵃ is 20 below planned I.

Now think about what that looks like from inside a firm. The warehouse is emptying faster than expected and the stockroom keeps running short. No survey told the firm that national planned expenditure is 220 — the empty shelf is the whole message. So it does the obvious thing and produces more.

Y < PE → U.I.↓ → firms produce more → Y↑

The punchline of Part 2

Nobody is aiming for 280

No firm in this economy knows what 280 is. No firm is trying to hit it. There is no meeting where output gets set. 280 is simply the only level at which nothing is pushing output anywhere else.

The same shape as the burrito market

In the supply and demand discussion, equilibrium was not the price anyone wanted. It was the price at which the shortage or surplus that pushes price around had gone to zero. Nobody chose it; it was what was left when the pressure stopped.

Here the structure is identical, with one substitution. Inventories play the role that price played. A surplus of goods used to bid the price down; now unsold goods pile up in a warehouse and firms cut production. Same logic, different adjusting variable.

shortage / surplus → price moves   |   inventory falls / rises → output moves

And notice what that buys you: you never have to memorize which way output goes. Ask what happens to inventories, and the direction falls out.

Before we shock it

What moves the planned expenditure line

The line is PE = 70 + 0.75Y. Only two things about it can change: where it starts and how steep it is. Press each card once you have predicted its answer.

Government purchases G↑
intercept ↑
G sits in the intercept directly, dollar for dollar. More G at every level of income, so the whole line shifts up.
Taxes T↑
intercept ↓
Higher taxes cut disposable income, which cuts consumption, which cuts planned spending at every level of income. The line shifts down — but by 0.75 of the tax rise, not all of it.
Interest rate r↑
intercept ↓
Higher r means fewer projects clear the bar, so investment falls, so the intercept falls. The line shifts down. This is the one Part 3 uses.
The slope is the MPC

The only thing that changes the steepness is the marginal propensity to consume. A larger MPC means each extra dollar of income pulls more extra spending along behind it, so the line is steeper. Hold on to that — Q9 turns on it.

Q8 · predict · a number, and say what makes you think so

Answer · Question 8

Output falls by 64

Run the same algebra with the new investment number and the new equilibrium drops straight out.

The new equilibrium
PE = 25 + 0.75(Y − 20) + 4 + 40 = 54 + 0.75Y
Y = 54 + 0.75Y  →  0.25Y = 54  →  Y = 216
The size of it
ΔI = −16     ΔY = 216 − 280 = −64

Investment fell by 16 and output fell by 64 — four times the size of the shock. Nothing else was touched. No second shock arrived.

Which raises the only interesting question in Part 3: where did the other 48 come from?

One round at a time

Where the other 48 comes from

Follow the shock through the economy the slow way. Before each button, write down what you think the next line says.

Round 1 · −16

Investment drops by 16, so planned expenditure drops by 16 at the current output. Firms are still making 280 while buyers now plan to spend 264, so inventories pile up by 16 and firms cut output by 16.

Round 2 · −12

Here is the step that does the damage. That cut in output is somebody's lost income — hours not worked, orders not placed. Income falls by 16, so consumption falls by 0.75 × 16 = 12. Planned expenditure falls again, inventories pile up again, and firms cut output by another 12.

Round 3 · −9

The 12 was income too. Consumption falls by 0.75 × 12 = 9, inventories pile up, output falls another 9. The mechanism has not changed once since round 1 — it is the same three steps on a smaller number.

Round 4 · −6.75

Consumption falls by 0.75 × 9 = 6.75, and output falls by 6.75. Each round is three quarters of the one before, because the MPC is 0.75 and that is exactly what an MPC of 0.75 means: three quarters of any income change comes back as a spending change.

−16 → −12 → −9 → −6.75 → …
Adding the rounds up

The rounds never stop, and the total is finite

Cumulative fall in output after each round
rounds so far 0
this round —
running total ΔY 0
Press play to add the rounds one at a time.
The investment multiplier
−16 − 12 − 9 − 6.75 − … = −64
ΔY = 11 − MPC × ΔI
ΔY = 11 − 0.75 × (−16) = 10.25 × (−16) = 4 × (−16) = −64

The fraction is the whole multiplier: 1 over the share of a dollar that does not come back as spending. Here only 0.25 of each dollar leaks out, so the number on the bottom is small and the multiplier is large. This is the same −64 we already got by solving the model twice.

Question 9 · predict · commit before you see the answer

Change the MPC and nothing else

Same fall in investment, −16. Same G, same T, same everything — except households now spend half of each extra dollar instead of three quarters. MPC = 0.5.

Q9 · predict · bigger or smaller, then say why

Is the multiplier bigger or smaller than 4? Write down your answer and, more importantly, the sentence about the rounds that justifies it.

Answer · Question 9
multiplier = 11 − 0.5 = 10.5 = 2     ΔY = 2 × (−16) = −32

Smaller. Output falls by only 32 instead of 64. Watch the rounds to see why: they now go −16, −8, −4, −2 instead of −16, −12, −9, −6.75. Each round is halved instead of merely trimmed by a quarter, so the sequence dies out fast and the total stays small.

In words: a larger MPC means more of each dollar of lost income turns into lost spending, so the rounds decay more slowly and there are more of them worth counting. The multiplier is a statement about how much of an income change comes back around.

Question 10 · silent poll · pick a number on paper

One of these three is wrong

All three sentences are about the fall in investment we just traced. Two of them are sound. One is not. Write down the number of the one that fails, and one line saying where its reasoning gives out.

Right for one round, then it stops reading

Sentence 2 fails. Notice that it is not a careless sentence — it is exactly right about round 1. Planned expenditure did fall by 16, inventories did pile up by 16, and firms did cut output by 16. Everything in it happened.

What it misses is what that cut is. The 16 units of output firms stopped producing were 16 units of somebody's income. Wages not paid, orders not placed, hours not scheduled. Sentence 2 treats a cut in production as an event with no consequences, when in this model production and income are the same number seen from two sides.

Once that lands, round 2 is unavoidable: 16 less income means 0.75 × 16 = 12 less consumption, which is a fresh gap between output and planned spending, which sets off round 3. The first cut is what triggers every round after it. Stopping at 16 is not a small underestimate — it misses three quarters of the answer.

Sentence 3 is the useful check on this. If the MPC were 0, no income change would come back as spending, the rounds would never start, and 16 would be the whole story. That the real answer is 64 is entirely a statement about the MPC.

The gap between those two arrows is the multiplier, and the geometry explains it. Because the PE line is flatter than the 45-degree line, a small drop in the line has to travel a long way left before the two lines meet again. The steeper the PE line — the larger the MPC — the longer that trip.

A third pair of axes — read them carefully

Two answers, plotted

The interest rate against equilibrium output

These are new axes. Output is now on the horizontal axis and the interest rate is on the vertical. Planned expenditure appears nowhere. Do not carry over the reading habits from the 45-degree diagram.

The IS curve

Point A is the answer we solved for at r = 5. Point B is the answer we solved for at r = 7. Nothing else went into either one.

Now imagine doing that for every interest rate in between, and for the ones above and below. Each one gives an equilibrium output; string them together and you get the line through A and B. That line is the IS curve.

Question 11 · write · commit on paper

What is a single point on this line?

Point A sits at (280, 5). Somebody hands you that pair of numbers with no other context.

Q11 · a full sentence, not a phrase

Write down what that point tells you about the economy. Then answer a second question: at point A, is anything in the goods market out of balance?

Answer · Question 11

Point A says: if the interest rate is 5, the goods market settles at an output of 280. That is a complete answer to the whole of Parts 2 and 3, compressed into one dot — the entire 45-degree diagram, solved, at that one interest rate.

So nothing at A is out of balance. Every point on the IS curve already has Y = PE. The unplanned inventory change is zero everywhere along it. The curve is a collection of finished equilibria, not a menu of pressures.

Two things it is not. It is not a demand curve — the vertical axis is an interest rate, not a price, and the horizontal axis is equilibrium output, not a quantity demanded. And a point off the curve is not a shortage or a surplus in the ordinary sense; it is simply an output level that is not the equilibrium for that interest rate, and inventories would be moving it.

The whole discussion in one line

Why the IS curve slopes downward

The slope is not a new fact. It is every mechanism from Parts 1, 2 and 3, in order, with nothing added.

r↓ → I↑ → PE↑ → PE > Y → U.I.↓ → firms produce more → Y↑
Four links, four earlier slides

r↓ → I↑ is Q3: more projects clear the bar. I↑ → PE↑ is the definition of planned expenditure. PE > Y → U.I.↓ → firms produce more is Q5 and Q6 — U.I. is the unplanned inventory investment from Part 1, and it goes negative here because buyers are taking goods off the shelves. And how far output rises is Part 3: four times the change in investment, because the rounds keep going.

Read the chain backwards and it works too: a higher interest rate cuts investment, planned spending falls short of output, goods pile up, firms cut back. Lower r, higher Y. Higher r, lower Y. The curve slopes down.

The throughline

What you can now do

Output settles where what gets produced equals what people plan to spend. Inventories are the signal that pushes it there, and they are why a small shock moves output by more than the shock itself.
The condition
Y = PE. Not because anyone aims for it, but because it is the only level where the unplanned inventory change is zero and no firm has a reason to move.
The mechanism
Inventories. They are what a firm actually observes, and they carry the message that planned spending and production disagree. Ask what happens to them and the direction of output follows.
The size
The multiplier. Cutting production cuts income, which cuts consumption, which cuts production again. With MPC = 0.75 that turns a shock of 16 into a fall of 64.

And the IS curve is all of that, at every interest rate at once, drawn as a single line. Every point on it is a solved goods market. Nothing on it is out of balance — which is exactly why it will be useful the moment we put a second market beside it.
