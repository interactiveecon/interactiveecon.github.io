Inflation:
a rate, not a level

Today we pin down exactly which number the word “inflation” refers to, build that number out of a shopping basket, find where it comes from over the long run, and work out whose money it actually moves.

Part 1 of 4

A rate,
not a level

Nearly everything people get wrong about inflation is a confusion among three different numbers: the price level, the rate at which that level is changing, and the price of one particular thing.

WHAT IT IS → MEASURING → WHERE IT COMES FROM → WHY IT MATTERS
Throughline: inflation is the rate at which the price level changes. Almost every mistake about inflation comes from confusing that rate with the level, or with the price of any one thing.
Setup, then Question 1 · commit on paper

Two numbers, and only one of them is inflation

Inflation is an overall increase in the price level, where the price level is roughly the average price across the whole economy. Two words in that sentence are doing all the work: overall, and increase.

The price level · a level
P = 110
An index number describing how expensive everything is right now, taken together. It answers how high. On its own it says nothing about which direction anything is moving.
Inflation · a rate
π = 10%
The percent change in that level over a year. It answers how fast, and which way. A rate can fall while the level it describes is still climbing.
Q1 · predict · write it down before the next slide

Four goods a student actually buys, priced in two consecutive years. Inflation over that year was 10% — we build that number ourselves in Part 2.

No. The textbook line fell from $125 to $120 — a drop of 4% — in a year when inflation ran at 10%. Nothing about that is a contradiction.

Individual prices move for their own reasons: a new supplier, a bad harvest, a shift in what people want, a used-book market that finally got organized. Inflation is not a claim about any one of those prices. It is a claim about the average of all of them, and an average can rise while some of its parts fall.

three prices ↑   one price ▼   →   the average still ↑ 10%

Answer · Q2

No, prices are not falling. The price level went from 110 to 115.5. It rose. What fell was the rate at which it was rising: 10% became 5%.

Answer · a true premise and a conclusion it does not support

Sentence 2 is the flaw, and notice how little of it is wrong. Inflation really did come down from 10% to 5%. The word doing the damage is so.

5% is a positive rate, and a positive rate means the level is still climbing — from 110 to 115.5 in this economy. Groceries cost more than they did; they simply got more expensive at half the pace. The only thing that would make the level fall is a negative rate, and 5% is not negative.

Sentences 1 and 3 are both true, and they are the tools. Sentence 1 is the correct reading of exactly the same fact. Sentence 3 is Q1: an average can rise while some of its parts fall. Whenever an inflation number moves, ask first whether it is the level or the rate that just changed.

Part 2 of 4

One number out
of millions

An economy has millions of prices, moving in different directions and by different amounts. Getting a single honest number out of that requires deciding what to hold still.

Setup · no question yet

Fix the basket, then price it again

In Q1, four prices went four different ways. Multiply that by every good in the economy and the question becomes real: which of those millions of numbers is “the” price?

The answer is to stop asking about goods one at a time. Choose a fixed list of goods and quantities — a basket — and find what that identical basket costs, year after year. The list never moves. So if its cost changes, prices are the only thing that can have changed it.

same basket → priced in 2024, 2025, 2026 → the change is prices, and nothing else

Answer · Q5
$1,100 + $360 + $480 + $260 = $2,200

The CPI turns that dollar figure into an index by comparing it with the base year:

CPI = 100 × (cost of the basket this year) ÷ (cost of the basket in the base year)
2024: 100 × $2,000 ÷ $2,000 = 100.0
2025: 100 × $2,200 ÷ $2,000 = 110.0

The base year always comes out at exactly 100, because you are dividing a number by itself. The CPI has no units and no meaning on its own — 110 is only informative next to the 100 it is being compared with.

And notice the textbook row: ▼ that price fell, from $500 of spending to $480, and the basket still cost 10% more. That is Q1, in this economy’s own arithmetic.

The same trick you have already seen, run backward

Hold one thing still, and measure the other

Freezing the quantities is not a convenience. It is the entire measurement. If the list of goods were allowed to change alongside the prices, a rise in the basket’s cost could mean prices went up, or that people simply started buying more — and there would be no way to tell which.

Real GDP · from Measuring GDP
hold prices fixed → measure quantities

Price every year’s output at one year’s prices. Then the only thing that can move the total is how much got produced. The ruler stops moving so the object can be measured.

The CPI · today
hold quantities fixed → measure prices

Buy the same list every year. Then the only thing that can move the total is what those goods cost. Same discipline, pointed the other way.

one moving part at a time → the number means something

Every good measure of anything in this course is built this way: decide what you want to see, then freeze everything else so it cannot contaminate the answer.

Question 6 · predict · the cost of freezing the basket

Coffee doubles. Everybody switches to tea.

The price of coffee doubles. Students respond the way people do: they stop buying 250 cups a year and drink tea instead, at roughly the old cost.

Q6 · predict · commit on paper

Does the CPI notice the switch? Write yes or no, and then say what that means for whether the CPI is telling the truth about the cost of living.

Answer · Q6

No, it does not notice. The basket was fixed in 2024 and it still contains 250 cups of coffee. The CPI keeps charging you for coffee at the new high price even though you no longer buy any.

So the CPI reports a bigger increase in the cost of living than students actually suffered — they dodged most of it by switching. Freezing the basket is what made the measure honest about prices, and it is the same freezing that makes it overstate the rise in the cost of living. This is substitution bias, and it is the price of the trick from the last slide.

1 · Substitution bias
When one good gets expensive, people buy less of it. The fixed basket cannot, so the CPI overstates how much worse off they are.
2 · New goods
A good invented after the base year is not in the basket, so the extra choice it gives consumers never shows up in the index.
3 · Unmeasured quality
A phone that costs 10% more and does twice as much is recorded as a 10% price increase, when part of that is simply a better good.
A second route to a price level

The GDP deflator

GDP deflator = 100 × nominal GDP ÷ real GDP

Nominal GDP is this year’s output at this year’s prices; real GDP is this year’s output at base-year prices. They describe the same pile of goods, so whatever separates them is only prices — which makes their ratio a price level. Here is the coffee-and-laptop economy from Measuring GDP, back again.

The coffee-and-laptop economy — 2025 is the base year

Worked through
2025: 100 × $20,000 ÷ $20,000 = 100.0
2026: 100 × $28,000 ÷ $25,000 = 112.0
π: 100 × (112.0 − 100.0) ÷ 100.0 = 12.0%

In the base year nominal and real GDP are the same number by construction, so the deflator is 100 — exactly as the CPI is 100 in its base year. Both indexes are built to be meaningless alone and informative in comparison.

The structural difference

Coverage is the easy half. The real difference is in the basket itself. The CPI’s basket is fixed at base-year quantities and only changes when the quantities are deliberately re-surveyed. The deflator’s basket is whatever the economy actually produced this year, so it changes every single year, on its own.

That is why substitution bias is a CPI problem and not a deflator problem — and why the deflator cannot answer the question the CPI is built for, which is what happened to the cost of buying a particular standard of living.

One adjustment, and one more index

Core inflation

Food and energy prices swing sharply from month to month — a bad harvest, a cold winter, a disruption in oil — and they are a large share of the basket. So a single shock can move the headline number without telling you anything about where prices are heading.

Headline inflation
every good in the basket

What households actually pay, including the volatile parts. This is the number that describes your year.

Core inflation
food and energy excluded

The identical calculation with the two jumpiest categories removed, so the underlying trend is visible through the noise.

One more index worth knowing by name: the PCE deflator covers consumer spending only, like the CPI, but with a basket that changes every year, like the deflator — and it is the measure the Federal Reserve prefers.

Where it
comes from

Any single price can move for its own reasons. But a rise in every price at once is a different kind of event, and over the long run it has one source — and the source is not the goods.

Setup, then Question 8 · predict

Five hundred goods, two thousand dollars

An economy produces 500 goods a year. There are $2,000 of money in it, and every dollar gets spent exactly once. All $2,000 is therefore spent on all 500 goods, which fixes the average price with no room for argument.

P = M ÷ Y = $2,000 ÷ 500 = $4
Q8 · predict · commit on paper

The money supply doubles to $4,000. The economy still produces 500 goods. Write down the new average price — and then write down whether anyone in this economy is better off.

Answer · Question 8 · two cases

The same 500 goods, either way

A dollar and a good are drawn the same length, so the money bar is as many times longer than the goods bar as the price is in dollars. The numbers count the goods-bar lengths that fit inside the money bar — four of them at $4 a good, eight at $8.

Answer · Q8

P = $4,000 ÷ 500 = $8. Twice the money is chasing exactly the same 500 goods, so every price doubles.

And nobody is better off. Every person is holding twice as many dollars and every good costs twice as much. There are still 500 goods to divide among the same people. Doubling the money changed the numbers on the price tags and changed nothing that anyone can eat, wear, or use.

The other case

Now suppose output had doubled too, to 1,000 goods. Then P = $4,000 ÷ 1,000 = $4, exactly where it started. The money supply doubled and the price level did not move an inch, because there were twice as many goods for those dollars to be spent on.

So it was never the money on its own. It is money relative to output.

The two cases, written as one rule

Inflation is money growth minus output growth

π = %ΔM − %ΔY

Both of your cases are in that one line. Case B: money grew 100%, output grew 0%, so π = 100% and $4 became $8. Case C: money grew 100%, output grew 100%, so π = 0% and $4 stayed $4. Everything in between follows the same subtraction.

M grows faster than Y
π > 0
Inflation. Money is being added faster than goods are, so each dollar ends up buying less. Money 8%, output 3% → about 5% inflation.
M grows at the same rate as Y
π = 0
Stable prices. The extra dollars have exactly the extra goods to be spent on. Money 3%, output 3% → the price level holds still.
M grows more slowly than Y
▼ π < 0
Deflation. Goods are arriving faster than dollars, so each dollar buys more. Money 1%, output 3% → about −2% inflation.

This is a statement about the long run. In any given month a hurricane, a strike or an oil shock can move the price level on its own. Over years, the arithmetic above is what is left.

Answer · Question 10

What the extra dollars can actually buy

Answer · Q10

It depends on inflation, and the number you needed was that one. Suppose inflation was 4%. Then the bundle of goods that cost $1,000 a year ago now costs $1,040.

You have $1,060 and you need $1,040 simply to buy what you could have bought last year. Your purchasing power went up by about 2%, not 6%. Four of your six points were spent standing still.

nominal rate i → 6%   what the bank pays, in dollars
inflation π    → 4%   what those dollars lost
real rate r    → 2%   the change in what you can buy
r = i − π   →   6% − 4% = 2%
Costs that survive even when everyone sees it coming

Three costs of expected inflation

Start with the easier case: inflation that everybody correctly anticipates. Contracts and interest rates can be written with it already in mind — and it still costs the economy something real.

1 · Shoe leather costs
Money loses value while you hold it, so people hold less of it — more trips to the bank, more moving of balances around, more attention paid to cash management. Real time and effort spent on nothing but staying ahead of the price level.
2 · Menu costs
Every posted price has to be changed: menus reprinted, shelves relabeled, catalogs and systems updated. Small for any one change, and the changes never stop.
3 · Tax distortions
Taxes are levied on nominal gains. An asset that rises 6% while prices rise 4% has gained 2% in real terms, but the tax bill is computed on the full 6% — so part of what is taxed as income was never income at all.

None of these requires anyone to be surprised. They are the running cost of a price level that will not sit still.

Answer · Question 11

The surprise is the whole story

Q11 — the same loan, expected and realized
 ExpectedActual
Nominal rate i6%6%
Inflation π2%8%

Answer · Q11

Expected: 6% − 2% = +4%. Actual: 6% − 8% = ▼ −2%.

The borrower comes out ahead. The $10,600 repayment is fixed in dollars, and those dollars turned out to buy considerably less than either party planned for. The borrower hands back something worth less than was borrowed; the lender ends the year able to buy about 2% less than at the start, having lent money out for a year. Six percentage points of real return moved from one side of the contract to the other, and no one renegotiated anything.

The same mechanism hits anyone whose income is fixed in dollars. A pension that pays $2,000 a month pays $2,000 a month while the cost of living climbs past it — the payment never changed, and it buys less every year.

Expected inflation
already inside the nominal rate

If both sides had expected 8%, the loan would have been written at a higher nominal rate to begin with. Anticipated inflation gets priced in, which is why its costs are the shoe leather, menus and taxes from the last slide — not a transfer.

Unexpected inflation
this is what moves wealth

Nobody can price in a surprise. Every contract already signed in fixed dollars — loans, pensions, long wage agreements — is quietly rewritten in favor of whoever owes the dollars. It is the surprise, not the inflation, that redistributes.

Question 12 · predict, then check yourself

So why is deflation a problem?

Deflation means the price level is genuinely falling ▼ — the third stretch of the line in Part 1. Everything you buy gets cheaper. That sounds like unambiguous good news for anyone doing the buying.

Q12 · predict · commit on paper

Write down why economists treat deflation as a problem anyway. List as many separate reasons as you can before pressing the button — there are five worth having.

Answer · Q12
  1. Purchases get postponed. If the same thing is cheaper next month, waiting is rewarded. Spending gets delayed, which weakens demand further, which pushes prices down again.
  2. The real burden of debt grows. A debt fixed in dollars has to be repaid with dollars that now buy more than the ones borrowed — Q11 run in reverse, with the borrower on the losing side.
  3. Wages are very hard to cut. Workers resist nominal pay cuts far more than they resist inflation eating the same amount. So when prices fall, firms cut jobs instead of pay.
  4. Profits shrink. Revenue falls with prices, while many costs — wages, leases, supply contracts — were committed earlier at higher levels and cannot follow prices down.
  5. Monetary policy becomes less effective. The usual response is to cut interest rates, and there is a limit to how far a nominal rate can be cut.
prices ▼ → wait to buy → spending ↓ → prices ▼ again

Notice what these five have in common. Not one of them is about goods being cheap. Every one is about a contract or a plan written in dollars while the value of a dollar was moving underneath it.

One distinction, four times

The same confusion, in four disguises

Part 1 · the rate is not the level
Inflation falling from 10% to 5% is a fall in the rate. The level went from 110 to 115.5 — it rose the whole time. And a 10% average is perfectly compatible with a textbook getting cheaper.
Part 2 · the index is not the rate
The CPI is a level: 100, then 110, then 115.5. Inflation is what you get by taking percent changes of it. Two different objects, one built out of the other, constantly swapped for each other in conversation.
Part 3 · nominal is not real
Doubling the money supply doubles the price level and every nominal number attached to it. The 500 goods do not move. Wealth is goods; money is the unit we count them in.
Part 4 · expected is not actual
Six percent in the bank is a nominal rate; 2% is what you gained. And the nominal rate already carries expected inflation inside it — so it is the gap between expected and actual that takes money from one person and gives it to another.
