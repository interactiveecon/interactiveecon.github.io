Question 1 · write before you turn the page · no reveal on this slide

Three words are doing all the work

Gross Domestic Product is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a given period by factors of production located within a country.

Final
Produced for use, not for resale by another firm. A good bought by a firm to make something else is an intermediate good.
Within a given period
A year, a quarter. GDP is a flow over an interval, never a stock at a moment.
Located within a country
Where the production physically happens. Not who owns the factory, not who holds the passport.
Q1 · commit on paper

Four things happen this year. Write down which ones belong in this year’s GDP, and beside each a few words for why.

  2. Ben buys 100 shares of stock for $4,000.
  3. Priya buys a new bicycle for $600.
  4. The used-car dealer takes a $500 commission for handling Ana’s sale.

Ask one question: was something produced this year?

Q1 — which transactions enter this year’s GDP
TransactionIn GDP?Why
Used Civic, $9,000✗ NoCounted in 2019, when it was built. Counting it now is double counting.

Reveal · the trap, and why it is a trap

Transactions 1 and 4 are the same sale, and they land on opposite sides. The car itself was produced in 2019 and is already inside 2019’s GDP; moving it from Ana to Ben adds no production to this year. But the dealer’s $500 buys something that did not exist last week — the work of finding a buyer, checking the title, writing the contract. That service was produced this year, so it counts.

Same logic on the stock. The $4,000 is not payment for output; it is a claim changing hands. But if Ben paid a broker $30, that $30 is in GDP. The rule is never “did money move.” It is always: was something produced, this year, that was not there before?

Question 2 · quick check · commit before the reveal

A sack of flour

The mill sells a 50 lb sack of flour to a bakery. It sells an identical sack to you, for your kitchen. Pick the sentence that gets the classification right.

Students commit on paper (or a show of fingers). Then reveal.

Reveal · Q2

Sentence 2. “Intermediate” is not a property of flour. It is a description of what happens next. The bakery’s sack is going to reappear inside the price of a loaf, so counting it now would count that flour twice. Your sack reappears in nothing — it ends its life in your kitchen — so this is the only chance to count it.

The same physical object, the same price, two different answers. Whenever you are asked “is this final?” the honest reply is a question back: final for whom, and what happens to it next?

Question 3 · poll · then we move on

Where it happens, or whose it is

GDP · a place
produced inside the borders
Everything made on U.S. soil, whoever owns the plant and whoever works in it.
GNP · a nationality
produced by the nation’s factors
Everything made by the country’s own factors of production, wherever on earth they happen to be standing.
GNP = GDP + factor payments received from abroad − factor payments made abroad

Students commit on paper (or a show of fingers). Then reveal.

Reveal · Q3

Sentence 1. The cars are built in Ohio, so that production happened here — U.S. GDP. The profits flow to Japanese owners, so they are a factor payment made abroad and drop out of U.S. GNP. The teacher’s lessons are produced in Korea, so they are Korean GDP; the wages come home to an American factor, so they are U.S. GNP.

GDP asks where. GNP asks whose. For the United States the gap between them is small — well under one percent of the total — which is why every model in this course, and everything after today, uses GDP.

Reveal · Q4

No. 420 is not a quantity of anything. Cups and laptops are measured in different units, and adding numbers whose units differ produces a number with no meaning — the same mistake as saying you own 3 shoes plus 2 hours.

So we need one common unit. Economies already have one lying around: value. Multiply each quantity by its price and every good is expressed in dollars, and dollars can be added.

400 cups + 20 laptops → ?    400×$5 + 20×$900 → $20,000

Prices are a translation device, not the thing we care about. They are here to make two unlike goods addable. Nobody wants to know the economy’s dollars; we want to know its cups and laptops.

Reveal · Q5 · the failure that motivates everything else

Measured GDP rose 12%. Production rose 0%. Not one additional cup was poured; not one additional laptop was assembled. Nobody worked an extra hour.

And notice why the measure did this. It is not a bug in the arithmetic — the arithmetic is correct. It is that we let the translation device change between the two years. We asked “how much was produced?” and answered with a number that also moves when nothing but the prices move.

Every single thing in the rest of this part exists to fix this one problem.

The fix · two versions of the same sum

Hold the prices still

If the trouble is that prices moved, the repair is obvious: stop letting them move. Pick one year’s prices, call it the base year, and use those same prices every time.

Nominal GDP
this year’s quantities
× this year’s prices
Every year valued at that year’s own prices. Moves when quantities move — and also when prices move.
Real GDP
this year’s quantities
× base-year prices
Every year valued at one fixed set of prices. The only thing left that can move it is the quantity column.
base year → the year whose prices we borrow → here, 2025

One consequence worth writing down: in the base year, nominal and real GDP are identical. Not approximately — exactly. They are the same quantities multiplied by the same prices. Real GDP only starts to differ from nominal once you walk away from the base year.

Reveal · Q6

Nominal 2026: 500×$6 + 25×$1,000 = $3,000 + $25,000 = $28,000.   Real 2026: 500×$5 + 25×$900 = $2,500 + $22,500 = $25,000.

The quantity column is identical in both calculations — 500 and 25 either way. The only difference is which price list you reached for. And look at 2025: $20,000 in both columns, because in the base year there is nothing to hold still.

40% or 25%

Both are real arithmetic on real data. Which one deserves to be called “economic growth,” and why?

Reveal · the answer and the reason

Real growth, 25%. Not because 40% is a miscalculation — it is perfectly correct as a statement about dollars — but because 40% answers a question nobody asked. It mixes together “we made more things” and “things got more expensive,” and reports one number for both.

Real GDP prices both years the same way, so the only surviving explanation for a change is that the quantities changed. That is the question we were asking from the start: how much did this economy produce?

Question 7 · diagnose · silent poll, then reveal

All three sentences use the same true numbers

Nominal GDP went from $20,000 to $28,000. Real GDP went from $20,000 to $25,000. One of these three sentences reasons badly. Find it, and be ready to say what is wrong with the reasoning rather than the number.

Students pick a number on paper (or fingers on the chest). Then reveal.

Reveal · a true premise with a false conclusion bolted on

Sentence 1, and notice how little of it is wrong. “Nominal GDP grew 40%” is simply true. We computed it: $20,000 to $28,000 is exactly 40%. There is no error in the measurement.

The error is the word so. Nominal GDP is a sum of prices times quantities, and between 2025 and 2026 both factors moved. A 40% rise in that sum is consistent with any number of production stories — including the Q5 story, where production did not rise at all. Real GDP, which holds prices at 2025, says the production part was 25%. The remaining gap is price.

This is the habit to build: separate what a number measured from what you concluded about it. Sentence 1 gets the measurement right and the inference wrong, which is far more dangerous than getting the arithmetic wrong — nobody checks a conclusion that starts from a correct figure.

Key result · copy this into your notes

Why prices, and why fixed

The two-sentence version

We use prices because goods have different values and cannot otherwise be added together. We fix those prices because prices change over time, and we want a measure that captures changes in production only.

Prices arrive because
cups + laptops has no answer

With one good, counting is enough — 400 cups. With two goods you need a common unit, and value is the one every good already has.

Prices get frozen because
otherwise the ruler moves

A measuring device that changes length between measurements cannot tell you whether the thing you measured changed. Fixing prices holds the ruler still.

From here on, whenever this course says “GDP” or “output” or “Y” with no adjective in front, it means real GDP.

Reveal · Q8

Adding every sale gives $6.90 — for a loaf of bread that a shopper can buy for $3.50. The economy did not produce $6.90 of anything.

The farmer’s 40 cents of wheat gets counted four separate times: once when the farmer sells it, again inside the miller’s $0.90, again inside the baker’s $2.10, again inside the grocer’s $3.50. Every intermediate stage drags the earlier stages along with it.

Route 1
sum the value added

$0.40 + $0.50 + $1.20 + $1.40 = $3.50. Each stage contributes only what it added on top of what it bought.

Route 2
take the final sale

Just $3.50. Every earlier stage is already folded into the price a shopper pays.

Punchline · the sentence students usually get wrong

Intermediate goods are not unimportant

Ask a room why intermediate goods are excluded from GDP and somebody will say “because they don’t really count” or “because they aren’t finished.” Both are wrong, and the second is worse, because it sounds right.

Say it this way instead

Intermediate goods are excluded not because their value does not matter, but because their value is already inside the final price.

$3.50 loaf  =  $0.40 wheat + $0.50 milling + $1.20 baking + $1.40 retailing

The wheat is in there. The milling is in there. The grocer’s shelf space and cashier are in there. Counting them again does not honor them — it just says the same production twice.

Value added: the difference between what a good is worth leaving a stage and what it cost entering that stage. Add those differences up and nothing is missed and nothing is doubled.

Three views of
one number

Every good that is produced and sold has a matching payment. And every payment is income to somebody. So if you can total up the spending, you have — with the inventory caveat — totalled up the production.

The expenditure approach · first two components

C and I

The expenditure approach computes GDP by measuring the total amount spent on all final goods and services in a period. Four buckets, and every dollar of spending goes in exactly one.

C · personal consumption expenditure
C
Household spending on consumer goods and services.
  • Durable goods — last a relatively long time: cars, refrigerators, furniture.
  • Nondurable goods — used up fairly quickly: food, fuel, clothing.
  • Services — non-physical things households buy: health care, haircuts, streaming.
I · gross private domestic investment
I
Spending by firms and households on new capital — plant, equipment, housing, and inventory.
  • Nonresidential — machines, tools, plants: what firms buy to produce with.
  • Residential — new houses and apartment buildings.
  • Change in business inventories — your 400 bicycles. Inventory sits here because, like a machine, unsold stock earns the firm revenue later. The accounts treat the firm as buying its own unsold output, which is precisely how production that nobody spent money on still lands inside an expenditure total.

Gross vs. net. Gross investment is all newly produced capital; depreciation is the amount an asset’s value falls over the period; net investment is gross minus depreciation. GDP uses the gross figure — the D in GDP.

Slide 27 said the unsold bikes would come back. That bullet is where they live.

The expenditure approach · the other two

G and NX

G · government consumption and gross investment
G
Federal, state and local purchases of goods and services, plus the government’s own investment.

G is purchases, not the whole budget. A payment that buys no good or service — a pension check, an unemployment benefit — is a transfer. Nothing was produced when it was sent, so it is not in G. It shows up in GDP later, if and when the recipient spends it, as part of C.

NX · net exports
NX = EX − IM
Exports minus imports. The component everybody misreads, which is what Q11 and Q12 are for.

Because C, I and G are measured as spending, and the people doing the spending do not check where a good was made. Some of that spending left the country. Meanwhile some things made here were bought by foreigners, so they never appeared in C, I or G at all. NX fixes both leaks at once.

GDP = C + I + G + NX

Four buckets. The next three slides are entirely about the fourth one.

Punchline · the most misused minus sign in macro

Subtracting imports is not a penalty on trade

What the minus sign is doing

Imports are subtracted only to remove foreign production that was swept into C, I and G in the first place. The 180 was never domestic output; it got into the total because consumers, firms and governments do not sort their receipts by country of manufacture.

This economy
trade deficit of 50

EX 130, IM 180, NX = −50. If the minus sign really were a penalty, that ought to be a drag on output.

And yet
it produced 1,000

Route 2 never subtracts anything. It just adds up domestic production directly — and arrives at 1,000.

Expenditure is used to figure out what GDP is. It does not determine what GDP is. Production happened on factory floors and in offices before anybody spent a dollar; the accounting comes afterwards, and it is trying to reconstruct that production, not to reward or punish anyone for it.

produced → sold → paid for → recorded  —  the arrow only points one way
Question 12 · diagnose · silent poll, then reveal

One of these sentences would fail an exam

Students pick a number on paper (or fingers on the chest). Then reveal.

Reveal · the minus sign never travels alone

Sentence 1 is the flaw, and it is seductive because the algebra looks like it is on its side: GDP = C + I + G + EX − IM, so a smaller IM must mean a bigger GDP. It would — if IM appeared only once.

It does not. Every imported good is sitting in two places at the same time: inside C, I or G as spending, and inside IM as the correction. Halve imports and households buy 60 fewer foreign goods — C falls by 60 and IM falls by 60. The two changes cancel exactly, and domestic production is untouched, because nothing about a factory here changed when a shopper stopped buying something made there.

The test to carry away: when a term appears on both sides of a correction, you cannot move it on one side only. Ask what else moves when it moves — and then ask the question that actually matters, which is whether anything was produced differently.

The income approach · conceptual only

One number, seen three ways

The income approach computes GDP from the wages, rents, interest and profits received by all the factors of production. And it has to land in the same place, for a reason you can state in one line:

The whole argument

Every expenditure by one person is income to another. The money a shopper hands over does not evaporate at the cash register — it becomes wages for the cashier, rent for the landlord, interest for the lender, profit for the owner. Total the payments made and you have totalled the payments received.

Production
what was made
Value added at every stage. The thing we actually want, and the only one of the three that is not money.
Expenditure
C + I + G + NX
What was spent on it — with unsold output filed under investment so that nothing produced goes missing.
Income
wages + rents + interest + profits
Who received the spending. Every dollar paid out is a dollar taken in by somebody.
Important result: GDP simultaneously measures production, expenditure, and income

Not three numbers that happen to be close. Three descriptions of one number — which is why economists switch between them mid-sentence and expect you to follow.

Limitations · brief, but do not skip it

What this number cannot tell you

GDP is a good measure of production, and it is used every day as a measure of welfare. Those are not the same thing, and the gaps are systematic rather than random.

Nonmarket work
Housework, child care, elder care, cooking at home. Real production, done in enormous quantity, invisible to GDP because no market transaction records it. Pay someone to do it and GDP rises — while the amount of care produced has not.
The informal economy
Transactions that are unreported, cash-only, or illegal. Income is genuinely generated; none of it is counted. The size of the gap differs wildly across countries, which makes cross-country comparisons shakier than they look.
Distribution
GDP is a total. It has nothing whatsoever to say about who received it. Two economies with identical GDP can be unrecognizably different places to live.
Leisure
An extra week of holiday is an increase in wellbeing and usually a decrease in measured output. GDP scores it as a loss.
Bads counted as goods
If crime fell, society would be better off — but less would be spent on security, and GDP would fall. Cleaning up after a disaster raises GDP.
The honest summary
GDP is not a measure of welfare. It never claimed to be. It answers “how much did we produce?” — well — and that is a different question from “how well are we living?”
Synthesis · the whole hour, in one line

Everything today was machinery

The throughline

GDP is a measure of production. Prices, sales, spending and income are all machinery for getting at production, which we cannot observe directly.

cups + laptops → prices → one number
fix the prices → only production can move it
final sales + Δ inventories → production
C + I + G + NX → production
wages + rents + interest + profits → production

Five different-looking procedures, one target. Prices showed up because cups and laptops will not add. Fixed prices showed up because a moving ruler cannot measure. Inventories showed up because sales are not production. Net exports showed up because spending is not domestic production. Income showed up because every payment has two ends.

Whenever a GDP question confuses you this quarter, ask the one question underneath all five: is this telling me something about what was produced? If a number moved and nothing was made differently, you have found the machinery, not the economy.
