Part 1 of 4

Two curves, one diagram

The IS curve gives the equilibrium level of output in the goods market for a given interest rate. You built it by asking what a change in the interest rate does to planned expenditure, and it slopes down.

The IR curve gives the interest rate for a given price level, level of output, and money supply. You built it out of the money market, and it slopes up in output.

r = cP + dY − eM

Throughline: the aggregate demand curve is not assumed, it is traced out. Every point on it is the goods market and the money market in equilibrium at the same time — which is why fiscal and monetary policy move output the same way but the interest rate opposite ways.
Question 1 · write before you look

Why does the IR curve slope up?

Question 1 · on your own paper

The IR curve says that when output is higher, the interest rate is higher. Write down why — not the equation, the mechanism.

  1. Which market are you standing in?
  2. What does higher output do to that market?
  3. What is fixed, and what is therefore left free to adjust?
Y↑ → money demand↑ → r↑

Higher income means more transactions to cover, so at every interest rate people want to hold more real money balances. Money demand shifts right.

But the supply of real balances is fixed: M is set by the Fed and P is given. Wanting more real balances does not produce more of them. So the only thing free to move is the price of holding money, and it rises until people are content to hold exactly what exists.

This is the money market diagram from the last deck, read a different way. There you fixed output and asked what the interest rate was. Here you let output vary and write down the answer at each level. That collection of answers is the IR curve.

Question 2 · write before you look

What is special about point A?

Question 2 · on your own paper

Point A is one point out of the thousands on that diagram. Write down what is true at A that is not true anywhere else on the page.

Then write down what would be wrong with a point that sat on the IS curve but off the IR curve.

Two markets, both clearing, at once

A is the only point where the goods market and the money market are both in equilibrium at the same time. Every other point on the page fails at least one of them.

On IS but off IR: firms are content. Planned expenditure equals output, so no warehouse is filling and none is emptying. But the money market is not clearing — at that interest rate people want to hold a different quantity of real balances than exists, so the rate is still moving. And once the rate moves, output will have to move too.

On IR but off IS: the reverse. The money market clears at that rate, but planned expenditure does not equal output, so inventories are moving and firms are changing production.

Only where the two curves cross is nothing pushing anything anywhere. That is the whole reason for drawing them together.

The test you have used all term

Output and the interest rate are the axes

If a variable sits on an axis, a change in it moves you along a curve. If it does not, it shifts one. That test settled the question in the supply and demand deck, in the goods market deck, in the deck on government spending and taxes, and in the money deck, and it settles it here.

Output and the interest rate are the two axes of this diagram. So everything else — G, T, P, M — has to shift something. The only question left is which curve it reaches first.

Shifts IS · the goods market
C   I   G   T
Every piece of planned expenditure lives on this side: consumption, investment, government purchases and taxes. Planned expenditure is what the IS curve is made of, so a change in any of them moves IS — and leaves IR exactly where it was.
Shifts IR · the money market
P   M
The price level and the money supply act on real balances, M/P. Real balances are what the IR curve is made of, so a change in either one moves IR — and leaves IS exactly where it was.

One catch worth writing down. Investment moving because the interest rate moved is a movement along IS, not a shift of it — the interest rate is on an axis. Investment shifts IS only when something else moves it at an unchanged interest rate, such as a change in what firms expect to sell.

Nothing in this unit shifts both curves at once. Every shock in the next three parts moves exactly one of them.

That line is the aggregate demand curve

The aggregate demand curve gives the level of output demanded at each price level. It runs through (340, 80), (280, 100) and (220, 120), and it slopes down.

Nobody assumed that slope, and nobody drew it from a definition. It came out of running the IS-IR diagram three times and writing down where it landed. That is the entire derivation.

Which also means the curve could have come out some other way. If the links in that chain worked differently, this line would have a different slope — or no slope at all. The next slide is about which link is doing the work.

The slope — and the reason that is not the reason

Why aggregate demand slopes down

✗ The familiar reason, which fails here
there is nothing to switch to

A single market’s demand curve slopes down partly because buyers switch to something else when the price rises. If pears get more expensive and apples do not, you buy apples instead.

That reason cannot do any work for aggregate demand. AD covers all goods at once. When the price level rises, the apples rise too, and so does everything else on the shelf. There is nothing left to substitute toward, so the substitution channel is simply not available.

✓ The reason it actually slopes down
it runs through real balances

A higher price level shrinks real money balances, which raises the interest rate, which cuts investment, which cuts planned expenditure, which cuts output.

Every link there is a link you built in an earlier deck. Nothing in the argument is about anyone deciding to buy less because their money does not stretch as far.

P↑ → M/P↓ → r↑ → I↓ → PE↓ → U.I.↑ → Y↓

The slope of AD is that whole chain compressed into a single line on a graph. Anything that breaks a link in the chain changes the slope — and if the interest rate had no grip on investment, the chain would break in the middle and AD would come out vertical.

A point on AD is the equilibrium output in the goods market and the equilibrium interest rate in the money market, for that price level. Two markets, cleared, packed into one dot.

Question 7 · silent poll · pick a number on your own paper

One of these three is wrong

All three sentences agree that the aggregate demand curve slopes down. Two of them give a sound reason. One does not. Write down the number of the one that fails, and one line saying where its reasoning gives out.

Right conclusion, wrong mechanism

Sentence 1 fails — and notice that its conclusion is correct. AD really does slope down. What is wrong is the reason, and the reason is the part you will be asked to use for the rest of the course.

A single market’s demand curve slopes down in large part because of substitution: if pears get more expensive and apples do not, you buy apples. Aggregate demand is every good at once, so when the price level rises the apples rise too. There is nothing left to substitute toward, and that channel is not available here at all.

The channel that is available runs through money: a higher price level shrinks real balances, raises the interest rate, and cuts investment. That is what pulls output down.

Sentence 3 is the check on this, and it is worth keeping. Cut the link from r to I and the entire slope disappears — which could not be true if sentence 1 were the explanation. Sentence 2 says the same thing from the other direction: the reason you can move along AD at all is that something is happening to the interest rate at every step.

Question 11 · the answer

They enter through different doors

Expansionary fiscal · enters at spending
G↑ → PE↑ → Y↑ → money demand↑ → r↑

Spending rises first. Output climbs because somebody bought more, and only then does the money market notice: higher output means more transactions, more money demand, and with real balances fixed, a higher interest rate.

The interest rate is the last link. It is dragged up behind output.

Expansionary monetary · enters at the rate
M↑ → M/P↑ → r↓ → I↑ → PE↑ → Y↑

The interest rate falls first. Nothing has been bought yet. The rate falls because there are more real balances than anyone wanted to hold, and only then does cheaper borrowing pull investment, spending and output up behind it.

The interest rate is the second link. Output is dragged up behind it.

One sentence for both

Fiscal policy pushes output, and the interest rate follows. Monetary policy pulls the interest rate, and output follows.

Which is why the rate has to move in opposite directions. In the fiscal chain the rate is a consequence of output rising, so it rises. In the monetary chain the rate is the cause of output rising, so it has to fall in order for output to rise at all.

The same move on one diagram, two different moves on the other

A lower price level and a bigger money supply are not the same thing

A fall in the price level to 80 and an increase in the money supply do the identical thing to the IS-IR diagram: IR shifts down, output goes to 340, the rate goes to 3%. On the AD diagram they could hardly be more different.

Question 12 · silent poll · pick a number on your own paper

One of these three is wrong

All three compare the two expansionary policies you have just run. Two of them are sound. One is not. Write down the number of the one that fails, and one line saying what it leaves out.

Same destination, different composition

Sentence 3 fails — and the half it gets right is the half you just proved. Output really does land on 340 either way, and on that score the two policies really are equal here.

What differs is the interest rate, and therefore investment. Fiscal policy took the rate from 5% up to 7%, so investment ends up lower than it was at point A: the expansion happened even though investment was pushed back, because government purchases more than made up the difference. Monetary policy took the rate down to 3%, so investment ends up higher than at point A: the expansion happened through investment.

Same output, different composition underneath. Two economies both producing 340 — one with more government purchases and less investment, the other with more investment — are not the same economy, and they will not have the same stock of factories and equipment a decade later.

Sentence 1 is true for exactly the reason sentence 3 is false: the AD diagram hides the interest rate. Sentence 2 says what it is hiding.
