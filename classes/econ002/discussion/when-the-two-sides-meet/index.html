Three curves on one diagram

Nothing new gets built today. The short-run aggregate supply curve and the long-run aggregate supply curve came from the deck on where output comes from. The aggregate demand curve came from the deck where you traced it out of the goods market and the money market together.

All three describe the same economy. Put them on the same axes and the economy has one place to sit.

Question 1 · write before you look

Three curves, three reasons

Shapes are easy to memorize and useless on their own. What matters today is why each curve has the shape it has, because the reason is what tells you which shocks move it.

Short-run aggregate supply
SRAS slopes up
A higher price level pushes the real wage below the marginal product of labor. Labor is now cheap relative to what it produces, so firms hire more workers and output rises. The nominal wage has not caught up yet — that is the whole meaning of “short run.”
Long-run aggregate supply
LRAS is vertical
Given time, workers negotiate nominal wages up in proportion to prices. The real wage returns to where it started, so employment returns to where it started, and output with it. Output ends at potential, whatever the price level is.
Aggregate demand
AD slopes down
A higher price level shrinks real money balances. With less real money to hold, the interest rate rises, investment falls, planned expenditure falls, and output falls with it. Nothing here is about substituting between goods.

Question 1 · the answer

Only one of the three moves

Aggregate demand, and nothing else

More money means more real balances at every price level, so the interest rate falls, investment rises, and planned expenditure rises. At every price level this economy now produces more. AD shifts right.

The two supply curves do not move, and the reason is worth saying out loud: they describe production, not spending. SRAS is built from firms deciding how many workers to hire given the real wage. LRAS is built from what the economy can produce when wages have fully adjusted. The Fed printing money changes neither the technology nor the number of workers nor the willingness of firms to hire at a given real wage.

Keep this test. To move a supply curve you have to change what it costs to produce or what the economy is capable of producing. To move demand you have to change what is spent. Money is spending.

M↑ → M/P↑ → r↓ → I↑ → PE↑ → U.I.↓ → Y↑   at every price level, so AD shifts right

That chain is the aggregate demand deck, unchanged. What is new today is that something is now standing in the way of it.

Question 2 · write before you look

Two kinds of equilibrium

Question 2 · on your own paper

Point A has all three curves running through it. Most points on that diagram do not. Write down the difference between a short-run equilibrium and a long-run equilibrium, stated in terms of which curves have to cross.

Then write down what has to be true about output at a long-run equilibrium, and why that follows from your first answer.

Two curves, or all three

Short-run equilibrium: SRAS and AD cross. That is all it takes. Firms are producing what they want to produce at the going price level, and buyers are buying it. Nothing is left unsold and nothing is rationed. It is a real equilibrium and the economy can sit there for a while.

Long-run equilibrium: all three cross at once. SRAS and AD still have to meet — goods still have to clear — but now they have to meet on LRAS.

And that extra requirement has a name you already know. LRAS stands at potential output, so demanding that the crossing land on it is exactly demanding that output equals potential and unemployment sits at its natural rate. A short-run equilibrium off LRAS is one where the nominal wage has not finished adjusting. As long as it has not, something is still moving.

Part 2 of 4

When demand moves

Aggregate demand moves for two kinds of reasons. The first is deliberate: fiscal policy changing government purchases or taxes, monetary policy changing the money supply.

The second is nobody's decision at all — a financial crisis, a swing in consumer confidence, a housing market crash, a change in what other countries want to buy. The diagram cannot tell the two apart, and does not need to. A shift is a shift.

Press play to run the short run first, then the long run.
Stage one · A to B · the real wage falls

AD shifts right and the economy climbs SRAS to B. Prices rose to 110 while the nominal wage stayed where it was, so the real wage fell below the marginal product of labor. That gap is exactly why firms hired the extra workers and produced 300 instead of 280.

Stage two · B to C · the wage catches up

Those workers notice. Everything costs more and their pay has not changed, so they negotiate higher nominal wages. Higher wages raise the cost of producing at any price level, which shifts SRAS left.

That continues until the real wage is back exactly where it started. And once it is, firms want exactly the number of workers they wanted before — so output is back at 280. The economy arrives at C: output 280, price level 120, with all three curves through it again.

Nobody chose this. No policy was used. The adjustment ran on its own, driven by workers bargaining over a real wage that had been quietly cut.

What a demand shock does

Temporary in output, permanent in prices

In the short run
Output moves  and  prices move

Output went from 280 to 300 and the price level from 100 to 110. They moved in the same direction, because the economy slid along a supply curve that had not moved.

A positive shock puts output above potential and unemployment below its natural rate. A negative shock does the reverse.

In the long run
Only prices move

Output returned to 280 on its own. The price level did not return — it settled at 120 and stayed there.

Positive demand shocks are inflationary. Negative demand shocks are deflationary. Neither one changes what this economy can produce.

Question 6 · silent poll · pick a number on your own paper

One of these three is wrong

All three sentences are about the demand shock you just worked through. Two of them reason correctly. One does not. Write down the number of the one that fails, and one line saying where its reasoning gives out.

A true observation, carried one step too far

Sentence 2 fails — and notice how much of it is correct. Output really did rise from 280 to 300. Anyone watching the economy in that period would have seen more goods produced and more people working. The observation is not the error.

The error is the word permanently. 300 was above potential, and the only reason it was reachable at all was that the nominal wage had temporarily fallen behind prices. That is a bargain with an expiration date, not an improvement in what the economy can do. Once wages caught up, output came back to 280 without anyone deciding it should.

The lasting change was the price level: 100 to 120. A demand shock leaves an inflation behind and nothing else.

Sentence 1 is the one worth keeping. SRAS shifted left with no change in technology and no change in the labor force — because SRAS is not a statement about capacity. It is a statement about how much firms choose to produce at a given price level, and that depends on the wage they are paying. Sentence 3 makes the same point from the other side: freeze the wage and the entire long-run adjustment never starts.

Question 7 · the answer

SRAS shifts left

Negative supply shock · short run
━━━ LRAS · solid ╌╌╌ SRAS · dashed —·— AD · dash-dot ╌╌╌ SRAS′ after the shock · dashed
Costs up → less produced at every price level

Energy is an input into almost everything, so a spike raises the cost of producing any given quantity. At every price level firms now find it worth producing less than before, and that is the definition of a leftward shift: SRAS moves left.

Aggregate demand has not moved. Nothing happened to the money supply, to government purchases, to taxes, or to how much households want to spend. So the economy slides up along the unmoved AD curve to point B: output 240, price level 120.

Output is now below potential of 280 and unemployment is above its natural rate.

The thing that makes this shock different

Output down and prices up

Every shock in this course so far has moved output and the price level the same way. Spending rose, both rose. Spending fell, both fell. That was not a coincidence — it is what happens whenever the thing that moves is demand and the economy slides along a supply curve.

Demand shock
Y and P move together

AD moves, the economy slides along SRAS. An upward-sloping curve means both coordinates rise, or both fall.

AD shifts → slide along SRAS → Y↑ P↑
Supply shock
Y and P move apart

SRAS moves, the economy slides along AD. A downward-sloping curve means one coordinate rises exactly when the other falls.

SRAS shifts → slide along AD → Y↓ P↑

High unemployment and high inflation, at the same time, out of a single shock. It has a name: stagflation — stagnation and inflation together. Before this diagram existed it looked like a contradiction, because the two problems were understood to be opposites. Here it takes one curve moving one direction.

Which curve moved is not a detail of bookkeeping. It decides whether the two problems you face are the same problem or two problems pulling against each other.

Underneath the diagram

Why demand comes down to meet supply

Firms cut production because their costs rose. But something has to bring spending down to match, or the goods market would not clear. Nobody told households to buy less. Press the links to walk the chain that does it.

Link 1 · the price level rose to 120

The Fed did not change the money supply. But the same number of dollars now buys less, so real money balances fell. Households and firms are holding less purchasing power in money form than they want to.

Link 2 · the money market clears on the interest rate

Wanting more real balances does not create them. The supply is fixed by the Fed and by the price level. What adjusts instead is the price of holding money — the interest rate rises until people are content with the balances that exist.

Link 3 · borrowing gets more expensive

A higher interest rate makes fewer investment projects worth undertaking, so investment falls. This is the same link that made your AD curve slope down in the first place.

Link 4 · PE↓ → U.I.↑ → Y↓

Lower investment means lower planned expenditure. Firms are still producing at the old rate, so the goods nobody bought pile up as unintended inventory — and that is the signal they act on. They cut production, and output falls, until unintended inventory is back to zero and spending and production match again at 240.

The chain, end to end
P↑ → M/P↓ → r↑ → I↓ → PE↓ → U.I.↑ → Y↓

This is not a new mechanism invented for supply shocks. It is the AD curve, read from the inside. Sliding up along AD from A to B just means running this chain, and the chain is what guarantees aggregate demand comes down far enough to meet the reduced supply.

Worth naming what the reduction in output is made of: it is mostly investment that got squeezed out, along with the consumption that the lost income would have supported. The energy shock hit costs; the interest rate is what passed the damage on to the rest of the economy.

If the shock is temporary

The curve that moved, moves back

Energy prices come back down. Costs fall to where they were, so at every price level firms are willing to produce what they were willing to produce before. SRAS returns to its original position, and the economy returns to point A: output 280, price level 100.

Question 8 · the answer · case two

If the shock is permanent

Permanent supply shock · a permanent loss of productivity
━━━ LRAS · solid ╌╌╌ SRAS · dashed ╌╌╌ SRAS′ · dashed —·— AD · dash-dot ━━━ LRAS′ · solid
Potential output itself falls

Now suppose the higher cost of energy is here to stay. Energy is an input, so a permanently higher price for it means every worker and every machine in the economy turns out less real output than before. That is a permanent fall in productivity.

Be careful about what has and has not changed. The economy has exactly as many workers, machines and factories as it had yesterday. What fell is how much output each of them yields — and potential output is not a count of inputs, it is what those inputs can produce. So even with wages fully adjusted and everyone who wants a job at the going real wage holding one, this economy now tops out lower. LRAS itself shifts left, to 240.

Now look at point B. It has AD through it, SRAS′ through it, and LRAS′ through it. All three — so B is not a short-run equilibrium waiting to be undone. B is the new long-run equilibrium.

There is no path back to 280, and it is worth being precise about why. Not because the adjustment is slow, and not because anyone is unemployed. Because 280 is no longer a quantity this economy is able to produce.

Two shocks that look alike and are not

Sentence 3 fails. It treats “permanent” as a quantity of time — a temporary shock that lasts a very long while — when the distinction on this diagram is about which curve moved.

Waiting works after a temporary shock for one specific reason: SRAS goes back on its own, because the cost that pushed it left goes back down. That is the entire mechanism, and it is a claim about SRAS only.

A permanent shock moved LRAS. No amount of waiting restores productivity that has been permanently lost, and there is no force in the model pulling LRAS back to the right. Waiting is not a slow version of the fix — it is not the fix at all.

Sentence 2 is the sharpest one on the slide. It separates two things students routinely fuse: closing the output gap and getting back to the old level of output. After a permanent shock the gap closes at 240, and the economy is fully employed and permanently poorer at the same time.

What policy can and cannot do

An amendment to the Federal Reserve Act in 1977 gave the Fed a dual mandate: promote maximum employment, and keep prices stable. Two targets, written into law together.

The federal government carries a parallel obligation under the Employment Act of 1946. Congress and the President are charged with the same two things.

Prices are too high and output is above potential, so the Fed wants both to come down. It reduces the money supply, which raises the interest rate, cuts investment, and pulls planned expenditure down — shifting AD back left to where it started, and the economy back down SRAS to A.

A negative demand shock calls for the mirror image: raise the money supply and shift AD right. Either way the rule is the same — lean against the wind, push demand back the way it came.

Question 11 · the answer

One instrument cannot push two variables apart

No. And the reason is geometric, not political.

Go back to the aggregate demand deck and read off the one fact that settles this: every shift in AD moves output and the price level in the same direction. Shift AD right and both rise. Shift AD left and both fall. There is no third option, because the economy slides along an upward-sloping supply curve either way.

Stagflation puts the two mandates out of position in opposite directions. Output needs to go up. Prices need to come down. The Fed has one instrument, and that instrument moves them together.

So the request is not difficult, it is unavailable. Fixing one problem necessarily makes the other worse, and no amount of skill or resolve at the Fed changes that.

What is wanted
Y↑  and  P↓

Output back up toward 280, price level back down toward 100. The two mandates point in opposite directions.

What AD shifts deliver
Y↑ P↑  or  Y↓ P↓

Both up, or both down. The one instrument available moves the two variables in the same direction, always.

A demand shock leaves the Fed a problem. A supply shock leaves it a choice.

The choice, drawn out

Two branches, neither of them home

Both diagrams start from the same place: the economy sitting at B after the energy shock, with SRAS′ where the shock left it. Same axes, same scale. The only difference is which way the Fed pushes aggregate demand.

━━━ LRAS ╌╌╌ SRAS′ —·— AD —·— AD′ ○ A · before the shock

Branch 2 · prioritize stable output
━━━ LRAS ╌╌╌ SRAS′ —·— AD —·— AD′ ○ A · before the shock
Output restored, prices worse

The Fed raises the money supply (M↑). AD shifts right, and the economy climbs SRAS′ to E: output 280, price level 140.

Output is back at potential and unemployment is back at its natural rate. The price level, which had already risen from 100 to 120, is now at 140. The employment mandate is met and the inflation mandate is missed by more than before the Fed acted.

Neither branch reaches A. Point D restores the old price level at the cost of output; point E restores output at the cost of the price level; and doing nothing leaves the economy at B, missing both. Every available move fixes one mandate by worsening the other.

Since the late 1970s the Fed has generally prioritized keeping inflation under control.

The other set of hands

Fiscal policy, same trade-off

Congress and the President reach aggregate demand from a different side, but they reach the same curve. Contractionary means higher taxes or lower government purchases; expansionary means the reverse.

Contractionary fiscal policy
T↑ or G↓ → PE↓ → U.I.↑ → Y↓

AD shifts left. On the two diagrams you just saw, this lands on the same point D the Fed reached with M↓.

Expansionary fiscal policy
G↑ or T↓ → PE↑ → U.I.↓ → Y↑

AD shifts right. This lands on the same point E the Fed reached with M↑. Different lever, same curve, same outcome.

So the constraint is not something peculiar to monetary policy. Both instruments shift aggregate demand, and that is the only thing either of them can do. Against a supply shock, both face exactly the same choice between the two mandates.

What differs between them is political, not economic. Contractionary fiscal policy means raising taxes or cutting spending while the economy looks strong, which is difficult to enact. Expansionary fiscal policy is easier to pass. That asymmetry gives fiscal policy an inflationary tilt — not because the model says so, but because only one of its two directions is politically available in practice.

Since the late 1970s, most stabilization has consequently fallen to the Fed. The exception is major downturns, where fiscal policy has been used heavily.

Judging an outcome without checking what was available

Sentence 1 fails. Controlling inflation really is the Fed's job, and inflation really is above target — so the sentence gets both of its facts right and still reasons badly.

The missing step is the set of outcomes that was reachable. After a supply shock every available move trades one mandate against the other. If the Fed took branch 2 and output is back at potential, the higher price level is not a mistake it made; it is the price of the branch it chose, and the alternative was 200 of output. An outcome that misses one target is evidence of the constraint, not evidence of an error.

This is a habit worth taking out of the course: before judging a choice by its outcome, ask what the other outcomes on offer were. A policy can only be assessed against the options it actually had.

Sentence 2 follows directly. The disagreement that remains is about how much a point of unemployment costs relative to a point of inflation, and whose costs count — a question about values, which the diagram does not answer and was never going to. Sentence 3 is the sharp version of the same test: after a demand shock the branch that fixes both exists, so missing both is a real failure; after a supply shock it does not exist.
