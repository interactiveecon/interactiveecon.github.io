Answer · Question 1

Output rises by 64

Trace it the way you traced the fall in investment. The government buying more is somebody selling more, and the chain starts from there.

What happens, in order

Round 1 is the whole 16

Look at where G sits: PE = C + I + G. Government purchases are a component of planned expenditure, so when G rises by 16, planned expenditure rises by the full 16 before a single household has reacted to anything. That is exactly the position investment held last time, which is why this is the same multiplier you already met.

Then round 2: the extra 16 of production is 16 of somebody's income. Consumption rises by 0.75 × 16 = 12, planned expenditure rises again, and the loop restarts on a smaller number. Each round is three quarters of the one before it.

+16 + 12 + 9 + 6.75 + … = +64
ΔY = 11 − MPC × ΔG   =   4 × 16 = +64

With MPC = 0.75 the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4. Hold on to the shape of that fraction: a 1 on top, because the whole of ΔG lands on planned expenditure. The next part is about what happens when that is no longer true.

Answer · Question 3

They are not in it

There is no T in PE = C + I + G. Not hidden, not implied — absent. And that single fact is the whole of today.

G and I
components of planned expenditure

They are terms in the sum. Change one by 16 and the sum changes by 16, immediately, with no household involved. That is why the numerator of the spending multiplier is a plain 1.

T
not a component at all

A tax is not something anyone buys. It reaches planned expenditure only by going through C — it changes what households have left to spend, and they decide what to do with the difference.

T↑ → disposable income↓ → C↓ → PE↓ → PE < Y → U.I.↑ → firms produce less → Y↓

Count the arrows. The spending chain had one step before it reached PE. This one has three, and something is lost at the step in the middle. That loss is the number we need next.

The step in the middle

The shift is the change in consumption, not the change in taxes

A household is handed a tax bill 16 larger than last year. It does not cut its spending by 16. It has two places to take the 16 from, and it uses both.

Straight from the consumption function
C = 25 + 0.75(Y − T)
ΔC = −MPC × ΔT
ΔC = −0.75 × 16 = −12

So planned expenditure shifts down by 12, not by 16.

It never was spending

The missing 4 comes out of saving. Households were putting a quarter of each dollar of disposable income aside, and when the tax bill arrives they take part of the payment from that quarter rather than from the shopping.

That 4 is real money leaving the household — but it was never going to be spent, so planned expenditure never feels it. Only the part of a tax change that would have been spent shows up in PE.

ΔT = +16  →  12 out of spending, 4 out of saving

Now put the two policies side by side at this point in the story. The same 16 has landed on planned expenditure as 16 in one case and as 12 in the other, and nothing after this step treats them differently.

Answer · Question 2

Output falls by 48

Once the shift is known, the rest is the machinery you already have. A shift of −12 in planned expenditure runs through the same rounds, with the same MPC, and gets multiplied by the same 4.

The answer to Question 2
ΔY = 4 × (−12) = −48     Y: 280 → 232

Check it directly: PE = 25 + 0.75(Y − 36) + 20 + 40 = 58 + 0.75Y, so 0.25Y = 58 and Y = 232. The same 232.

The tax multiplier
ΔY = −MPC1 − MPC × ΔT   =   −0.750.25 × 16 = −3 × 16 = −48

It is the two steps stacked into one number: 0.75 converts the tax change into a change in planned expenditure, and 1 ÷ 0.25 runs that through the rounds. The tax multiplier is −3.

Compared with 1 ÷ (1 − MPC)
1 · The negative sign
Taxes and output move in opposite directions. Raising T lowers output; cutting T raises it. The sign is not an extra rule to memorize — it is the middle of the chain, where more tax means less disposable income.
2 · MPC on top instead of 1
The numerator measures how much of the policy reaches planned expenditure in the first place. For G it is all of it, so the numerator is 1. For T it is the MPC, because the rest of a tax change is absorbed by saving.

Since the MPC is always less than 1, the tax multiplier is always smaller in size than the spending multiplier. Not because taxes are gentler, but because part of a tax change never touches spending.

The sentence to leave with

A 16 increase in government purchases raises output by 64. A 16 increase in taxes lowers it by 48. Same size policy, and the effects are not the same size — because only one of the two lands on planned expenditure in full.

True about the household, false about the economy

Sentence 3 fails. And notice how much of it is correct. $16 really does leave the household in both cases. The household is $16 poorer either way. Nothing in that first clause is wrong.

The error is in what the sentence assumes those $16 were doing. The spending cut removes 16 from planned expenditure; the tax increase removes only 12 — because households pay part of a tax bill out of saving rather than out of spending. Four of the sixteen was never going to be spent, so it cannot be subtracted from spending.

The consequence is not small. Multiply both through and the spending cut costs 64 of output while the tax increase costs 48. Two policies described as equally hard differ by a third.

Sentence 2 is the check on this. It says: if the MPC were 1, nothing would be diverted to saving, the initial shift would be 16 in both cases, and the two really would be equal. That the answers differ is entirely a statement about the MPC.

Not a coincidence

Why the answer is the size of the spending change

Output rose by 16, and the spending change was 16. That is not an accident of these numbers. It happens for any MPC, and one line of algebra shows why.

They differ by exactly one
11 − MPC − MPC1 − MPC = 1 − MPC1 − MPC = 1
With MPC = 0.75:   4 − 3 = 1     so   ΔY = 1 × 16 = +16
A fully financed increase moves output dollar for dollar

Two fractions with the same denominator, and their numerators are 1 and MPC. Subtract them and the denominator cancels against the numerator. Whatever the MPC, the spending multiplier is exactly one larger than the size of the tax multiplier.

So a spending increase paid for entirely by taxes raises output by exactly the amount spent — no more, and no less. The multiplier gets almost all of its work undone by the tax, and what is left over is the original purchase itself.

And you can see the same fact on the diagram: the line went up 16 from the spending, down 12 from the tax, and the leftover 4 is exactly the quarter of the tax that households would have saved. Multiply that 4 by 4 and you are back to 16.

One equation for any pair of changes

When both move, the effects simply add

There is no separate formula for the case where G and T both change. Work out what each one does on its own, then add the two answers.

Before you use it
Signs are not optional
ΔG and ΔT carry their own signs, and the tax term already has a minus built in. A cut in taxes is a negative ΔT meeting a negative multiplier, and two negatives raise output.

A statement about the budget, mistaken for one about output

Sentence 1 fails. It is doing something subtle: it takes a fact about the government's accounts — that the two changes are equal and opposite in dollars — and assumes it must also be a fact about the economy. Those are two different ledgers.

Q8 · predict · a direction, and the mechanism

Write down what happens to the budget deficit. Wider, narrower, or unchanged — and, more importantly, name the thing that makes it move when no one has voted for anything.

Answer · Question 8

The deficit widens on its own

The budget moves because much of it is written as a rule rather than as an amount. The law does not say how many dollars to pay out; it says who qualifies. When a recession changes how many people qualify, the dollars change by themselves.

These are the automatic stabilizers: items in the budget that move with the state of the economy in the direction that steadies output. Press each card once you have predicted which way it moves.

Unemployment insurance
Payments rise. More people are out of work, so more people claim. Nothing about the program changed — the number of eligible claimants did. Spending goes up.
Progressive income tax
Receipts fall more than proportionally. Because higher income is taxed at a higher rate, falling income moves people into lower brackets. Their average tax rate drops as well as their income, so revenue falls faster than income does. Taxes go down.
Food assistance and Medicaid
Enrollment rises. Eligibility depends on income, and incomes have fallen, so more households qualify and enroll. Spending goes up.
Spending up, taxes down, no vote taken

Every one of the three moves the same way: G up, T down. That is the definition of a widening deficit — and in the language of this discussion, it is a positive ΔG together with a negative ΔT, arriving on their own.

recession → claims↑ and receipts↓ → deficit widens   |   no legislation required
The punchline of Part 4

That widening deficit is the budget doing its job

It is tempting to read a deficit that grows during a downturn as a budget that has gotten out of control. Look at what those payments are doing instead. They are landing on household income at the exact moment income is falling — which is where the whole of Part 1 tells us to look.

Every round gets smaller

Go back to the multiplier rounds. A contraction propagates because lost production is lost income, and lost income is lost consumption. Each round is the MPC times the last.

Now put the stabilizers into that loop. A household whose earnings fall is partly replaced by an insurance payment and pays less tax at the same time, so its disposable income falls by less than its earnings did. Less of the fall reaches consumption, so the next round is smaller than it would have been, and so is every round after it.

The stabilizers do not stop the recession. They shorten every step of the chain, and a shallower chain is a shallower contraction. The deficit widened because the budget was absorbing part of the shock instead of passing it on.

earnings↓ → disposable income↓ by less → smaller rounds → shallower contraction
The same mechanism, running the other way

Fiscal drag

A stabilizer has to work in both directions or it is not stabilizing anything. Run the expansion and watch the progressive income tax do the reverse of what it did in the recession.

In a recession
incomes fall into lower brackets

Average tax rates fall, disposable income is cushioned, and the contraction is shallower than it would have been.

In an expansion
incomes rise into higher brackets

Average tax rates climb without any law changing, disposable income grows more slowly than earnings, and the expansion is slowed. That slowing is fiscal drag.

One rule, two directions

Nobody legislated the tax increase during the expansion — the rate schedule sat unchanged while incomes moved up through it. That is precisely what happened in reverse during the recession, and it is the same sentence about the same tax code.

So fiscal drag is not a separate phenomenon. It is what an automatic stabilizer looks like when it is holding back a boom rather than cushioning a bust, and someone who welcomes the cushion has already accepted the drag.

Question 9 · write · commit on paper

Which deficits were chosen?

We now have a problem. Deficits widen when the government decides to spend more or tax less — and they also widen on their own when the economy weakens. Looking at a deficit tells you it is large. It does not tell you which of those two things put it there.

Q9 · design the comparison, do not just describe the problem

Write down how you would tell a deficit caused by the business cycle from one that would be there anyway. You are being asked for a method: what would you have to know, or hold fixed, to separate the two?

Hold the economy fixed, not the budget

The trick is to stop asking what the budget is and ask what it would be at one agreed level of output. Take today's tax rules and today's spending programs, and ask what they would collect and pay out if the economy were producing at the level associated with the natural rate of unemployment — the same natural rate you met in the unemployment discussion.

That number is the full-employment budget. Because the state of the economy is held fixed inside it, anything left in it has to come from policy.

Split the deficit in two
Structural deficit
The part that remains in the full-employment budget — the deficit that would still be there at the natural rate of unemployment. This one was chosen: it is a statement about the tax rules and the spending programs themselves.
Cyclical deficit
The rest — the part that exists only because output is below that level. Nobody voted for it. It is the automatic stabilizers doing their work, and it closes on its own as output recovers.

Two economies with identical tax law and identical programs can run very different deficits, if one of them is in a downturn. Their structural deficits would be the same, and the whole of the difference would be cyclical.

Question 10 · silent poll · pick a number on paper

One of these three is wrong

All three sentences are about reading a deficit that has grown. Two of them are sound. One is not. Write down the number of the one that fails, and one line saying where its reasoning gives out.

A real observation, and an inference that does not follow

Sentence 2 fails. The observation is fine: deficits are measured, and this one grew. The word doing the damage is “must.” It treats a vote as the only thing that can widen a deficit.

Part 4 is one long counterexample. A cyclical deficit widens with no vote at all — claims rise because more people qualify, receipts fall because incomes dropped into lower brackets, and the rules that produced both were written years earlier. That is exactly what automatic stabilizers do.

Notice too that the sentence could be wrong in the opposite direction. Congress could cut spending in the same year, and if the downturn were deep enough the deficit would still widen. The number moved; the number alone cannot tell you who moved it.

Sentence 3 is the repair. Hold output fixed at the natural rate, look at what the budget would be there, and whatever remains is the part that policy is responsible for.
