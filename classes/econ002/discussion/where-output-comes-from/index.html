A different question about GDP

Two units ago we measured GDP and split it into the categories people spend on: consumption, investment, government purchases, net exports. That told us where output went. It said nothing about where it came from, or about how much an economy is capable of producing in the first place.

Q1 · write it down before the next slide

An economy is going to produce something — bread, haircuts, software, anything. Write down what it needs in order to produce. List as many things as you can, then circle the two you think are the most basic.

Answer · Question 1

Two inputs, and a recipe for combining them

Almost every list collapses into two factors of production. Everything else is either one of these two wearing a different name, or a statement about how well they are combined.

Capital · K
K
The tools workers use. An accountant's computer, a carpenter's hammer, a professor's projector, a bakery's ovens. Capital does not get used up in an afternoon — it sits there and gets used again.
Labor · L
L
The time people spend working. Not the people themselves and not their paychecks — the hours. A bakery with six bakers on shift is using six units of labor.
The production function

The two factors are combined by a production function, written

Y = F(K, L)

where F is the technology available — the recipe. A recipe tells you how to combine flour and an oven into bread; a production function tells you how to combine capital and labor into output. Better technology is simply a better recipe: more output from exactly the same inputs.

Marginal product of labor

The middle column is the marginal product of labor: the extra output the firm gets from one more unit of labor, holding capital fixed.

MPL = ΔY / ΔL

It falls all the way down: 24, 20, 16, 12, 8, 4. That pattern has its own name — diminishing marginal product of labor. Holding capital fixed, more labor means a lower marginal product for each additional unit.

All three sentences are about the table you just filled in. Two of them are sound. One is not. Write down the number of the one you think fails, and one line saying what goes wrong in it.

Adding less is not the same as subtracting

Sentence 2 fails. It slides from a falling marginal column to a falling total column, and those are different columns. Look back at the table: the marginal product falls the whole way — 24, 20, 16, 12, 8, 4 — and the total rises the whole way, 24, 44, 60, 72, 80, 84. Six bakers make 84 loaves, which is more than the 60 that three bakers make, not fewer.

Every number in the marginal column is still positive. Each baker adds something; each just adds less than the one before. Output would only fall if the marginal product went negative — if a baker got so far in the way that the shop turned out fewer loaves with them there.

The same story for the other factor

Capital diminishes too — and for the same reason

Turn the experiment around. Fix the bakers and add ovens one at a time instead. The first oven is transformative. The second helps a lot. The fifth sits half empty, because there are only so many bakers to load it.

Marginal product of capital · MPK
MPK = ΔY / ΔK
The extra output from one more unit of capital, holding labor fixed. It falls as capital rises, exactly as MPL falls as labor rises.
Marginal product of labor · MPL
MPL = ΔY / ΔL
The extra output from one more unit of labor, holding capital fixed. 24, 20, 16, 12, 8, 4 in our bakery.
It comes from the factor you held still

Both stories have the same shape because both have the same cause: something is being held fixed. Three bakers sharing two ovens spend part of the day waiting for an oven, reaching around each other for the same bench, lining up for the same mixer. The fourth baker walks into a shop that is already crowded, so she finds less to do that adds a loaf.

There is nothing wrong with the workers. The fourth baker is not lazier or less skilled than the first — swap them and nothing changes. She adds less because of what she was given to work with, not because of who she is.

What the firm is actually trying to do

One goal: the largest possible profit

A firm hires capital and labor for one reason, and it is not to be generous or to be stingy. It hires whatever combination leaves it with the most profit. Profit is what comes in minus what goes out, and there are exactly three pieces.

Money in · revenue
P × Y
The firm sells Y units of output at a price of P each.
Money out · labor
W × L
It hires L workers at a wage of W each.
Money out · capital
R × K
It rents K units of capital at a rental rate of R each.
Π = PY − WL − RK

Note which letters are prices and which are quantities. P, W and R are prices — the price of a loaf, the price of an hour of work, the price of an oven for a day. Y, L and K are quantities.

Q5 · write it down

  2. What changes on the cost side, and by how much?

Answer · Question 5

Revenue changes by P × MPL. One more worker raises production by MPL loaves — that is exactly what MPL means — and every one of those loaves sells for P. So the extra loaves bring in P × MPL dollars.

Cost changes by W. One more worker is paid one more wage. The ovens are unchanged, so RK does not move.

hire one more → revenue +(P × MPL) → cost +W

Answer · Question 6

Yes, hire the first baker. She brings in $12.00 and costs $8.00, so profit rises by $4.00. The second is worth hiring too, at $10.00 against $8.00. The third brings in exactly $8.00 and costs exactly $8.00. The fourth would bring in $6.00 against a wage of $8.00, so hiring her would lower profit by $2.00.

The stopping point

Three bakers, 60 loaves

The hiring rule

Keep hiring while P × MPL > W. Stop when P × MPL = W. The optimum is 3 bakers, producing 60 loaves.

The break-even worker is what marks the optimum — not the most profitable worker, the last one who does not lose money. This is the same shape as the decisions in the first lecture: study one more hour while the benefit of the hour beats its cost, and stop at the hour where the two are equal.

The result this whole part was built for

Divide both sides by P

The stopping condition was P × MPL = W. Divide both sides by the price level and it turns into something with a name.

MPL = W / P
W / P · the real wage
$8 / $0.50 = 16
What a worker's pay actually buys, measured in goods — here, 16 loaves a day. Not the number printed on the check. A wage of $8 means one thing when bread is $0.50 and something else entirely when bread is $1.00.
MPL · the productivity
16 loaves
What the last worker hired actually produces, measured in the same goods. At three bakers, the third one adds 16 loaves a day.
Labor is paid its productivity

Both sides are measured in loaves, and in equilibrium they are equal. A worker is paid, in goods, exactly what that worker produces in goods. Not what the owner feels like paying, and not what the worker deserves in some broader sense — what the last worker hired adds to output.

The implication is blunt and worth stating directly: to be paid more, you have to produce more. A raise has to come from a higher MPL — better tools, a better recipe, more skill.

The identical argument run for ovens instead of bakers gives MPK = R / P. Capital is paid its productivity too.

Right answer, wrong reasoning

Sentence 3 fails — and notice that its conclusion is correct. The third baker is the last one to hire. But the reason given is only half of a comparison. She also costs $8. Bringing in $8 is not what makes her worth hiring; it is what makes her the last one.

Run the sentence's logic on the fourth baker and it breaks immediately: she brings in $6, which is a positive number, so by that reasoning she is worth hiring too. She is not. Revenue added is never a verdict on its own — it only becomes one when it is set against the wage.

That the third baker adds exactly zero profit is precisely why she marks the stopping point rather than a bargain. Hire one more and profit falls; hire one fewer and you gave up a worker who was still adding something.

Three facts you already have

From Part 2
More labor drives the marginal product down, because the ovens do not multiply along with the bakers.
L↑ → MPL↓
From Part 3 · arithmetic
A higher price level shrinks the real wage, as long as the nominal wage sits still. The same $8 buys fewer loaves.
P↑ → W/P↓
From Part 3 · the rule
Firms hire up to the point where the real wage and the marginal product are equal, and no further.
W/P = MPL

Read them in order and a question forms on its own. If a higher price level pushes the real wage down, the third fact is broken — the real wage is no longer equal to the marginal product. Something has to move to repair it. The first fact says what.

Answer · Question 8 · one link at a time

Higher prices make workers cheaper

P↑ from $0.50 to $1.00 → W/P↓ from 16 to 8

The wage is still $8, but a loaf now costs $1.00 instead of $0.50, so that $8 buys 8 loaves instead of 16. In real terms the bakers just got cheaper, without anyone cutting anyone's pay.

W/P = 8 < MPL = 16 → not in equilibrium

At three bakers the third one still adds 16 loaves, but now costs the equivalent of only 8. The hiring rule is violated, and violated in the direction that says hire more.

hire more → L↑ from 3 to 5 → MPL↓ from 16 to 8

Adding bakers pushes the marginal product back down — the fourth adds 12, the fifth adds 8. At the fifth baker MPL = 8, which is exactly the new real wage, and the rule holds again.

L↑ → Y↑ from 60 to 80 loaves

Five bakers produce 80 loaves where three produced 60. A higher price level produced more output — not because anyone wanted more bread, and not because the recipe improved, but because the wage stood still while prices moved.

The real wage rises to $10 ÷ $0.50 = 20 loaves. Now W/P = 20 is above the third baker's MPL of 16, so she is no longer worth keeping. Employment falls to 2 bakers, where MPL = 20 and the rule holds again, and output falls from 60 to 44 loaves.

Left: the real wage line moves up to 20 loaves, so hiring backs down the curve to two bakers. Middle: two bakers make only 44 loaves instead of 60. Right: less output at the very same $0.50 is the whole aggregate supply curve moving left, from A to D.

The same test, for the third unit in a row

Along the curve, or the whole curve?

You met this test with demand curves, and again with supply curves. It has not changed. Look at what is written on the axes.

On an axis → move along the curve
The price level, P

P is written on the vertical axis. So a change in the price level cannot move the curve — it just tells you which point on the curve you are standing at. That was Q8: prices doubled and we slid from A up to B along one unchanged SRAS.

Not on an axis → shift the whole curve
The nominal wage, W

W appears on neither axis. So a change in the nominal wage has nowhere to show up as a point, and it re-prices output at every price level at once. That was Q9: the wage rose and the entire curve moved left.

If the thing that changed is measured on one of the two axes, you move along the curve. If it is anything else, the curve moves. Same question you asked of the burrito market; same answer procedure; new pair of axes.

Give the wage time to catch up

Everything so far assumed the nominal wage could not move. In the long run it can. Contracts come up for renewal, and workers who have watched their real wage get cut do something about it.

Question 12 · quick check · pick one

So what separates the short run from the long run?

The price level doubles from $0.50 to $1.00. In which case does the bakery end up back at 60 loaves?

Commit on paper, then the answer.
Potential output

The entire difference between the short run and the long run is whether the nominal wage has adjusted yet. That is the whole distinction. Same bakery, same ovens, same table of marginal products, same hiring rule — the only question is whether W has caught up with P.

The 60 loaves the bakery returns to has a name: Ȳ = 60 is potential output, also called the natural rate of output. It is the level produced once the labor market has settled — which is the same state you met in the unemployment unit as the natural rate of unemployment. Not a ceiling the economy can never pass, and not a happy outcome by definition. Just the level it comes back to.

The whole discussion in one line

It all came from one column

Output comes from capital, labor, and the recipe that combines them. Almost everything else in this unit follows from one fact: each extra worker adds less than the one before.
Why wages are what they are
Because MPL falls, the firm eventually reaches a worker who adds exactly her wage — and stops. That gives MPL = W/P: labor is paid its productivity.
Why SRAS slopes up
A higher P cuts the real wage while W sits still, so firms hire. But each new hire adds less, so it takes ever larger price increases to buy more output — an upward, steepening curve.
Why LRAS is vertical
When W finally rises in step with P, the real wage is back where it began, so the hiring problem is unchanged and output returns to Ȳ = 60, whatever the price level.

Take away diminishing marginal product and none of the three survives. Every worker would add the same, so there would be no last worth hiring, no stopping point to set the wage, and no reason for the amount produced to depend on the price level at all.
