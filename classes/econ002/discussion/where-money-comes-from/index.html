Part 1 of 4

What money is

Money is less a substance than a job description. Three jobs, and anything that does all three is money — whatever it happens to be made of.

Question 1 · write before you look

What a twenty dollar bill does

A gold bar is valuable. A house is valuable. A twenty dollar bill is worth about a tenth of a cent as paper, and yet you would rather be handed the twenty than a tenth of a cent of gold.

Q1 · two things, in your own words

Write down two things a twenty dollar bill does that a gold bar and a house do not. Not two reasons it is convenient — two jobs it performs.

Money has three jobs

A medium of exchange: what sellers generally accept and buyers generally use to pay for goods and services. A store of value: an asset that carries purchasing power from now into later. A unit of account: a consistent way of quoting prices, so that “eight dollars” means the same thing at every counter in the country.

Measure the gold bar and the house against those three. Gold is a fine store of value and a poor medium of exchange — you cannot shave a flake off it at the register. A house is a store of value and nothing else. Neither one quotes a price for anything.

What the first job buys you

Without a medium of exchange you have to trade goods directly for goods. That is barter, and barter requires a double coincidence of wants: you must find somebody who has what you want and wants what you have. Not one condition. Two, at once, in the same person.

Every hour spent hunting for that person is an hour not spent producing anything. What money removes is the search, and the property that lets it do so is liquidity — money is portable and it is accepted without an argument. That is not a small convenience. It is most of why an economy with money produces more than one without.

Question 2 · write before you look

Why anyone takes the paper

You cannot eat it, wear it, or build with it, and no government office will trade it for gold. It is a rectangle of cotton and linen with ink on it.

Q2 · one sentence

Write down why you are nevertheless willing to accept it. Push past “because it is money” — that is the thing being explained.

Commodity money and fiat money

Commodity money has intrinsic value in some other use: gold, silver, copper, salt, grain, shells. If everybody stopped accepting it tomorrow it would still be worth something, because it is still salt.

Fiat money has no other use — it is intrinsically worthless. It works for two reasons. It is legal tender, which means the government requires it to be accepted in settlement of debts. And, far more importantly, everyone expects everyone else to take it. Your twenty is valuable to you because you are confident it will be valuable to the next person. The belief is the backing.

Whoever decides how much exists is tempted

If the value of money does not come from what it is made of, then whoever controls how much of it exists can simply decide there should be more. Doing that quickly is currency debasement: the value of every dollar already in someone’s pocket falls.

You have already met the consequence under another name. This is inflation, arriving deliberately. And it is the reason the money supply in most countries is run by a partially independent central bank rather than by whoever is standing for reelection — the person with the most to gain from printing is rarely the person who should hold the switch.

M1 is transactions money: cash plus deposits. It is money you can spend without converting it into anything first.

M2 is M1 plus near monies — close substitutes for transactions money, such as certificates of deposit and money market accounts.

There is no rule, and that is the honest answer

Broader measures than M2 exist, and at least one of them counts the unused credit on your credit cards as part of the money supply — on the grounds that you can walk into a store and spend it. Once you allow that, the house starts looking like a hard case too: it is spendable, just slowly.

So there is no rule for deciding what is and is not money. There is only a judgment about how liquid something has to be before it counts, and different judgments give different measures. If you were torn on the certificate of deposit or the credit limit, you were torn about exactly the right thing.

In this course, money always means M1. Every number for the rest of today is a number about M1.

Loans and bonds

A loan
principal
interest rate
length
Issued by a bank to a borrower — a car loan, a student loan, a mortgage. Three properties and no more: the principal is the amount borrowed, the interest rate is what the borrower pays for the use of it, and the length is how long they have.
A bond · the one we will use
maturity: 10 years
face value: $5,000
coupon: $200 a year
Issued by a government or a corporation when the sum needed is larger than a bank loan would cover. Whoever holds this bond collects $200 every year for ten years, and then $5,000 when it matures.
The issuer names the terms; the market names the price

The issuer would like to sell this bond for its face value of $5,000. It does not get to decide. The bond goes into the bond market, and the market sets the price. If buyers will not pay $5,000 for a $200 coupon, the issuer has to raise the coupon to move it.

That is the only bond arithmetic you need today: the coupon and the price move in opposite directions. A bond nobody wants is a bond that has to pay more.

Bonds issued by the federal government are Treasury securities, and they are the ones the Fed buys and sells in Part 3. Corporations issue bonds on the same three terms; those are corporate bonds.

Part 2 of 4

Banks make money

A bank performs financial intermediation: it transfers funds from savers to borrowers. Which makes a bank sound like a corridor money walks down. It is not a corridor. It is a place where money is manufactured.

What a bank owns and what it owes

The simplified balance sheet

A bank is a business, so it keeps the same books as any other business: assets equal liabilities. What makes a bank strange is which items land on which side.

One bank, the instant a deposit of 400 arrives and before it does anything
AssetsAmountLiabilitiesAmount
Reserves400Deposits400
Loans0——
Total assets400Total liabilities400

The liabilities are the deposits. Your checking balance is money the bank owes you, which is why it sits on the side of things owed.

The assets are what the bank owns: its reserves — its own deposits in its account at the Fed, plus the cash in its vault — and its loans.

The reserve ratio

Regulation requires a bank to hold a certain percentage of its deposits as reserves. That percentage is the required reserve ratio, and the ratio a bank actually holds is written rr = R / D. Anything held above the requirement is excess reserves: excess reserves = actual reserves − required reserves.

Reserves are the part of a deposit the bank does not lend. Everything in the next six slides follows from that single sentence.

The money multiplier

The multiple by which deposits can increase for every dollar of reserves. One number, and it replaces the entire chain of T-accounts.

Same answer as the bars, reached without a single balance sheet. The chain converges to 1,600, so 1,200 of money was created out of a deposit of 400.

The reserve ratio belongs to the banks

The reserve ratio is chosen by the individual banks. The required reserve ratio is a floor: a bank may hold more than the requirement, never less. In this example we assumed banks hold exactly what is required, 25%, which is the only reason m̂ came out to exactly 4.

Hold on to that. In Part 3 the Fed changes the money supply by changing a number it does not control.

Both chains shrink by 0.75 each round and both multipliers come out to 4. That is not luck with the numbers — it is the same sum. In one case the fraction surviving each round is the MPC, the share of income that gets re-spent. In the other it is 1 − rr, the share of a deposit that gets re-lent.

What is being multiplied could hardly be more different. There, income was re-spent and output rose — a story about production. Here, deposits are re-lent and the money supply rises — a story about accounting entries, in which nothing is produced at all.

The two share the shape of the sum and nothing else. Recognizing a familiar formula is not the same as understanding what it is doing.

Question 6 · predict · commit before you look

And the reserves?

All of that lending has now happened. Deposits stand at 1,600 where there were 400. Every bank in the chain is holding reserves against its own deposits: Bank 1 has 100, Bank 2 has 75, Bank 3 has 56.25, and so on down.

Q6 · a number, and one line saying why

Write down how many reserves exist in this economy now. One number for the whole banking system, and the sentence that gets you there.

Answer · Question 6

Still 400

Not one new reserve was created

100 + 75 + 56.25 + 42.19 + … = 400. Every bank holds a quarter of its deposits, so the reserve chain shrinks by the same 0.75 and sums to a quarter of 1,600 — which is exactly the 400 that walked in.

Watch where each bank’s reserves came from. Bank 1 kept 100 and sent 300 away. Bank 2 kept 75 of that 300 and sent 225 away. At no point did a reserve appear; reserves only ever moved from one bank to another. What grew was the column of deposits stacked on top of them.

Deposits multiply. Reserves do not.

Financial intermediation multiplies deposits. It never multiplies reserves. Banks create money every time they lend — a loan becomes somebody’s deposit, and deposits are money — but no bank, however large, can manufacture a single reserve.

So the quantity of reserves is fixed from outside the banking system. And whoever fixes it is, by way of the multiplier, deciding the size of the entire money supply. That is the Federal Reserve, and it is the only reason Part 3 exists.

Question 7 · silent poll · pick a number on paper

One of these three is wrong

All three describe what a bank does with a deposit. Two of them are sound. One is not. Write down the number of the one that fails, and one line saying where its reasoning gives out.

Right about the dollar, wrong about the total

Sentence 3 fails — and notice how much of it is correct. A bank really does lend out money that was deposited with it. No bank prints anything, and sentence 1 says so in almost the same words without going wrong. The first half of sentence 3 is exactly right.

The error is in what it concludes from that. Follow the 300 Bank 1 lends. The borrower spends it; it lands at Bank 2 as a deposit of 300. Now look at the two accounts: Bank 1’s depositor still has 400, and the person who received the spending has 300. Deposits are money, so the money supply just grew by 300 — and nothing was taken away from anybody.

That is what “banks create money” means. It is not a figure of speech and it needs no printing press. It needs only that a loan ends up as a deposit somewhere, which it must, since the borrower spent it and somebody received it.

Sentence 2 is the check. Reserves ended at 400 and deposits ended at 1,600. Both statements are true at the same time, and they only sound contradictory if you believe sentence 3.

Where Part 2 leaves us

There are exactly two ways to change the money supply

M = m̂ × R   where   m̂ = 1 / rr

There are two symbols on the right-hand side, so there are two ways in and no others. Anything that changes the money supply has to change one of them.

Lever one · the quantity of reserves
M = m̂ × R
Put more reserves into the banking system and every one of them gets multiplied on the way through. Banks cannot do this. Somebody outside the banking system has to.
Lever two · the multiplier
m̂ = 1 / rr
Change the fraction banks keep and you change how far each reserve stretches. But rr is the banks’ choice — so this lever can only be pulled indirectly, by changing what banks want.

The Fed has one instrument for each. That is Part 3, and it is only two slides of new economics.

Part 3 of 4

The Fed’s levers

The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States, founded by act of Congress in 1913. It is independent: it does not take orders from the president or from Congress. Monetary policy is set by the Federal Open Market Committee, which sets goals for the money supply and for interest rates.

It is also the banks’ bank — the place banks hold their reserves, exactly as you hold your deposits at yours. And it is the lender of last resort: it lends to banks that cannot get funds anywhere else.

Question 9 · predict · commit before you look

The Fed raises the interest it pays on reserves

Back to the starting economy: R = 300, rr = 0.25, M = 1,200. This time the Fed buys nothing and sells nothing.

Since October 2008 the Fed has paid banks interest on the reserves sitting in their accounts at the Fed — much as your bank pays you interest on a savings balance. Today it raises that rate. No bonds change hands. No reserves enter or leave the system.

A bank with a deposit has two options: lend it, which is risky, or leave it at the Fed as reserves, which is safe. Paying interest on reserves puts a return on the safe option, so lending now has to clear a higher bar to be worth it.

Say rr rises from 0.25 to 0.50. Then m̂ = 1/0.50 = 2 and M = 2 × 300 = 600. Lower the rate instead and rr falls to 0.20, m̂ rises to 5, and M rises to 1,500.

Read the R column this time. It never moves. The money supply halved because each reserve now stretches half as far.

The Fed changed an incentive, not a rule

Notice what the Fed did not do. It did not order any bank to hold more reserves, and the required reserve ratio never entered the story — it is still whatever it was. The reserve ratio in m̂ = 1/rr is the banks’ own choice, and the Fed has no authority to set it.

What the Fed changed was the price of making that choice. Every bank then re-ran its own arithmetic, decided independently to hold more, and the money supply fell by half. Nobody was told to do anything.

That is how most of monetary policy works. The Fed rarely commands. It makes one option pay better than another and lets the banking system arrive at the result on its own.

The price of money

You now know where the money supply comes from. The reason that was worth three parts is what comes next: the money supply sets the interest rate — the number every deck since the goods market has simply handed you.

Answer · Question 10

It is the price of money

An interest rate is what you pay to have money now instead of later, and what you give up by holding money instead of lending it out. Strip away the quoting conventions and it is a price — the price of money.

The apparatus you already own

If it is a price, it is determined the way every price in this course is determined: by supply and demand. Nothing new is required. This is the second discussion’s machinery pointed at a different market.

Two things about this particular market are unusual, and both are worth naming before the diagram appears. The supply curve will be vertical. And the quantity on the horizontal axis will not be dollars.

What is actually on the axis

Real money balances, M/P

Nominal money
M
The number of dollars you hold. Count the bills and the balance; that is the whole measure.
Real money
M / P
The quantity of goods and services those dollars will buy — nominal dollars divided by the price level.
M = 1,200 and P = 2  →  M/P = 600
M = 1,200 and P = 3  →  M/P = 400

Not one dollar went anywhere. The same 1,200 dollars sit in the same accounts, and they now command a third less. Real balances are what anybody actually cares about holding, so real balances are what goes on the horizontal axis.

The supply side

Supply does not respond to the price

The supply of real money balances

M is set by Fed policy — you spent Part 3 on exactly how. P is determined in equilibrium, which this course takes up later and takes as given for now.

So M/P is a number handed to this market from outside it. A higher interest rate does not persuade the Fed to create reserves, and it does not persuade banks to conjure any either.

Quantity supplied therefore does not respond to the price, and the supply curve is vertical. Read the line across: at r = 2 and at r = 8, real balances are 600 either way.

Question 11 · write before you look

Money demand, L(r, Y)

Money demand is not the demand for wealth — nobody has ever wanted less wealth, and a demand curve for it would be a strange object. Money demand is how much of your wealth you want to hold in liquid form, ready for transactions.

It rises with income: more income means more transactions to cover, so more money is wanted at any interest rate. That much is easy. The other argument is the interesting one.

Q11 · one sentence

Write down why a higher interest rate makes people want to hold less money. You are holding the same wealth either way — so what exactly changed?

The interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money

Cash in your pocket earns nothing. A checking balance earns close to nothing. Every dollar you hold as money is a dollar not earning the interest rate — so the interest rate is precisely what holding money costs you.

When that cost rises, you hold less: less in checking, more in something that pays. Your wealth has not changed at all; only its composition has. That is the whole content of a downward-sloping money demand curve, and it is why we write demand as L(r, Y) — falling in r, rising in Y.

You met opportunity cost in the first discussion, as the value of the next best thing given up. Here it is doing structural work: it is the entire reason this curve slopes down.

Putting the two sides together

Where the interest rate comes from

The money market
── M/P supply (vertical) ── L(r, Y) money demand

The interest rate settles where the real balances people want to hold equal the real balances that exist: r = 4 at M/P = 600.

What pushes it there

Above r = 4, people want to hold less money than exists. They try to lend the excess out, and competing to lend drives the rate down.

Below r = 4, people want to hold more than exists. They sell other assets to get it, and competing for it bids the rate up.

Only at r = 4 is nobody trying to change what they hold. Notice what did the adjusting: the quantity could not move, so the price had to.

This is the sentence that joins the two halves of today. The Fed does not lower the interest rate by announcing a lower interest rate. It buys bonds, or it cuts what reserves pay; reserves change, the multiplier carries the change through the banking system, the money supply moves — and the rate is what gives way.

The equation contains nothing the three diagrams did not already contain. It is those three diagrams with the pictures removed.

The number you have been handed all quarter

Every deck since the goods market has taken the interest rate as given. When investment fell because r rose, nobody asked why r rose — it rose because the problem said so. This equation is the answer to the question nobody asked: r moved because something moved P, Y, or M.

And notice the loop that just closed. Y is in this equation. But r is in the investment equation, and investment helps determine Y. So the money market is watching the goods market, and the goods market is watching the money market. Neither can be solved on its own any more.

Q12 · one sentence

At its next meeting the Fed will raise rates, cut them, or leave them alone. Write down what you think it is trying to achieve when it chooses.

The dual mandate

The Federal Reserve Act of 1977 requires the Fed to do two things: maintain stable prices and maximize employment. That is the entire brief. Every instrument in Part 3 is a means to those two ends and to nothing else.

The two goals can conflict, and often do. Cooling inflation generally means raising rates; raising rates generally costs jobs. When they point in opposite directions the Fed cannot satisfy both, and has to choose. This course comes back to exactly that conflict.

Leaning against the wind

Fed policy is often described as leaning against the wind. When the economy runs hot, raise rates and cool it. When it runs cold, cut rates and warm it. Written as compactly as the last rule:

Output above its normal level pushes the target up. Rising prices push the target up. Z is everything else — for instance, holding still to see how a fiscal or trade policy actually lands before moving at all.

The throughline

Banks create money every time they lend, but they cannot create reserves. That is why the Fed controls the money supply, and why the money supply sets the interest rate.

Part 2 · the fact
A deposit of 400 became 1,600 of money, and not one new reserve appeared anywhere in the chain. Lending multiplies deposits. It cannot multiply reserves.
Part 3 · the consequence
Because reserves come from outside the banking system, the Fed controls them — directly through open market operations, and indirectly, through the multiplier, by changing what reserves pay.
Part 4 · the payoff
Money is supplied vertically and demanded downward, so the quantity of money sets its price. That price is the interest rate every earlier deck simply handed you.
