Thinking like
an economist

Three habits, and only three. What a choice actually cost you. Why the next step is the only step that matters. And whether a sentence is a claim about the world or a claim about what is better.

Work on your own paper. Write before each reveal — the point is your reasoning, not a clean final answer.

actually cost?

Nothing you own is unlimited — not your money, and definitely not your hours. So every yes is also a no, and the no is where the cost lives.

Question 1 · write two things, then stop

What is scarce in your life?

Write · 90 seconds

Name two things you genuinely do not have enough of this week. Not two things you want — two things that are actually running out on you.

Scarcity is the whole reason this course exists. If you had unlimited amounts of everything, there would be no choosing to do, and nothing for an economist to study.

What almost everyone writes

Money goes down first. It is the one that comes with a number attached, so it feels like the real constraint.

But look at the second thing you wrote — for most people it is time, or attention, or sleep. And that is usually the one actually doing the binding. You can borrow money. You cannot borrow Tuesday.

This is why everybody faces trade-offs, including people who are not short of money at all. Twenty-four hours is twenty-four hours, and it is the same twenty-four for everyone in this room.

Question 2 · predict · commit before any vocabulary

The extra hour

Write · one sentence · no discussion yet

After work today you planned to go out to eat with friends or exercise. On your way out, your boss asks you to stay an extra hour. You stay.

Write down what that hour cost you. Use whatever words you have — the vocabulary comes after you commit.

Do not write "an hour." An hour of what? The clock is not what you gave up; something you would have done with the clock is.

The next best option — and only that one

Suppose that, left alone, you would have gone out with your friends. Then the hour cost you dinner with your friends. Full stop.

It did not also cost you the workout. You were never going to do both — that was the whole point of the word or. The gym was already off the table the moment you picked dinner over it.

opportunity cost = value of the NEXT BEST forgone option
Follow-up · why not add them up?

Only one thing gets crossed out twice

Options 2 and 3 were already given up — you gave them up when you ranked dinner first, long before your boss said anything.

Two traps this kills

Do not add. "Dinner plus the gym plus my phone" double-counts things you had already turned down.

Do not use the price tag. The cost is what you would have chosen — not what it would have cost in dollars.

Why sentence 2 fails

The conclusion is right and the reasoning is wrong — the dangerous combination, because the answer key would mark it correct. What makes something the opportunity cost is that you would have chosen it. Not its price.

Test it in five seconds: suppose dinner were a $3 taco and the gym were a $40 boutique class. Sentence 2 now says the cost is the gym. But nothing about your actual ranking changed — you still would have gone to dinner, so the cost is still dinner. The price tag moved and the answer did not, which means the price tag was never doing the work.

Sentence 2 is a rule that happens to agree with the truth on this one example. Give it a different example and it breaks.

Question 4 · write, then hold

The free lunch

Write · one sentence

Your department is holding a meeting over lunch. Lunch is provided, at no charge to you. You go.

What are you giving up? If your answer is "nothing," write down why you believe that.

"Free" is a claim about a price. It is not a claim about a cost.

The hour, and what you would have done with it

You gave up the hour — and, more precisely, whatever you would otherwise have done with that hour. Caught up on the reading. Eaten somewhere you actually like. Gone home.

The price of the lunch is zero. The cost of the lunch is your next best use of that hour, and that is never zero, because your hours are scarce whether or not anyone charges you for them.

price = $0  ≠  cost = $0
Part 1 · the punchline

One rule, three questions

CHOOSE A → GIVE UP NEXT-BEST B → COST = B
Run this on any choice

1. What did I actually pick?  ·  2. If I had not picked it, what would I have done instead?  ·  3. That second thing is the cost.

Notice what is not in the rule: no dollar sign, no receipt, no adding. The cost of a choice is a thing that did not happen, which is exactly why it is so easy to leave off the ledger.

Keep this rule warm. In Part 2 the cost of each extra hour is an opportunity cost — and watching it rise is the whole point.

Part 2 of 3

One more
hour

Almost no real decision is "do it or don't." It is "do a little more, or stop here." So the only comparison that ever matters is the one about the next step.

Last week you learned that a point on a graph is a pair of numbers you can read from either axis. Today you read two harder things: the vertical gap between two curves, and a second graph that reports nothing but how steep the first one is.

The framework travels. Lecture worked this out on hours of studying. We are going to run the identical machinery on something you have never seen it applied to — because a framework that only works on the example it was taught with is not a framework.

Setup · no question yet · read the two curves

The fundraiser table

Your club runs a fundraiser table on campus and you decide how many hours to keep it open. Each extra hour raises less than the one before — the lunch crowd thins out. And each extra hour costs you more — the evening hours you give up are worth more to you than the mid-afternoon ones.

The red column is not a bill anybody sends you. It is the opportunity cost of each hour — the next best thing you would have done with it. Question 5 · predict · commit before any new vocabulary

The table has been open three hours

Write · yes or no, AND a reason

It is 3:00 and the table has been open for three hours. You have raised $102 so far, and given up $27 worth of your afternoon.

Should you stay open a fourth hour? Circle yes or no, then write the sentence that justifies it. The sentence is the graded part.

A reason like "we should raise as much as possible" is a real answer — write it down if that is what you think. You will get to test it in four slides.

Two answers that are both very common

"Yes — $102 is not enough yet."  ·  "No — three hours is plenty, we are tired."

Both are about the total, or about the whole day. Neither is the answer — and neither is wrong yet, because nobody has looked at the fourth hour on its own. Hold on to what you wrote.

Back to Question 5 · check what you wrote

Should you have stayed open a fourth hour?

Do not look at the totals. Look only at the fourth hour itself — the one row of the table you are actually deciding about.

hour 4  ·  brings in $22  ·  costs $17
Yes — and here is the only arithmetic you need

The fourth hour brings in $22. It costs you an evening-ish hour worth $17. So it adds $22 − $17 = $5 to the club's net. Worth it.

Notice what you never used: the $102 already raised. It is done, it is not coming back, and it is identical whether or not you stay. The only thing on the table is the next hour.

Check it against the totals: net goes from $75 at three hours to $80 at four. Up by exactly the $5 you just computed.

Part 2 · the punchline

The rule you just used twice

MB > MC → do it  ·  MB < MC → stop  ·  MB = MC → the optimum
Marginal benefit · MB

What the next step adds. In this table, the dollars the next hour brings in — read straight off the row. Hour 4: $22.

Marginal cost · MC

What the next step costs. Here, the value of the next best thing you give up to run that hour. Hour 4: $17.

Both words mean the same thing: the change caused by one more step. Not the total, not the average, not "was it worth it overall." Rational decisions are made at the margin because the margin is the only part of the situation you can still do anything about.

Keep the fourth hour in the corner of your eye. It is the last one that still pays for itself — and before the end of this part you will see that it is also, exactly, where the two lines meet.

Question 7 · diagnose · silent poll

Find the flawed sentence

All three sentences are about the fundraiser table. Every factual claim in all three is true — check them against the table if you want. One of them still gives you bad advice. Pick a number on your paper.

Students pick a number on paper (or fingers on the chest). Then reveal.

Why sentence 2 fails

The fact is true. The reasoning is not. Total raised really does go from $140 to $150 — sentence 2 is not lying to you, and that is what makes it dangerous. You cannot catch it by checking whether the number is right, because the number is right.

What it does is answer a question nobody asked. "Does the total go up?" is not the decision. "Is the next step worth what it takes?" is the decision. The sixth hour brings in $10 and costs $25, so the club is $15 worse off — while the total raised is climbing the whole time.

A rising total does not mean the next step is worth taking. Say that sentence back to yourself. If sentence 2 were sound reasoning it would never let you stop: a seventh hour would raise something too, and an eighth, right up until the table raises literally zero. Any rule that can only ever say "keep going" is not a decision rule.

Sentence 1 and sentence 3 both look at the row. Sentence 2 looks at the column total. That is the entire difference.

The boundary · where exactly does it turn?

The fifth hour still raises $16

Net climbs 35 → 60 → 75 → 80, and then falls back to 75 and 60. One hour is strictly better than every other.

Why the dots sit between the hour marks: each figure describes a whole hour, not an instant — the fourth hour runs from the 3-hour mark to the 4-hour mark, so its dot sits at 3½.

Same hour, twice

Both graphs answer at 4 hours. Same x on both — the widest gap on the left sits directly above the crossing on the right. They have to agree: the gap stops widening at the exact moment the blue total stops out-climbing the red one, and "how fast is this curve climbing?" is all the right-hand graph reports.

And now MB = MC is not a slogan, it is something you can point at. By the time the table has been open four hours, money is arriving at $19 an hour and the hour you are giving up is worth $19. Dead even. Every minute before that, the table earned more than it cost — so keep going. Every minute after, less — so stop. The best place to be is the one point where the two rates match.

Last week's skill, one level up. A point on the right-hand graph is still a pair — hour and dollars — but the dollars it reports are how steep the left-hand curve is at that hour. The marginal graph is not new information. It is a report on the slope of the graph you already had.

Which is why you never need both. Read the gap, or read the crossing. Most economists read the crossing, because two lines meeting at a point is far easier to see than two curves quietly drifting apart.

Practice · commit to all three before revealing any

Name the MB and the MC

No numbers. For each decision, write what the marginal benefit is and what the marginal cost is — in words, about the one more, never the total.

1 · One more slice of pizza
MB → how good that slice tastes
MC → its price, plus feeling too full

MB falls as you eat: the fourth slice is nowhere near as good as the first, though it is the same pizza. Stop at the slice where "how much do I want this one" drops below "what it costs me." Not at the slice where you stop enjoying pizza in general.

2 · One more hour of sleep
MB → how much better that hour makes tomorrow
MC → the best thing you would do with that hour instead

Straight out of Part 1: the marginal cost is an opportunity cost. Hour six of sleep helps a lot and costs you an hour you would have spent scrolling. Hour ten helps barely at all and costs you the morning. Same person, same bed, opposite verdicts.

3 · One more job application
MB → the extra chance that application adds
MC → the hour it takes, and the care it pulls from the others

You apply to your best-fit jobs first, so MB falls down the list. And a rushed fortieth application can lower the quality of the rest. "Apply to as many as possible" is sentence 2 from Q7 wearing different clothes — the total keeps rising, and you should still stop.

Every one of these is the fundraiser table again: something that falls, something that rises, and a place where they meet.

Part 3 of 3

What kind of
claim is that?

Two sentences can sound equally confident, equally political, and equally annoying — and only one of them is the kind of thing evidence could ever settle. Telling them apart is a separate skill from knowing which is true.

Setup · no question yet · learn the test

Two kinds of sentence

Positive · how the economy does work

A description, a measurement, or a prediction. It can be right or wrong, and evidence is what decides.

Normative · how the economy should work

A recommendation or a judgment. No amount of data settles it, because it rests on what you think is better.

the test → could data settle this, even data we don't have yet?

"Yes, in principle" → positive. "No, it depends what you value" → normative.

Normative claims are not sloppy thinking — economists make them constantly. They just appeal to values, and in this course the values almost always come from the same short list:

Efficiency

Are we getting the most out of what we have?

Equity

Is the split fair?

Growth

Is the pie getting bigger over time?

Stability

Are output, jobs and prices steady?

Watch for the trap. "Should" is a good hint but a bad rule. Plenty of positive claims sound like advocacy, and that is exactly where people go wrong — as you are about to.

Question 8 · label all six on your paper FIRST

P or N?

Write 1 through 6 down your page and put a P or an N next to each. Do all six before anything is revealed — then the TA opens them one at a time, so we can stop and argue about the ones that split the room.

1 · "The unemployment rate was 4.2% last month."
P · positive

A measurement. Somebody went and counted. If they counted wrong it is a false positive statement — still positive, just wrong.

2 · "The government should raise the minimum wage."
N · normative

The word "should," and underneath it a judgment about equity — that the current split is not fair enough. No dataset settles that.

3 · "Raising the minimum wage would reduce teen employment."
P · positive

Stop here. Economists genuinely disagree about whether this is true — and disagreement is not the test. It is a prediction about what would happen, and data can push on it. Compare with 2: same policy, completely different kind of sentence.

4 · "It is unfair that a full-time worker cannot afford rent."
N · normative

An equity judgment. Whether a full-time worker can afford rent is positive and measurable; whether that is unfair is not.

5 · "Inflation fell faster in countries that raised rates sooner."
P · positive

A historical claim about what already happened. Go get the numbers for those countries and you can check it.

6 · "Getting inflation down matters more than getting unemployment down."
N · normative

This is stability ranked against itself — which pain counts for more. Data can tell you the size of each; it cannot tell you which one you should mind more.

TA: 3 is the one worth the argument. If the room labelled it N, ask what evidence would change their mind — if they can name any, it is positive.

Question 9 · diagnose · silent poll

Find the flawed sentence

Three sentences about the same claim: "Cutting interest rates will lower unemployment." One of them is wrong — about the label and about why. Pick a number on your paper.

Students pick a number on paper (or fingers on the chest). Then reveal.

Why sentence 2 fails

Wrong label, wrong reasoning. Sentence 2 asks who is saying this and what do they want. That question has no bearing on whether the claim is testable — and testability is the entire test.

A positive statement can be false. It can be contested by serious people. It can be politically motivated, said by someone with an enormous stake in your believing it. None of that makes it normative. It is normative only if no possible evidence could settle it — and "did unemployment fall after rate cuts" is a question with data behind it.

Students reach for "normative" whenever a sentence sounds like advocacy. But that is a fact about the tone, and the classification is about the content. Ask the one question: could data settle this? Nothing about the speaker enters into it.

And the practical cost of getting this wrong: if you file a testable claim under "normative," you have just excused yourself from ever checking it.

Question 10 · mark your own copy first

Where are we in the cycle?

Real output does not grow smoothly — it wanders above and below its long-run trend. On your own paper, sketch this shape and mark four things: a peak, a trough, an expansion, and a recession. Do it before you press anything.

current phase  →  —
Mark your paper first. Then press play — each label goes on as the dot passes it.
Four phases, and only four

A peak is the turning point where output stops rising and starts falling. A trough is the turning point the other way. Expansion is the stretch from trough up to peak; recession is the stretch from peak down to trough.

Two things people get wrong. Peaks and troughs are turning points, not "high" and "low" — the second peak here is above the first trough and above nearly everything else on the chart, and it is still a peak. And the economy is always in one of the four; there is no neutral setting.

A common working definition of a recession is two successive quarters of falling real GDP. Notice that this is a positive statement: it is a rule for reading data. Whether a recession is bad enough to act on is normative — that is the stability judgment from the last slide.
