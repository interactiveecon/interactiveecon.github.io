ECON 002 — Measuring GDP

Did the Economy Actually Grow?

GDP measured in dollars can rise because a country produced more things, or because those things got more expensive. This lab walks you through how economists tell those two apart.

You will work through:

The problem

Island A's residents drink 50 more cups a year. Island B's residents drink exactly what they drank before. Yet both islands report GDP rising from $200 to $300.

GDP valued at the prices actually charged in each year is called nominal GDP. It adds up dollars, and dollars move for two different reasons — more stuff, or pricier stuff. Nominal GDP cannot separate them.

Step 2 — Freeze the price tags

Here is the fix, and it is the entire idea behind real GDP: value every year's output using the prices from one single year. That year is called the base year. If the price tags never change, then the total can only move when the quantities move.

Let's use Year 1 prices as the base and redo Island B — the island that produced no extra lemonade.

Each year at that year's own price
Year 1 100 cups × $2 = $200
Year 2 100 cups × $3 = $300
+50%
Island B — real GDP
Every year at Year 1 prices
Year 1 100 cups × $2 = $200
Year 2 100 cups × $2 base price = $200
0% — no growth

Now Island A, the island that genuinely made more lemonade:

Island A — nominal GDP
Each year at that year's own price
Year 1 100 cups × $2 = $200
Year 2 150 cups × $2 = $300
+50%
Island A — real GDP
Every year at Year 1 prices
Year 1 100 cups × $2 = $200
Year 2 150 cups × $2 base price = $300
+50% — real growth
The whole trick

Nominal GDP = this year's quantities × this year's prices.

Real GDP = this year's quantities × base-year prices.

Because the base-year prices are the same number in every single year, the only thing that can move real GDP is a change in quantities. Real GDP is a measure of how much stuff got made. It just happens to be reported in dollars.

One more case — the one that catches people out

A third island. Its statistician reports that GDP was $200 in Year 1 and $200 in Year 2. Not a dollar of growth. Before you read on: has this island stagnated?

Island C — nominal GDP
Each year at that year's own price
Year 1 100 cups × $2 = $200
Year 2 200 cups × $1 = $200
0% — looks like stagnation
Island C — real GDP
Every year at Year 1 prices
Year 1 100 cups × $2 = $200
Year 2 200 cups × $2 base price = $400
+100% — output doubled
Read that again

Island C doubled its lemonade. Everyone on the island drinks twice as much as before. And the GDP figure did not move by a single dollar, because the price fell by exactly as much as output rose.

Nominal GDP does not only overstate growth when prices rise. When prices fall it hides growth that really happened. It is not biased in one direction — it is simply the wrong measure for the question "did this country produce more?"

So the report should have read

Nominal GDP gets one of the three right, and only by accident. Real GDP gets all three right, every time.

Step 6 — Practice

Now you do the arithmetic. Five problems, each with two goods and two years. Year 1 is the base year every time. Work them on paper first — the calculator is not the point, the two formulas are.

The two formulas

Nominal GDP in Year 2 = (Year 2 price × Year 2 quantity) for each good, added up.

Real GDP in Year 2 = (Year 1 price × Year 2 quantity) for each good, added up.

Write it in your own words

Finish both sentences. Write your answer before you reveal the model one — the writing is what makes it stick.

"Nominal GDP can rise even when a country produces nothing extra, because ______. Real GDP fixes this by ______."

One good answer

Nominal GDP can rise even when a country produces nothing extra, because it values each year's output at that year's own prices, so a pure price increase shows up as a bigger GDP number. Real GDP fixes this by valuing every year's output at the prices of a single base year, so the price tags never move and the total can only change when the quantities produced change.

If your wording differs but says those two things, you have it.

Why this matters outside the classroom

Economies going through high inflation routinely post spectacular nominal GDP growth while producing no more than before — sometimes less. Any headline claiming a country's economy "grew" is making a claim about real GDP, whether or not it says so.
