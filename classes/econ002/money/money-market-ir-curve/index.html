ECON 002 — Money

From the Money Market to the IR Curve

See how the money market sets the interest rate, build the IR curve point by point, and learn which changes move you along it and which shift it.

Instructions

Two linked graphs run through the whole lab: the money market on the left and the IR curve on the right. A dashed r bridge carries the interest rate the money market sets across to the IR graph, where it is plotted against income. The key idea is the difference between moving along the IR curve and the curve shifting. Parts 1–5 unlock in order; Part 6 opens once you finish Part 2.

You will be asked to answer the following questions:

  1. Read the money market. Find the interest rate where money supply and money demand meet.
  2. Build the IR curve. Change income, read the new interest rates, and connect the points into the IR curve.
  3. Shift the IR curve (P). Predict, then see, what a higher price level does to both graphs.
  4. Shift the IR curve (M). Predict, then see, what a larger money supply does to both graphs.
  5. Movement vs. shift. Fill in a summary table, then experiment in a sandbox and solve a challenge.
  6. Practice. Work through randomized problems in three steps. New problem generates a fresh scenario each time, so you can practice as much as you like.

The lab ends with three Check your understanding questions. For each step, enter or choose your answer and click Check Answer. If your answer is incorrect, hints and the worked solution are available.

Part 1Read the money market

Supply of real money balances is a vertical line at M/P: the Fed sets M, and the price level P is taken as given. Demand, L(r, Y), slopes down because r is the opportunity cost of holding money, and it rises with income. Right now income is Y = 2,000, the money supply is M = 500, and the price level is P = 1.00.

r = 5. At r = 5, people want to hold exactly the $500 of real money balances that exist. The dashed r bridge carries that interest rate across to the IR graph, where it is plotted against income: Y = 2,000 → r = 5.

Part 2Build the IR curve (a change in Y)

Finish Part 1 to unlock this part.

The IR curve is a list of money-market equilibria, one for each level of income.

Y↑ → L↑ → r↑

Higher income means people want to hold more money at every interest rate, so money demand shifts right, from L(r, Y1) to L(r, Y2). Supply hasn't moved, so r rises from 5 to 7 (point B). On the IR graph that is a new point: Y = 2,400 → r = 7.

Lower income, lower money demand, lower r: Y = 1,600 → r = 3 (point C). You now have three money-market equilibria, one for each income level.

Connect the three points on the IR graph.

That line is the IR curve, IR1. It slopes up because Y↑ → r↑.

Now sweep income from 1,200 to 2,800 and watch both graphs.

Key resultEvery point on the IR curve is a money-market equilibrium for one level of Y. A change in Y moves the economy along the IR curve. It does not shift it.

Part 3Shift the IR curve (a change in P)

Finish Part 2 to unlock this part.

Back to the baseline: Y = 2,000, M = 500, P = 1.00, with IR1 and its three points.

P↑ → M/P↓ → r↑ at every Y → IR shifts up

M/P falls from 500 to 400, so at Y = 2,000 the interest rate rises from 5 to 6. Higher prices mean the same dollars buy less, so at every income level the interest rate is higher: 3 → 4 at Y = 1,600, 5 → 6 at Y = 2,000, 7 → 8 at Y = 2,400. A higher r at every Y is a whole new curve, IR2.

Part 4Shift the IR curve (a change in M)

Finish Part 3 to unlock this part.

Back to the baseline again: Y = 2,000, M = 500, P = 1.00, with IR1.

M↑ → M/P↑ → r↓ at every Y → IR shifts down

More money at the same prices is more real money balances: M/P rises from 500 to 600, and r at Y = 2,000 falls from 5 to 4. People will only hold the extra money at a lower interest rate, and that is true at every income level: 3 → 2, 5 → 4 and 7 → 6. The whole curve shifts down to IR2.

    Under the hood

    Both graphs come from one small model, so they always agree exactly.

    Money demand: L(r, Y) = 0.5·Y − 100·r

    The money market clears where M/P = L(r, Y), so r = Y/200 − (M/P)/100.

    For a given M and P, that is the IR curve. Its slope, 1/200, never changes, so a change in P or M moves the whole line up or down in parallel.

    The notes write the interest rate rule as r = cP + dY − eM, where c, d and e are positive constants. What matters here are the signs: P +, Y +, M −. The numbers in this model are chosen to keep the graphs readable; they are not estimates of c, d and e.
