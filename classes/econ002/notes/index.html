Accessible Lecture Notes

The lecture handout for each unit, as a web page. Same content as the printed notes, with the blanks as buttons you click to check yourself and the figures animated one step at a time. Everything works by keyboard and with a screen reader. Nothing is saved, so the blanks start empty every time you open a unit.

    Equilibrium in the Goods Market
    Where the demand for goods comes from — consumption out of disposable income, investment out of the interest rate, and government purchases taken as given. Then the Keynesian cross, what unplanned inventories do to firms that guess wrong, the multiplier that makes output move by more than the shock, and the IS curve that falls out of running the whole thing at two interest rates.

    Fiscal Policy
    The same economy as Unit 7 with one lever moved at a time. A rise in government purchases and a rise in taxes, each traced through planned expenditure, through the rounds of the multiplier, and out to the IS curve — so the two multipliers can be read off the same three pictures. Ends with automatic stabilizers and the full-employment budget.

    Money
    What counts as money and how it is measured, then where it comes from: bank balance sheets, the deposit cascade that turns $100 of reserves into $500 of money, and the two levers the Fed has over it. Then the interest rate as the price of money — what moves it, and the rule that summarises all three.

  • Unit 10 · Aggregate Demand
    Aggregate Demand
    The IR and IS curves on one pair of axes, so fiscal and monetary policy can be read off together — and so you can see that they move output the same way but the interest rate opposite ways. Then the price level is allowed to change, which is all the aggregate demand curve is: the same diagram answered once per price level.

    Open  ·  Interactive notes
  • Unit 11 · AS–AD
    Aggregate Supply and Aggregate Demand
    All three curves on one graph, and the difference between the two supply curves doing all the work. A demand shock moves prices and output together and, once wages catch up, leaves only prices changed. A supply shock moves them opposite ways — stagflation — and that is why one policy lever cannot fix both halves of the mandate at once.
