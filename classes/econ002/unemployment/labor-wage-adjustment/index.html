How Fast Does the Wage Fall?

Watch the labor market adjust to a recession month by month when wages are flexible versus sticky.

Instructions

At month 1, households cut spending and labor demand falls from LD to LD′. You will run the same recession twice: once with flexible wages and once with sticky wages. Both worlds end at the same point. Your job is to see how the speed of the wage adjustment creates temporary (cyclical) unemployment.

Same start, same finish

Same starting point, same ending point (C). The only difference is how fast the wage falls. The shaded area between the sticky path and the natural rate is cyclical unemployment.

Where did the workers go?

Employment fell from 5 to 4 million, yet unemployment is zero. At the lower real wage, 1 million people chose not to look for work. They moved down the labor supply curve and are out of the labor force, not unemployed. Remember: to be unemployed you must be actively looking for work.

Why are wages sticky?

In the short run, wages do not tend to fall. The notes give three reasons:

1. Social (implicit) contracts Firms avoid cutting pay because it hurts morale and feels unfair.
2. Explicit contracts Union and employment contracts fix wages for a period.
3. Relative wages Workers care about pay relative to others, so no group wants to accept a cut first.
What about a boom?

Wages are sticky downward. In a boom, labor demand shifts right and the real wage rises in both worlds, so flexible and sticky wages give the same result. Stickiness matters in recessions.
