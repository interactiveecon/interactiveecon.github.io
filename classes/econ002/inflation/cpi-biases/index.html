ECON 002 — Inflation

Is the CPI Overstating Inflation?

See how substitution, new goods and quality improvements make a fixed-basket CPI overstate the rise in the cost of living.

Part 1: Substitution bias

The Rivera family buys beef and chicken. In the base year both cost $5 per pound, and they buy 10 lb of each: $100 a month. This is their CPI basket. Then a drought hits cattle ranchers and beef doubles to $10/lb. Chicken stays at $5.

Predict first

The CPI will say the cost of feeding the family rose by 50%. Will the family's actual cost of staying just as well fed rise by more than 50%, exactly 50%, or less than 50%?

Step 3

Compare

What the CPI says vs. what it really costs

The CPI assumes the family keeps buying 10 lb of beef no matter what. In reality they substitute toward chicken, whose relative price fell. Because the CPI's basket is fixed, it overstates how much the cost of living rose.

This is one reason the Fed prefers the PCE deflator. Its basket changes over time.

Part 2: New goods

Before streaming existed, the Riveras watched 4 movies a month in a theater at $15 each: $60. Then a streaming service appears: unlimited movies for $15 a month. They switch.

The new good isn't in the fixed basket, and the price of a movie ticket didn't change, so the CPI doesn't move. But each dollar now buys more entertainment: the real value of the dollar rose. The CPI misses this, so it overstates the cost of living.
