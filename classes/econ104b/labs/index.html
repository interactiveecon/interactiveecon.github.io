Monopoly with a Per-Unit Tax

Conceptual

Why Marginal Revenue is Less Than Price

For a firm with market power, selling one more unit forces the price down on every unit sold. Drag the point on the demand curve to see the output effect and the price effect, and watch the MR curve emerge as the net of the two.
Open Lab →

Shock the Monopolist

Apply shocks to a single-price monopolist's environment — a rise or fall in marginal cost, a parallel demand shift, or a rotation of demand to become more or less elastic — and watch how the profit-maximizing price and quantity respond in each case.
Open Lab →

Why a Monopolist Never Prices in the Inelastic Region

Along a linear demand curve, elasticity changes continuously — elastic at the top, unit elastic at the midpoint, inelastic at the bottom. Drag a price marker through every region and see why a profit-maximizing firm always operates on the elastic side: raising price in the inelastic region simultaneously raises revenue and lowers cost.
Open Lab →

Welfare Decomposition under Market Power

Slide continuously from competition to monopoly and watch consumer surplus split into three regions: the smaller triangle consumers still get, a rectangle transferred to the firm as producer surplus, and a triangle of deadweight loss that disappears entirely. See visually why not all of consumers' loss becomes the firm's gain.
Open Lab →

Game Theory

Problem Solving

Solving a Simultaneous Game

A 2×2 simultaneous-move game step by step: click the best-response payoffs in the matrix, choose each player's dominant strategy, list every pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, then compute the mixing probabilities (p, q) that form the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium.
Open Lab →

Iterated Elimination of Dominated Strategies

Extend the dominant-strategy logic from the 2×2 case to 3×3 and 4×4 games. Iteratively remove strictly dominated rows and columns, declaring the dominator each time, until no dominations remain — then identify what the surviving sub-game tells you about the Nash equilibria of the original.
Open Lab →

Solving a Sequential Game

An extensive-form game with two players and 2–4 decision rounds. Solve it by backward induction round-by-round: at each level, click the better payoff and the branches to eliminate. The tree collapses one level per confirmed round; once solved, it expands back to its full size with the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium path highlighted.
